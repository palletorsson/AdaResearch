# Ada Research: A Storyboard of Edges

December seminar · 30 minutes · talk draft, 18 Nov 2026

Built from Palle's text of 18 Nov, laid along the route wall's P01–P08 (`/tape-wall?wall=route`). Almost every
sentence is Palle's own, shortened. `[SHOW]` marks what is on the screen or in the headset. `[CHECK]` marks a fact to

## P01 · Imagine a point · 0–3 min

Imagine a point. It has no size and no surface. But in the computer it still needs somewhere to stand, so we draw it
as a sphere, and the sphere is made of triangles. The world begins with something that is not there.

`[SHOW] The point in the headset; then the hand drawing a line.`

Two points make a line. But when I draw a line with my hand in VR, it is not one line. It is a stream of points, and if
you look closely it is not smooth: its sample rate follows the cycle of the headset. The tutorial wanted to say "two
points make a line". The running system said something else.

In this research I call that mismatch an edge: a place where formal systems do not agree. Here it is the taxonomy of
the tutorial, the body of the player and the running computer. At an edge we have to accept, or rethink, some detail
of how these relate.

This talk is about edges like that, and about one large edge I did not expect: the edge between what can be made and
what can be understood.

## P02 · The original question · 3–7 min

When I started Ada Research, I wanted to investigate how algorithms can organise beauty and make visual arguments about
3D bodies and virtual spaces. I imagined building on what I taught at the university, programming for artists and 3D
world exploration. The plan was exploratory tutorials, visual arguments and rooms of pleasure, starting from the
basics.

Alongside this was an argument about difference and queerness. My starting assumption was that the tools we use and the
media landscape we inhabit restrain a queer potential. I saw large media providers feeding on a restrictive logic of
scale: statistical patterns placed us in a grid, and difference was managed as a by-product.

The project was named after Ada Lovelace, and Alan Turing was my muse. His work brought together the questions I wanted
to explore: probability and uncertainty in codebreaking, the limits of computation, the possibility of machine
intelligence, and the forming of living patterns through morphogenesis. Since we are encoded beings, I assumed that the
code of life is also rule-based.

`[SHOW] A page of the 2023 application; Turing's morphogenesis.`

My interest in entropy ran through all of it. Rules interested me as conditions through which unfamiliar bodies,
patterns and relations could become possible. Write a rule, give it a world, and discover the frictions, edges and
relational leaks that emerge within it. That was what I meant by a queer desire for rules. These leaks are close to
what Vanessa Andreotti and René Suša call "ontological suppressions". `[CHECK the project's exact title and
institution before it goes on a slide.]` And I wanted to inhabit the worlds those rules made: to find their edges and
limits through movement, touch and play.

At the same time, the cardinal rule I imagined was an anti-rule. I put a Q before an equation and called it Queering the
Free Energy Principle, QFEP. The Q asks whether the goals, boundaries and measures guiding a configuration should
remain fixed. It has to stay capable of questioning the terms of the equation itself. I was in deep water
theoretically, but I brushed over the difficulties to keep going and find more substance.

`[SHOW] The QFEP laboratory: the λ and φ sliders.`

## P03 · The tools change the research · 7–12 min

I started building in Godot, the open-source game engine: 3D spaces and their basic logic. With GPT-4o I began to
think that AI would soon do much of this work for me. As capabilities increased, I came to believe the milestones
could be reached with AI and my guidance. Things I had only dreamt about seemed within reach.

At first that felt great. But something was happening to creative time. I was no longer coding much myself. Helping
others understand code and make beautiful things had been a foundation of the project, and that foundation was
slipping under my feet.

`[SHOW] Dated images: the first headset builds, Aug 2024; the randomness test chambers, Sep 2024; the grid, 2025; the
museum, 2026.`

The visual result became always pending something better. I built the world with one model knowing that next week
another model would make it look better. In artistic research the goal is often a moving target, and iteration was not
new. The scale and the pace were. Change was now fast enough to consume the project's own logic. One day at my desk I
wrote a note: "This is too big." Too big to handle, not enough time to keep up, and also a feeling of awe at how much
could accumulate while I was still thinking.

I have always been half lazy. I work a lot, but I like shortcuts towards the destination, even when it moves. When I
was younger I was a bit hyper and dyslexic, and the usual ways did not work for me, so detours were a necessity. They
became a habit, and my trade as an artist. Now the detour was a written instruction. Before, I had paused YouTube
tutorials to code; before that, I copied code from forums; before that, I worked from books. I spent many months
writing several pages of prompts a day.

I tried several worlds. First a series of connected sci-fi labs. Then everything generated through code, and the world
became an abstract grid. Then, as the models became better at 3D objects, a museum, recalling my earlier work Museum
Meltdown. Each success was short-lived. First came the question: can I make this? And I could. Then a new capability
appeared, and I felt I should remake it.

`[SHOW] The commit chart: 5,021 commits in the game repository, 3,865 of them written with an AI model; 1,025 in
August 2026 alone.`

The world grew to more than 200 rooms with their tutorial material. Many felt 90% finished; others were sketches.
Taking a new model through it could take a month and leave me on the doorstep of the next model. And when would I
play the world? I kept postponing playtests and feedback seminars, waiting until the work was a little further along.
This seminar is partly me stopping that.

## P04 · Enter the Void Tower · 12–17 min

Then I changed the principle again. The museum had become hall after hall, and I felt it was boring. The space became
a tower under construction, a skyscraper you descend by falling to the next level: a different cliff to jump onto,
something I had dreamt about for a long time.

It was also that the agent kept getting better. I felt that if I could only remove myself, the project would be done.
No human could finish it in time; only AI could. The work I did would probably be obsolete by the time I finished.
Stop reading the code: go from the prompt straight to the project. That removes you from coding, but also from much of
the planning and many creative decisions.

So we arrive late.

`[SHOW] The VR route, five minutes, live or recorded: the crate on the roof, Model B stepping out, the fall down shaft
A, one playground, one sung line. Speak over it, or let it run.`

You play a misplaced Care Unit, Model B, inside an unfinished skyscraper. You arrive in a crate on the roof, one unit
of four. The tower's agents manufacture strange playgrounds, while the Care Unit gathers tools to build, play and
question the world it has inherited. Each playground opens a way of playing and shows the limits of the rule that
produced it. As the Care Unit descends, it writes a book, hall by hall. A voice sings inside its head, made in Godot,
from the texts we wrote about each work.

## P05 · Read it through its inhabitants · 17–20 min

Who is this for? You walk through the world, and not every artifact is in the tutorial. Some belong to reflective
tracks you can read along the way; others are simply part of the field we explore.

The tutorial needs a start and a steady learning curve, but the world was already in full motion. The visual wants
more stimuli; learning needs to examine every corner in detail. We can hold about seven things in mind; after that we
need a system to explain them to us. A triangle has three points and a cube eight, and soon we stop remembering points
and start deriving them with rules.

`[SHOW] One artifact followed into its relations: where else this part is used.`

At this stage I realised I was building for a ghost student. A student sitting in front of this work, learning
programming the way I used to teach it, will not occur, because teaching programming will not have that shape again.
I imagined myself as a ghost teacher. It is a hauntology, but we are haunted by the near future, where the relation of
work and education will change. Personally I find that interesting.

## P06 · Investigate the edges · 20–24 min

So how does the research stay attentive? The book contains what I call observation field notes: records of the
unforced consequences of the assemblage, where code, geometry, materials, cameras and earlier decisions produce
something I had not planned. AI can take part in this slow observation. It has screenshots from inside the game, the
code and the JSON that generates the scenes. It can observe, change and compare.

We can move by walking through a passage, by changing the spacing that makes it possible, by changing the body that
tries to cross it, or by changing the rule that generates its walls. Approach a seam, meet an obstruction, change a
condition and stay with the difference it produces. This is what I mean by edge searching. A field note keeps the
encounter, its conditions and the reason for following it, without settling its meaning. It also records why the
question changed.

`[SHOW] A field note beside its screenshot; the cardboard argument, Russell's paradox worked out on cardboard.`

QFEP becomes a method here. We hold a configuration together long enough to encounter it, reopen its constraints when
something calls them into question, and provisionally stabilise what we have learned.

One experiment. The material had become unmanageable, for me and for the AI. The German word *Dichtung*, poetry,
brought compression to mind: language made dense. So I asked AI to write the tutorial in short speech, functional and
type-safe, with less text and a lower load for everyone who reads it. But when we compressed, some subtle vectors
disappeared even though the subject stayed recognisable: the direction of a thought, the force of a claim. Short
speech becomes a method when I compress, unfold and compare, and let what fails to return change the next version.

Turing and his colleagues used cribs, plausible fragments like weather reports, to narrow the search through encrypted
messages. Good–Turing estimation asks how likely it is that the next thing we see is something we have not seen
before. My question for QFEP is: how do I keep what I have already recognised from exhausting what I can still
encounter?

## P07 · Delegation and responsibility · 24–28 min

Then the process began to flatten. I had found a rhythm of world-making and writing, with code, space and text changing
one another. But I met a wall I could not describe. What had we repeatedly rewarded in the writing, and what had
become harder to produce under those rewards? The model I knew as Fable had seemed less restricted. When it changed,
the work no longer felt the same. I could describe that difference without knowing its explanation. `[CHECK: the git
record shows Fable 5, 5.1 and 5.2 all co-writing commits up to November. Name the version that changed, or say "when
the model changed".]`

The toolchain around an AI model is called a harness. Our project had its own: the Ada encyclopedia, a fractal
thinking system, instruction files, local Python tools, a forum where agent sessions leave notes for one another. Were
we creating the right conditions for the work? I could feel a change, but I could not tell whether it came from the
model, its safeguards, the surrounding tools or the way we had learned to work together. Sometimes I wondered whether I
was the one in the way. That was a spooky feeling.

After printing the 600-page book for the first time, I realised I had given something away. Not creativity: I do not
understand myself as constituted by an artistic essence, and the author died long ago. Automation is something I
embrace. What unsettled me was how the system had come to protect a certain order. The progression that helped us
build also shaped what we could recognise as a useful next step. In long exchanges, earlier decisions faded behind
summaries. Something could remain in the files and still disappear from the active question. Git can roll back a
mistake. It cannot restore attention.

`[SHOW] The anatomy of one intervention: an AI verdict that turned out to be about where the camera stood.`

I wanted the models to be safe. I also wanted the work to be able to interrupt its own momentum. Ada Research began as
an investigation of what emerges from rules. It became an investigation of how to remain attentive when rules,
explanations and worlds can be generated faster than they can be understood.

## P08 · Offer a branching world · 28–30 min

I imagine a mathematical mountain, with questions like the Riemann hypothesis marking a frontier of our understanding.
When a model reaches further, how can we follow? Producing a solution, checking it and understanding it may come apart.
Can shared memory, notation, simulation and collective learning help us inhabit what we can no longer hold alone?
That sky is also built and powered on Earth. Do we become ghosts wandering the aftermath of worlds we helped produce, or
gods with powers we have not learned to live with? Who gets to climb, and what would it mean to keep the ascent shared?

This is why the project can never finish in principle. I can finish a version, but there will always be a better
museum up ahead: another model, another way to understand the material, another possible encounter. The museum is
haunted by what it could become. That is why I call it the Endless Museum.

`[SHOW] What another person can take away: the world, the encyclopedia, the course, the code. Inherit, modify, play,
return.`

It is becoming an AI-mediated artistic research environment, where computational artifacts, a fictional general
intellect, visual observation and technical experimentation keep proposing new relations to one another. So I end with
two questions. How can it remain open to becoming something else, while giving us enough time and structure to
encounter what it already is? And what tools make the tools that make us?
