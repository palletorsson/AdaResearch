# Color_Rainbow - Critical Reflection

## Newton's Prism Extended

In 1672, Newton passed white light through a prism and saw it split into colors. He identified seven bands: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. The spectrum was born - color revealed as wavelength, light decomposed into its constituents.

But Newton observed from outside. The prism sat on a table; the spectrum projected on a wall. Observer and spectrum were separate - the classic subject-object division of scientific observation.

The rainbow corridor dissolves this division. You do not observe the spectrum; you walk through it. The spectrum is not an object but an environment. You are inside the prism.

## The Spectrum Has No Magenta

Walk through the rainbow corridor and notice what's missing. Red fades to orange to yellow to green to blue to violet - then jumps back to red. Where is magenta? Where is pink?

They are not here because they are not in the spectrum. Magenta has no wavelength. It exists only in perception - the brain's construction when red and blue receptors fire without green.

The rainbow corridor tells the truth that color wheels hide: the physical spectrum is linear, not circular. There is no smooth transition from violet back to red. The loop we draw in color theory is perceptual, not physical.

## Color as Environment vs. Color as Property

In previous maps, color was a property you assigned to objects. Paint this tile red. Make this nail blue. Color belonged to things.

Here, color is the environment itself. You don't color the corridor; the corridor colors you. You are bathed in red light, then orange light, then yellow. Color is not on objects - it is in the air, surrounding your body, entering your eyes from all directions.

This shifts the ontology. Color is not possessed by things but fills space. You don't have a color; you are in a color.

## The Wave That Carries You

The phase offset creates a wave - color flowing through the corridor like water through a channel. If you stand still, the wave washes over you. If you walk, you move through the wave.

But you cannot outrun it, and you cannot stand outside it. The wave is everywhere you can be. This is immersion: not looking at the phenomenon, but being within it, unable to achieve the outside perspective.

The scientific observer stands apart. The subject in the rainbow corridor has no "apart" available.

## Temporal Color

The spectrum usually appears spatial - spread across wavelength, laid out like a ribbon. But the rainbow emitters make it temporal. Red becomes orange becomes yellow - not across space, but across time.

This temporalization changes the experience. You cannot see the whole spectrum at once; you can only experience its unfolding. Memory must hold the red while you perceive the green. The spectrum becomes narrative rather than image.

All experience is temporal, but we pretend images are instantaneous. The rainbow corridor refuses this pretense. It insists that seeing takes time.

## The Corridor as Constraint

The walkway is narrow, suspended over void. You cannot wander freely; you can only proceed forward or backward. The architecture constrains movement to a single axis.

This constraint serves the experience. If you could walk in any direction, the spectrum would have no order. You might skip from blue to yellow to red. The corridor forces you to encounter colors in sequence - the sequence of wavelengths, the order of the spectrum.

Architecture here is pedagogy. The walls that constrain are the walls that teach.

## Continuous vs. Discrete

The physical spectrum is continuous - infinitely many wavelengths between any two you name. But the rainbow emitters are discrete - ten objects, ten positions, ten phase offsets.

The animation creates the illusion of continuity. Colors blend smoothly because they cycle through HSV values, because the eye interpolates, because we want to see gradients. But underneath, the emitters are discrete points.

This is the fundamental compromise of digital representation: continuous experience from discrete computation. The rainbow corridor performs this compromise openly.

## Color Without Object

What is the color of light? Not the color of a surface reflecting light - the color of the light itself, the light that fills the room before it hits anything.

The rainbow corridor foregrounds this question. The emitters glow; the air takes on color; your skin becomes tinted. But the air has no pigment. The color is in the light, not in any surface.

In physical reality, we rarely see colored light directly - only its effects on surfaces. The corridor presents pure colored light, filling space, coloring everything without being located anywhere.

## The Pickup Cube Interrupts

Mid-corridor, a pickup cube. You must interact, must do something other than walk and observe. The pickup breaks the meditative flow.

Why interrupt immersion? Perhaps to remind you that you are an agent, not just a perceiver. Perhaps to mark progress, to divide the corridor into halves. Perhaps simply to give your hands something to do.

The pickup is a design choice that reveals assumptions: that passive experience needs activation, that walking is not enough, that interaction is required to maintain engagement.

## Spectrum as Political Metaphor

The rainbow flag appropriated the spectrum for LGBTQ+ identity. Six stripes (originally eight): red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet. Diversity visualized as spectrum - each color distinct, all colors necessary, the whole more than the sum of parts.

Walking through the rainbow corridor, you walk through this political symbolism whether you intend to or not. The spectrum is not innocent. It carries meaning loaded by history, by community, by struggle.

The map does not make this explicit. But meaning seeps in anyway. No rainbow is just physics anymore.

## Exiting the Spectrum

At the corridor's end, the teleporter waits. You will leave the rainbow, return to "normal" light, see colors as properties of objects again.

But having been inside the spectrum, you might see differently. The white light of the next map contains all these colors, potentially. The surface colors are selections from this continuum. The rainbow is always implicit.

The corridor teaches you to see white as composite, colored surfaces as selective, the spectrum as hidden but present everywhere light shines.
