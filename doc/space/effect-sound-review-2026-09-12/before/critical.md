# Sound is an infrastructure — latency, quantisation, and the politics of what can be heard

The waveform on the scope and the sound in the room are not the same thing. The waveform is a continuous mathematical object; the sound is a sequence of pressure differences transmitted through air, sampled by transducers, reconstructed by speakers, and heard by ears. Between the function and the experience is a full stack of infrastructure, and this hall does not hide it.

Wendy Chun argues that software's power is the power to make infrastructure invisible. When a system works, the learner hears the note; when it fails, they hear nothing, or noise, or a glitch. Chun's insight is that the invisibility is not neutral. It is a political achievement: a claim that the translation from waveform to sound is a trustworthy process, and that the translation's costs can be ignored.

The hall's primary unhides one stretch of that stack. The FM rig on its two desks does not play a recording; every note is computed when a ball moves or a button is pressed — 44,100 samples for each second, a decaying sinusoid inside the carrier's phase, an attack, a decay, a tanh that keeps the result within half of full scale — and the readout above the panel prints how many milliseconds that took. The note you hear began that long after your hand moved, plus the buffer, plus the metre of air. Real-time is a claim, not a property. The scope above the desks shows what was computed: the modulator, the bare carrier, a rule, the output. The colour cube on the shelf shows only three of the six values as a hue nobody can decode, and the hall says so; it is a sign, not a drawing.

The racks along row five extend the argument the other way, toward a studio's density of gear. The sine rack is the atom; the Moog carves harmonics away until a bass remains; the DX7 lets sines modulate sines into a piano that never had strings; the 303 bends a filter into melody. The audio monitor draws whatever plays; the timbre sculptor stacks only integer multiples of one fundamental, so no bell is reachable from its sliders — a limit of the instrument, made audible. What looks like one sound is a handful of dominant partials plus a floor that never settles to zero, and each stage of the stack subtracts information that the next cannot recover.

The audition panel is the hall's politics of listening. HOLD keeps the synthesised note itself, not a memory of it; COMPARE replays it beside the note you just made; the readout names the one value that differs. A preference has to survive hearing the reference again. That is a small discipline, but it is the one that the rest of the chapter's sound rooms assume: fix one thing, change one thing, listen through the tail.

Within the sequence, Effect Sound is the pivot from visible oscillation to audible oscillation. The rooms before treated the wave as something to see or walk; this one treats it as something to hear, and shows the machinery that hearing depends on. AirMusic follows on the active route with a struck resonance; Bernini's twisted columns and Cage's silence lie further along, and all of them depend on the infrastructure this hall makes visible. The learner leaves knowing that hearing is as constructed as seeing, and that the construction is always political.
