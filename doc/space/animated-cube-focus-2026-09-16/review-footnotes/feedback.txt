This is very strong, and I think the hall has found its real problem:

> **What must remain invariant for something still to count as a cube?**

That is a better question than simply “how is a cube constructed?” because it continues the method established in the previous rooms: change one part of the implementation and see which identity survives.

The small tutorial lesson can stay very simple:

> **A cube can be represented as vertices, edges and triangular faces.**

The research question underneath it is:

> **Which properties belong to connectivity, and which belong to geometry?**

Your `v6` experiment makes that distinction physical.

The important discovery is:

* vertex `6` can move;
* the same vertices remain neighbours;
* the edge/triangle index lists remain unchanged;
* but equal edge lengths, right angles, planar square faces and perhaps valid enclosure can disappear.

So the object preserves **combinatorial structure** while losing some of the properties by which we recognize a geometric cube.

That is an excellent Ada experiment.

### This may be the hall's truth line

That is very concise and very deep.

It gives you the distinction:

**same adjacency / different geometry**

**same topology / changed metric geometry**

I would be slightly careful with the word *topology* in the visitor text because technically the mesh may become self-intersecting or degenerate if pushed far enough. That is exactly what the artifact demonstrates.

### `REPLAY` versus `RESTORE CUBE` is surprisingly important

This is a very good distinction:

That is a subtle programming lesson and a philosophical one without needing to announce itself as philosophy.

It also echoes the opening of the book: restarting how something is shown is not the same thing as returning the world to an earlier condition.

## This is a very good place for several footnotes

I would use three.

**Connectivity versus geometry.** Introduce the distinction between indexed mesh topology/connectivity and vertex positions.

**Triangulation of quads.** Explain briefly that GPUs render triangles, so a quadrilateral face is usually represented as two triangles; choosing a diagonal is a construction decision that may become visible under deformation.

**Euler's polyhedron relation**, perhaps very lightly: a cube has 8 vertices, 12 edges and 6 faces, satisfying `V - E + F = 2`. Not needed for the walk, but it is an elegant technical genealogy for the counts in your instrument.

And potentially one artistic/theoretical note around the familiar object becoming strange through manipulation. But I would prioritize technical lineage here. Not every room needs a philosopher.

That heterogeneity will make the book stronger.

Most importantly, this hall develops the queer research methodology in a particularly precise way.

It doesn't ask:

> How do we deform the cube into something queer?

It asks:

> **How much can change before the name “cube” stops being useful? Which relations persist through transformation? Who or what supplies the criterion of sameness?**

And the answer is not supplied in advance.

The visitor can pull `v6` a little and think:

> still basically a cube.

Pull farther:

> maybe not.

Meanwhile the program can report exactly which structural relations stayed constant.

That gap between **formal invariance, perceptual recognition and naming** is extremely rich territory for Ada.

So I think this chapter is among the strongest so far. It is focused, technically real, and the philosophical question arises directly from moving one vertex rather than being placed on top of the cube afterward.
