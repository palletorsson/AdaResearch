## The walk

A cube that will not hold its shape. Press it, drop it, watch it recover — or not. The `jelly_cube` is a spring-mass system, and the whole chapter is built from its one rule: every pair of neighboring particles is joined by a spring that pulls back in proportion to how far it has been stretched from rest — Hooke's law, force = stiffness × displacement, the smallest possible model of a thing that wants to return. Stack thousands of those springs into a lattice and you get matter: press a corner and the displacement travels inward as every spring passes the push to the next; let go and the same springs haul the vertices home. Deformation is the visible argument between force and freedom playing out in real time — gravity pulls the vertices down, the springs pull them back, and what you see is the running negotiation between the two. The `softmill` grinds rotation against that compliance. The whole map is one felt claim: this matter *remembers* a shape (the rest lengths encode *this is what I was*) while the world keeps insisting otherwise.

## The turn (critical)

The jelly cube is a philosophical object, and the sequence is right to open on it: it has **no fixed shape, only a rest configuration it perpetually renegotiates with the world.** That is the entire integration-phase thesis in one bounce — form is a dynamic equilibrium, not a static property; a thing's shape is a verb. And the cube quietly refutes the binary the whole earlier curriculum leaned on: it is neither solid nor fluid but the continuum between them, matter at λ = 0.5, halfway between rigid and liquid. Primitives spent eleven maps teaching you the clean, timeless solid — the cube whose corners you could trust. This sequence's first act is to grab that cube and squeeze, and show you the "solid" was a simplifying story all along. What deforms is what remembers; what cannot deform can only break.
