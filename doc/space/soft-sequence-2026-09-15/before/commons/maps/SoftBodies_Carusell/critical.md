# What hangs from the carousel never chose to spin — orientation as imposed rotation, flesh as centripetal memory

Ahmed writes about being turned. Not turning — being turned. The direction a body faces is not always the direction it chose. Compulsory orientations are the trajectories that power arranges before the body arrives: the corridors, the curricula, the career paths that present themselves as neutral while quietly requiring particular shapes to pass through them. The carousel is a compulsory orientation made mechanical. The soft body did not ask to spin. It was attached to an arm, pinned at a single point, and the hub began rotating. Every force the body now experiences — the centripetal pull outward, the gravity pulling down, the collision with obstacles that appear without warning — follows from a rotation it did not initiate.

The pin joint is the site of this imposed orientation. One vertex, fixed to the rigid arm. All other vertices free to respond to physics. The body's entire trajectory depends on this single attachment — where it was pinned, at what radius, on which arm. Change the attachment point and the swing angle changes, the deformation pattern changes, the collision history changes. Ahmed would recognize the pin joint as the moment of interpellation: the point where the body is hailed into a particular trajectory, where its future motion is determined by forces that preceded its arrival.

But the carousel also reveals something Ahmed pushes toward in her later work: the body does not merely comply with its imposed orientation. It responds with its own materiality. The soft mushroom, spun outward by centripetal force, does not extend uniformly. Its cap — wider, heavier — swings further than its stem. Its particular geometry shapes its particular response to the universal force. The body is not infinitely malleable. It brings its own constraints to the encounter. The spring constants, the mass distribution, the topology of internal connections — these are what the body *is*, and they determine how it negotiates the rotation it was given.

Merleau-Ponty's flesh enters through the cloth straps. When the learner walks through hanging fabric that parts around their body, they are inside the reversibility that Merleau-Ponty describes: the cloth touches them and they touch the cloth. The strap deforms around the hand, recording the hand's shape in its displacement. The hand feels resistance — the springs pushing back, the fabric's memory of its rest configuration asserting itself against the intrusion. Neither party is purely active or purely passive. The cloth perceives the hand through deformation. The hand perceives the cloth through resistance. This is the chiasm: the crossing point where toucher and touched exchange roles so rapidly that the distinction loses meaning.

The centripetal force gradient makes this tangible in a way that gravity alone cannot. Every vertex on the carousel sits at a different radius. Every vertex experiences a different outward pull. The body is not subjected to a uniform force but to a field — stronger further out, weaker closer to the axis. The soft body maps this field through its deformation. The shape it assumes under rotation is a legible record of the force field it inhabits. Merleau-Ponty would call this the body's motor intentionality: not a conscious reaching-toward, but a structural orientation that arises because the body is embedded in and responsive to its environment.

The swing angle — where gravity and centripetal force balance — is never static for a soft body. A rigid pendulum finds one angle and stays. A soft body oscillates, overshoots, wobbles, and settles into a dynamic equilibrium that shifts with every perturbation. The mushroom that strikes an obstacle mid-swing compresses on impact, then rebounds into a new trajectory that recalculates the swing angle from scratch. There is no permanent equilibrium. There is only continuous negotiation, the body always somewhere between where it was and where the forces want it to be.

This is the integration phase made kinetic. The carousel integrates rotation with gravity, rigid constraint with soft compliance, imposed trajectory with material response. The body that hangs from the carousel is both determined (by the pin joint, the angular velocity, the gravitational field) and self-determining (by its stiffness, its topology, its particular way of yielding). Ahmed and Merleau-Ponty agree: the body is never just acted upon. It acts back, through the only means available to it — its own material constitution. The mushroom's elongation under centripetal force is not submission. It is the body's answer to the question the carousel posed.
