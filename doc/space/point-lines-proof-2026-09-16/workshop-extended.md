## Two lines, then another

<!-- @line_space_workshop -->

Start with two. On the first monitor, two straight segments move alongside one another, cross into a plus, then an X. The count stays the same. A relation changes.

Four screens, four studies: two lines, three, four, more. Each arrangement rests for six seconds, then takes five seconds to move into another. The counts sit above the images. There are no buttons to press. Stay long enough for what looked settled to change.

There is no little sculpture above the monitor to settle the image for you. The lines are being arranged in three dimensions elsewhere in the simulation, then filmed into this flat surface. One camera keeps a frontal view; two look obliquely; the camera watching the four-line study slowly turns. Monochrome, ink, contours, luminous scan lines. The screens give the same kind of material different ways to appear.

Move beside a monitor. You see the screen become oblique; you do not see around the object inside its image. Your viewpoint and the filming camera's viewpoint have come apart. Yet the image can still offer depth through shortening, overlap and movement. What begins to look like a body, and when?

The two-line study moves one segment a quarter of a metre in depth. From its fixed frontal camera, the crossing can remain. This view alone cannot tell you that the segments have separated. The code can show the change the image withholds:

```gdscript
# Move both ends of one segment equally: its length stays fixed.
a.z += 0.25
b.z += 0.25
```

Watch the two lines become parallel rails, then a drawing whose ends meet. In one arrangement the two segments remain parallel in space. In the other, two ends really meet. Both may suggest a journey into the distance from a suitable view. The frontal camera can even show rails end-on, as small marks. What did the view supply, and what has it taken away?

Now watch three. Their ends join into a triangle. An outline closes, and you may already begin to supply the face it could carry. No face has been made here.

Watch one whole edge move out of the plane. The oblique image lets you follow the separation. Which joins have opened? Could all three ends remain joined in a non-flat outline? Three non-collinear vertices determine a plane; joining them in pairs cannot produce a closed, non-planar triangle of straight segments. You can carry or turn the triangle in 3D, but it still lies in a plane. To keep closing while leaving that condition behind, we need another possibility.

At four lines, a square waits. Watch one corner fold out of the plane. The filming camera slowly changes its angle too. The outline is still closed. Each side has kept its length. One corner has turned around the diagonal between its two neighbours, carrying its two adjoining edges with it. Four sides let this boundary fold out of a plane without tearing its joins apart.

There is still no surface between them. The folded outline does not tell us how to fill it. That decision will return with triangles: a choice of faces can make different surfaces from the same boundary. For now, notice the opening the outline has helped you see. Your hand can reach the monitor, but cannot enter that pictured opening.

Four also gives us a grid: two lines in each direction. The crossings occur within the spans, rather than at their ends. Is meeting in the middle the same arrangement as joining at a corner?

At the last station, count before naming the form. Six rods make a grid of three by three, with nine crossings. The horizontal rods then lift away, separating the two directions in depth. The same six rods can instead outline a tetrahedron. Six more rods gradually appear as the arrangement becomes a twelve-edge cube. These are arrangements of edges, still without filled faces. More rods give us further choices; the number alone cannot decide which body we will imagine between them.

A segment is one-dimensional in the geometry we are studying. The rod used to render it has thickness; the screen carries pixels. A three-dimensional arrangement can arrive here as a flat image and still ask you to imagine a body. What can you establish from this image, and what would require another view—or access to the rule?
