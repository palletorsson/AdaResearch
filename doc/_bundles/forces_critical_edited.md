# Basis is a convention — the three arrows, the politics of axes, and the labour of decomposition

The three basis vectors stand in the ground like flags planted by an earlier expedition. The i points east, the j points up, the k points north. Every location in the map is written as a combination of these three — P equals x·i plus y·j plus z·k — and the combination is presented as though it were a description of the location rather than a decision about how to describe it.

Sandra Harding's standpoint epistemology insists that every description is from somewhere. The choice of basis is a standpoint. Different flags could have been planted. A mathematician working in spherical coordinates decomposes the same point into radius, polar angle, and azimuth. A sailor using a compass decomposes it into bearing and range. The Cartesian triad is one option among many, and the map's friendliness toward it is a tradition, not a discovery.

The three islands of the map make the tradition walkable. The first island plants the basis vectors as physical arrows. The learner grabs a point in the space, and the map labels it as a triple of coefficients. Moving the point updates the coefficients live. The labels feel like measurements; they are actually projections, and the projections depend on which arrows are planted where.

The second island assembles vectors tip-to-tail. A red arrow followed by a blue arrow produces a green resultant that closes the triangle. The parallelogram rule is visible as a physical consequence rather than as a memorised identity. Harding would note that the procedure — draw A, draw B from the tip of A, close with the resultant — is a protocol. Other protocols produce the same sum, and the protocol's choice was made long before the learner arrived.

The third island runs the operation in reverse. A single vector projects shadow components onto each axis; sliders decompose the vector back into its parts. The decomposition is the labour the map is mostly about. Projecting a vector onto a basis is work, and the work is invisible if the basis is held to be natural. Making the basis explicit — three arrows in the ground — converts the labour from an assumed background into a visible foreground.

A becoming_catalyst pickup sits at the edge of the third island. Retrieving it hands the learner the force catalyst that the rest of the sequence builds on. The catalyst's first appearance is deliberately procedural: the learner has to locate it, pick it up, and carry it across the sequence. The tool is not given; it is acquired, and the acquisition is part of the sequence's argument.

Within the sequence, VectorFoundations is the grammar lesson. It names the basis as a convention the rest of the sequence can rely on but cannot escape. Every later vector operation is a manipulation of the coefficients this map introduces, and the coefficients are always coefficients against a chosen frame.

# Operation is relation — dot, cross, projection, and the politics of turning arrows into scalars

Dot product, cross product, and projection are usually introduced as formulas. One returns a scalar; another returns a vector; the third decomposes an arrow into pieces aligned with a reference. The formulas can be memorised without ever touching the geometric reality they operate on, and a generation of students has learned to compute dot products without being able to say what they measure.

Alfred North Whitehead's process philosophy pushes in the other direction. For Whitehead, entities are what they do; their relations are the primary data, and the things we treat as substances are actually stable patterns of relation. Vectors are Whiteheadian objects. A vector considered alone has magnitude and direction, but neither of those is legible without a reference. The operations are the relations that give vectors their sense.

The first island stages the dot product as alignment. Two arrows meet at the same root; a projection tool drops the shadow of one onto the other; a scalar readout shows A · B equals the product of their magnitudes and the cosine of the angle between them. Rotating one arrow toward the other drives the scalar toward its maximum; rotating it perpendicular drives the scalar to zero. The scalar is the measured agreement between two directions, and agreement is a relation rather than a property.

The second island stages the cross product as displacement. Two input arrows lie in a plane; the cross product rises perpendicular to that plane, its length equal to the area of the parallelogram the inputs span. Reversing the input order flips the result. The handedness of the operation is visible rather than memorised, and the visibility is the point: the cross product's sign depends on which side of the plane the learner is willing to call positive, and that willingness is a convention.

The third island grounds the abstractions mechanically. A ball rests on an inclined surface; gravity decomposes into a component parallel to the slope and a component perpendicular to it. The parallel component moves the ball; the perpendicular component meets the normal force and cancels. The projection tool from the first island is the same tool doing this decomposition, and the equivalence is a small political claim: the same operation that measures alignment also decides how a weight is distributed across a constrained body.

Whitehead would argue that the three operations are three relations, and that the vector is the kind of thing that can be related in these ways. Treating vectors as standalone arrows is a shorthand for a set of possible relations. Dot product is the relation of agreement; cross product is the relation of displacement; projection is the relation of accountability. Without these relations, the arrow is a drawing rather than a mathematical object.

Within the sequence, VectorOperations converts the previous map's basis into operating machinery. The next map will put the machinery to work aiming turrets, driving weather systems, and filling space with instructions. The politics that this map names — that alignment and displacement are chosen relations rather than natural facts — travel forward with the machinery.

# The turret aims — vectors at work and the politics of targeting

A turret is a small machine that decides where to point. The decision is usually hidden in code, but this map exposes it. A target drone moves through the space; the turret subtracts the drone's position from its own, normalises the result, and obtains a unit vector pointing from gun to target. A dot-product check against the current barrel direction decides whether to fire. The arithmetic is live on a panel above the gun, so the decision is legible rather than assumed.

Caroline Criado Perez's work on default design argues that tools are built for a presumed user and that the presumption leaks through into every use. The turret is a presumed user made mechanical. Its default is vigilant. Its default is lethal under conditions the designer chose. The learner watching the turret at work is watching a designer's decisions play out at the speed of arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the part that feels neutral while the decisions do not.

The second station runs a superposed weather system. Gravity, wind, and turbulence are three vector fields stacked on top of one another, and a cloud of light particles drifts through their sum. Each field can be toggled independently. The particles redraw their paths as soon as the composition changes. The superposition is an addition; the emergent behaviour is not. The learner can see that two simple fields and one chaotic one produce a weather pattern that none of them produces alone.

The third station is a field visualiser. A cubic grid of arrow glyphs fills a volume. A selector switches between gravitational, electric, and magnetic fields. The arrows change length and orientation to match. A small test mass released into the grid rides the streamlines the arrows describe. Three different physics, one shared display. The map argues that a field is a way of filling space with instructions, and that the space's behaviour depends on which instructions fill it.

Criado Perez would point at the choice of default fields. Gravity, electric, magnetic — the three that a physics curriculum expects. A different tradition might fill space with tidal fields, or with rotational fields, or with the velocity fields of fluid dynamics. The map's menu is not exhaustive; it is inherited. Choosing one of three is easier than asking why these three and not others.

The turret, the weather, and the field visualiser share a politics. Each is a decision about how arrows act on bodies in a space. Each was made long before the learner arrived. Each is labelled as an operation rather than as a choice, and the labelling is what makes the operation feel inevitable. The map's contribution is to expose the arithmetic on a panel, so the decisions can at least be watched while they play out.

Within the sequence, VectorApplied is where vectors start doing work. The operations from VectorOperations are put to use, and the work has consequence rather than just form. VectorAdvanced will push the consequences further into embodied practice.

# The body throws — vectors in practice and the politics of embodied mathematics

Picking up a ball and throwing it is already a vector operation. The release vector is the ball's velocity at the moment the hand opens. Torque is the cross product of a force's application point and the force itself. Steering is the subtraction of a current velocity from a desired one. These operations are not abstractions; they are things the body does, and the map treats them as embodied labour rather than as equations to derive.

Maurice Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of perception argues that the body is the primary site of knowledge. Before any proposition is thought, the body has already oriented itself in space and acted on objects. The four islands of this map take that thesis seriously. Torque, bouncing, attraction, and throwing are not studied; they are performed, and the performance is the learning.

On the torque island, the learner drags a force vector off-centre on a free-spinning object. The cross product computes the resulting rotational impulse. Shifting the application point changes the torque; reversing the force reverses the spin. The operation is visible because the body does it rather than because an equation names it. Merleau-Ponty would call this operative intentionality: the body's knowledge of how to produce rotation is anterior to the learner's capacity to articulate the rule.

The bouncing island runs a small simulation of a ball under random acceleration. Each bounce projects the incoming velocity onto the surface normal, reflects it, and plays the result. The projection operation from VectorOperations is the same tool doing the collision, and the map marks the equivalence explicitly. The body watching the simulation is watching its own intuitive collision physics rendered as arithmetic.

The attraction island holds a grabbable attractor that pulls a satellite body into orbit. Dragging the attractor reshapes the orbit in real time. The steering demo shows a small agent using the desired-velocity vector to steer toward a moving target, substituting one vector for another each frame. Both stations operate on bodies that respond to vector adjustments, and the responses are immediate rather than computed-then-displayed. The learner grabs; the world moves.

The fourth island converts the learner into the experiment. Picking up a ball and throwing it records the release vector and draws it as a live arrow. The throw's magnitude and direction are the hand's decisions; the arrow is the hand's decision made legible. Muscle memory and arithmetic share a display, and the map does not privilege either over the other. The body's throw is not a noisy approximation of an equation; the equation is a formalisation of the body's throw.

The political register is in the reciprocity. A curriculum that treats embodied knowledge as pre-mathematical — as something the body does before the mind learns to describe it — tends to subordinate the body to the description. This map inverts the subordination. The description is legible because the body performed it, and the performance remains the primary datum.

Within the sequence, VectorAdvanced ends the pure-vector sub-sequence. The forces half of the sequence begins after it, and the tools the learner carries forward are tools they have used with their hands as well as with their minds.

# Newton's laws as compulsory orientations — inertia, F equals ma, and the politics of the jump pad

Newton's three laws are the foundation of classical mechanics. An object at rest remains at rest unless acted on. Force equals mass times acceleration. For every action, an equal and opposite reaction. The laws are usually introduced as discoveries, as though Newton had uncovered a hidden structure in the world. The map prefers a different framing: the laws are compulsory orientations, enforced by physics infrastructure, and the learner encounters them as consequences rather than as theorems.

Sara Ahmed's concept of compulsory orientation names the directions that bodies are forced to face because the world was built for particular trajectories. Gravity is a compulsory orientation at Earth's surface. The map stages three islands connected by a single jump pad, and the jump pad is part of the lesson: the learner does not walk from island to island, they are thrown across, and the throw is a force applied over a short interval. The body's arc is Newton's second law made physical.

The first island hosts the three laws as interactive demonstrations. A sliding block resists a push; raising its mass raises the force required to accelerate it, and the F equals ma relationship is live on a panel. An action-reaction rig pairs two carts on a shared spring so the learner can feel the symmetry of the third law. A low-friction track makes inertia visible as continued motion in the absence of force. The panels convert the laws from abstract rules into felt regularities, and the regularities are the only thing the laws actually claim.

The second island is a projectile range. Cannons of adjustable muzzle velocity launch objects along parabolic trajectories. The learner studies the paths at range, then walks onto the jump pad and becomes the projectile, arcing across the gap to the third island with initial velocity and gravity as the only inputs. Ahmed's orientation analysis lands here: the trajectory is compulsory given the launch conditions, and changing the trajectory requires changing either the launch or the gravity, neither of which is available to the projectile mid-flight.

The third island introduces friction and drag. A ramp lowers at an adjustable angle. Surface-property sliders change the coefficient of friction between block and slope. Air drag on a falling mass is tuned separately. The learner stages the same trajectory on two surfaces and watches how motion degrades differently under each. The degradation is the friction's signature. The signature is not a deviation from the ideal; it is the ordinary case, and the ideal is the abstraction.

The politics of the map are in what counts as friction. A physics textbook treats friction as a nuisance — an inconvenient term that has to be added to the idealised equations. The map treats friction as a first-class force and the frictionless case as the rare exception. Bodies in the real world move across surfaces that resist them, and the resistance is the surface's contribution to the motion rather than an accident of its material.

Within the sequence, Foundations sets the vocabulary that ForcesComposition and ForcesSystems compound. The jump pad threads through as the recurring reminder that Newton's laws are mechanical infrastructure. Every transition between islands is a case study in the laws the map has just named.

# Superposition is a politics — net force, addition of pulls, and the decision to add

The sum of forces determines the acceleration. Two pulls on one body become a single resultant; three pulls become a different resultant; any number of pulls sum into one net vector that Newton's second law applies to. Superposition is the rule, and it feels like arithmetic — forces are added because forces add, tautologically.

Bruno Latour's actor-network theory argues that every apparent unity is the work of summing many heterogeneous contributions. A body moving under many forces is an actor-network whose motion is the emergent result of many pulls. The net force is a description of the result, not a cause of it. The forces that add to produce the net force are the actors. The body is the site where their summation is registered as motion.

The map stages superposition as an uncluttered workbench. Two draggable arrows, A and B, sit in a shared workspace. A panel next to them shows their addition, subtraction, dot product, cross product, projection, and reflection simultaneously, each computed from the current positions. Rotating A rewrites every derived quantity live. Labels on each output name the operation and the formula, and the labels are deliberate: the operations are the work being done, and naming them makes the work visible.

A small demonstration on one side extends the table into dynamics. Several force arrows attach to a single body; their sum is drawn in a contrasting colour as the net force. Adding or removing a force changes the sum immediately. The body accelerates along the net vector, so the learner sees superposition as the rule that turns any set of pulls into a single trajectory. Latour's insight lands directly: the body's trajectory is the summary of the pulls, not a fact about the body itself.

The politics of superposition are in its generality. The rule says that any set of forces can be summed. It does not distinguish between forces the learner wants and forces the learner does not. A body experiences the net of every force acting on it, not the net of the forces it would have chosen. The superposition principle is democratic in an exhausting sense: every contribution counts, and the count is addition.

The workbench stages the generality as a feature rather than as a limit. The panel's six operations — addition, subtraction, dot product, cross product, projection, reflection — are the complete toolkit the sequence's earlier maps built. The map consolidates them into a single table and shows that every operation is available on any pair of vectors, regardless of where the vectors came from. The consolidation is the closing gesture of the pure-vector sub-sequence.

Within the sequence, Composition is the checkpoint. Every vector operation the learner has seen is here, simultaneously, on one workbench. The next maps — Systems, Chaos, Arena — take this toolkit and apply it at population scale. The toolkit is complete; the politics of what to do with it are about to become the substance of the work.

# Population is behaviour — fields, coupling, and the politics of aggregation

A single body under a single force follows a predictable trajectory. A population of bodies under shared forces behaves differently. The aggregate can form orbits, waves, flow patterns, or statistical shapes that no individual body computed. ForcesSystems arranges four such regimes around a clockwise loop of islands, and the loop is the lesson: the same vector primitives produce very different global behaviours depending on how they are organised across a population.

Isabelle Stengers argues that emergence is the term scientists use when aggregation produces behaviour that cannot be reduced to individual parts. Her critique is that the term often functions as a placeholder for unresolved analysis — a way of naming what aggregation does without explaining why. The map takes Stengers's scepticism seriously by refusing to treat emergence as automatic. Each island demonstrates a specific mechanism, and the mechanism is named.

The first island holds attractors. A central mass pulls a set of satellites into orbits; the learner adds or removes attractors and watches the orbits reshape. The aggregation mechanism is gravitational superposition: each satellite feels the sum of every attractor's pull, and the sum determines the orbit. No satellite computes its own orbit; the orbit is the trajectory the summed field produces.

The second island renders a full vector field as a grid of arrows. Test particles released into the field ride the streamlines the arrows describe. A toggle adds a second field, and the two superpose. The aggregation mechanism is the same as on the first island, but the aggregation is over space rather than over sources. The field is the spatial distribution of the summed force, and the particles are probes that read it back.

The third island runs an array of coupled springs. A row of masses connected by identical springs oscillates according to Hooke's law. Striking one mass sends a wave down the row; the map reads off wavelength, amplitude, and damping. The aggregation mechanism is local coupling: each mass feels only its neighbours, and the wave is the propagation of that local coupling across the population. Stengers would note that the wave is a non-trivial achievement of the local rule; calling it "emergent" does not explain it, but watching it propagate does.

The fourth island runs a particle system of many independent agents under shared random forcing. No single particle is remarkable; the aggregate shape is. The aggregation mechanism is statistical: each agent follows a noisy trajectory, and the population's distribution converges to a recognisable shape. The map's panel tracks the distribution's variance and mean as the population evolves, making the statistical signature visible.

The politics of aggregation are in the leap from individual behaviour to population behaviour. A population is not reducible to its individuals; the individuals are not reducible to the population. The loop of islands makes the irreducibility physical: walking from one regime to the next requires accepting that each regime's dynamics belong to it and cannot be transferred. The map does not propose a unified theory of aggregation; it proposes four incompatible examples and lets the incompatibility do the teaching.

Within the sequence, Systems is where single-object dynamics give way to population behaviour. ForcesChaos will next exploit the fact that some of these systems have no closed-form solution at all. The loop hands the learner forward with four regimes in hand and no reduction between them.

# Sensitivity is a politics — the three-body problem, strange attractors, and the limits of prediction

Henri Poincaré's three-body problem proved that three mutually gravitating masses have no closed-form solution. Two bodies produce Keplerian orbits; three bodies produce trajectories that depend on initial conditions so sensitively that no finite computation can predict them over long horizons. The map stages Poincaré's result as a physical gap: two islands separated by a distance that can only be crossed by a ballistic jump, and the jump is the map's argument about the relationship between simple rules and complex behaviour.

N. Katherine Hayles's work on chaos theory argues that the discovery of deterministic chaos reframes prediction as a political rather than merely technical question. A system can be fully determined and yet unpredictable. The predictions that science promises depend on assumptions about sensitivity that the three-body problem invalidates. The map takes Hayles's framing and installs it as architecture: the jump between islands enacts the limit of prediction.

The first island holds gravitational simulations. A two-body system settles into stable orbits; a three-body system does not. The simulation runs live, with sliders for each mass and each initial velocity. A small perturbation to any slider sends a previously stable configuration into ejection, collision, or slow precession. An n-body variant with a larger swarm shows the same sensitivity scaled up. The learner can replay the same configuration with a tiny offset and watch the trajectories diverge after a few orbital periods.

The second island hosts strange attractors. The Lorenz, Rössler, and Aizawa systems draw their trajectories in 3D space. The learner walks through the butterfly shape and sees how two nearby starting points separate as the system evolves. Alongside the attractors, a force-directed graph layout lets the learner grab nodes and watch a network of repulsions and attractions find a local minimum. Neither the attractor nor the graph has a predictable specific trajectory; both have characteristic shapes that the dynamics produce reliably even as the points within them scatter.

The ballistic jump between the two islands is pedagogical. It tells the learner that the gap between simple rules and complex behaviour cannot be walked across in steps. It has to be crossed at once, as an acceptance of the discontinuity. The jump is gravity plus initial velocity — a trajectory governed by exactly the kind of simple rule that, applied to a few more bodies, would become unpredictable. The learner crosses the gap by inhabiting the rule that makes the gap visible.

Hayles would note the political consequence. A world governed by chaos is a world in which long-term prediction is a political claim rather than a technical guarantee. Institutions that promise to predict — weather services, economic forecasters, actuarial models — operate under sensitivity conditions that guarantee their predictions will eventually fail. The question is not whether they will fail but over what time horizon, and the horizon is much shorter for chaotic systems than for Keplerian ones.

Within the sequence, Chaos marks the point where prediction gives way to description. ForcesArena will next ask the learner to act under these conditions rather than only to observe them. The map hands forward a body that has felt deterministic unpredictability and has no illusions about escaping it.

# The exam is the sequence — drone combat, physics destruction, and the politics of accumulated skill

An exam in a curriculum is usually a paper. ForcesArena is a different kind of exam. Three large arenas connected by long jump pads test the learner's accumulated vector and forces knowledge under pressure. The test is not propositional; it is practical, and the pressure is the part that converts the proposition into practice.

Pierre Bourdieu's notion of habitus describes the durable dispositions a body acquires through training. A learner who has spent a sequence manipulating vectors, aiming turrets, tracking superposed forces, and weathering chaotic instabilities has acquired a forces-habitus — a set of reflexes and anticipations specific to this subject matter. The arena tests whether the habitus has formed. It does not test the propositions; it tests the reflexes.

The first arena is a drone combat range. An enemy drone moves, aims, and fires using the same subtraction, normalisation, and dot-product operations the learner studied in VectorApplied. The learner does the same to return fire. The arithmetic is visible on a side panel, so the combat doubles as an open-book exam in which the book is the code the drone is also consulting. Both sides are running the same operations. The question is which side has developed the faster habitus.

The second arena is a physics sandbox. Objects under impact decompose through one of eight fracture algorithms — Voronoi partitioning, Cantor recursion, planar cuts, constructive-solid-geometry booleans, and four others — each selectable from a control at the entrance. The learner hurls objects, triggers fractures, and compares how different rules cut the same geometry. Bourdieu would note that the comparison is itself a habit: deciding which algorithm produces a fracture worth staging is a decision no propositional rule exhausts.

The third arena is an exhibition gallery. Every vector and forces artifact built across the sequence is placed on a plinth with a short label. Walking the gallery is the sequence's self-review. The learner can pick up any tool and drop it back into play. The gallery is the exam's honest part: it displays the curriculum's inventory without hiding the pedagogy behind simulations of enemies.

The politics of accumulated skill are the map's central question. A curriculum produces a habitus, and the habitus is durable. The learner will carry it into situations the curriculum did not anticipate, and the carrying is the curriculum's most important output. The arena tests whether the habitus has taken, and whether the learner can use it when the conditions change.

Within the sequence, Arena is the synthesis. It names what was learned by asking the learner to use it under pressure, and the pressure is the ordinary condition of practice outside the curriculum. The chamber that follows will convert the accumulated skill into a different register — from combat into care — and the transition depends on the body this arena has produced.

# Force is a practice of contact — the kresling, the steady field, and the politics of calming

Chamber_Forces is the catalyst chamber for the Forces sequence, and it is the first place in the sequence where force stops being aimed at targets and becomes a way of relating to another creature. The chamber is small and amber-lit. A kresling_spire creature paces the floor, unsettled on approach. The learner holds the forces catalyst, which projects a slow, steady field rather than an impulse.

Emmanuel Levinas's ethics of the face argues that the other's face makes a demand before any proposition can be spoken. The demand is not for agreement; it is for attention. The kresling_spire's face — its folded origami posture, its drift, its defensive twist — stages that demand physically. The learner cannot ignore it and move on; the chamber is small enough that encountering the creature is the only available activity.

The catalyst's steady field is the learner's response to the demand. Entering the field does not strike the kresling; it slows it. The folded origami eases out of its defensive posture as the applied field's magnitude and direction stabilise. The eashing is a body-level conversation rather than a contest, and the conversation's terms are force parameters: how much, in what direction, for how long. The kresling responds to steadiness, not to force magnitude alone.

The science screen on one wall reads out both bodies as points in a small two-body system. The learner's projection pulls on the creature; the creature's mass pulls back. Newton's third law becomes the chamber's hinge: it is not a mechanical identity but a practice of contact. Every force the learner applies is a force the creature returns. The symmetry is legible on the screen as two traces that mirror each other with a small temporal lag.

Levinas would note the structural asymmetry nevertheless. The learner holds the catalyst; the kresling does not. The learner can enter the chamber and leave; the kresling belongs to it. The power to initiate contact is not shared, even if the forces the contact generates are paired. The map does not pretend otherwise, and the chamber's politics are in acknowledging the asymmetry while using it carefully.

The kresling_spire is the specific creature the chamber is organised around because its defensive posture is a folded origami structure the learner has seen earlier in the curriculum. Seeing the kresling ease out of its twist is seeing the folding vocabulary of the Transformation sequence inhabited by a creature rather than by an object. The chamber draws on the learner's earlier encounters to make this creature legible as a being rather than as a prop.

Within the sequence, Chamber_Forces closes Forces by converting the accumulated vocabulary — fields, superposition, reaction — into a relationship with a creature the learner can calm rather than push. The chamber hands the learner back to the Lab with the forces catalyst in their kit, and with a body-level sense that force is a practice of contact rather than an imposition.
