Weaving a program into cloth

Punch cards, tartan and a surface that opens to show how it was made

Later on 10 October: the hanging chain described below is now built. Its cards advance into the reader one at a time, pause for the shuttle pass and repeat around the loop. The colour controls have moved beside the bed so the growing cloth stays visible. Watch the working cycle in Godot.

The cloth grows one pass at a time. A card selects which threads rise. The shuttle crosses the opening. Another row remains. Change the next card and the cloth begins to differ, while the rows already woven keep their earlier arrangement.

Today we built this relation into Endless Museum: a punch-card loom beside a table where its woven record can open into three dimensions. It sits in Symmetry Seventeen, among the pattern machines, with the earlier hand loom still nearby. The room now gives us several ways to approach repetition: as an image, an instruction, an action and something that has accumulated.

The first machine was deliberately small. Twelve hook choices made the crossing rule readable. Eight removable cards formed the program, with four more waiting on a rack. A visitor could lift a card out, change its holes, return it in another position and watch the next pass. This makes an ordered list tangible. Moving an entry changes what happens when the machine reaches it.

A hole has a particular job: it selects a warp thread to rise. The warp runs along the cloth; the weft crosses it. The colour belongs to the yarn. Keeping those decisions separate became the basis of the tartan experiment.

We added two colour sequences, one for each direction. Our small authored sample repeats navy, green, cream and red in a two-over, two-under twill. The lift shifts with successive passes, producing the diagonal crossings. The colour sequence determines the stripes. The Scottish Register of Tartans describes this relation through the threadcount: the ordered colours and the number of threads assigned to each stripe. Our sample uses that principle without claiming a named historical tartan. Threadcount guidance

The interface had to respect what had already happened. Changing a future weft colour leaves the current pass and earlier rows intact. Changing the warp requires an explicit rethreading and a fresh sample. Each finished pass keeps the lift choices and yarn colours used to make it. The cloth can therefore show a decision made before the present settings existed.

This is useful to the tutorial lineage. Arrays return as ordered choices; loops return as repeated passes; colour returns as material distributed along two directions. The student can already recognise the ingredients. The new question is how they meet, and what must be kept when the next operation begins. The wider museum can hold the whole machine while a tutorial selects one of these relations to reconstruct.

Then came a visual objection: the threads were too sparse. We could read the diagram, but we wanted the attraction of cloth—the point where individual crossings gather into a surface.

The sample now has 96 warp threads. The twelve-hook pattern repeats eight times across its width. A batch control weaves 96 actual passes through the card and colour program, producing a sample about 2.4 metres square on the inspection table. The yarns are flatter, closely packed and matte. Their surface has subtle filament shading. This is still an enlarged textile, with room to see its construction.

Making it denser created another problem: the structure became harder to inspect. The slider answers that by opening the same sample. Successive passes rise through space; yarn widths narrow for visibility and the over-and-under height is exaggerated. A flat diagram remains below, showing which thread is uppermost at each crossing. Close the slider and those same paths gather back into cloth.

The transformation runs on the GPU, using the existing mesh while the hand moves the control. It leaves the recorded choices untouched. The opening is an explanatory transformation, not a simulation of pulling a real fabric apart. Tension, friction and deformation remain outside this model. Its exactness is in the relation between the instructions, the recorded crossings and their visible arrangement.

The latest reference photograph brought the machine itself back into focus. Its cards hang in a long, laced chain. The program occupies space: it has an order, a length, folds and weight. That physical arrangement suggests a clearer next version of our loom, with one card advancing into a visible reader on each pass and the finished cloth hanging below.

The Science Museum Group's collection includes card chains accompanied by samples of the ribbon they produced. Instruction and result can be held together as objects. That is a productive reference for Ada: the visitor should be able to connect a choice in the program with a consequence in the cloth. Jacquard cards and woven samples

Our current cards still occupy fixed numbered sockets. The hanging chain is the next design proposal. It could make the duration and order of the program more apparent without asking the visitor to read another explanation.

We checked the working version in Godot: 58 runtime assertions and nine museum checks passed, including retained history, colour changes, repeated crossings and opening the existing mesh. The museum checks also covered the installed pair and clear standing approaches. Simulated controllers exercised the interactions; physical headset use remains to be tried.

What matters for the research is the movement between these distances. We can enjoy the tartan as a surface, approach until a crossing becomes legible, and open the construction without losing the pattern we came to inspect. The cards carry instructions. The yarn carries colour. The cloth keeps the result, including choices we have since changed.
