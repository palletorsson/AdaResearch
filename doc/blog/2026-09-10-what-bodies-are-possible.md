A door can grow a little at a time. At some particular clearance, a body can pass.

The parameter changes gradually. The consequence arrives as a possibility that was unavailable a moment ago. The door has not acquired a new hole. What counts as an exit has changed through a relationship between bodies.

“What bodies are possible?” has been a recurring question in Ada Research. It now gives us a way to organise the work: what can exist with the tools available here, and what becomes possible when we learn to combine them differently?

We have made a [working guide from Colour to Forces](/research/possible-bodies/index.html). It covers every hall in that part of the generated museum order, with the current primary artifacts and book anchors beside a proposed question, lead encounter, handoff and next action. The guide is a dated source review. Its proposals are available to test; they have not silently rearranged the museum.

![Thirty-five coloured pillars stand across a spatial gradient, each sampling a position in the field.](/blog-images/2026-09-10-what-bodies-are-possible/pillars.png)

*An isolated Godot development capture of the enlarged pillar field. The colour at a position can become a visible sample. A different receiving system could use that value otherwise; that connection would need to be implemented.*

A wall, a garment, a creature and a landscape can share an algorithm or a source of data. Their separation into categories is partly authored. We can let the learner inspect those choices and construct other relationships.

Sharing a computational medium does not supply every crossing automatically. Colour becoming height needs a mapping. A rendered surface becoming ground needs collision. An animated body becoming a force-driven one needs a model of support and motion. Those additions are places for the research to become specific. Which operation makes the crossing possible, and what does it fail to carry across?

The Turing connection gives this question two directions. His morphogenesis paper investigates how reactions and diffusion can generate differentiated patterns from a near-uniform state. It proposes conditions under which structure can form. [Turing, The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis, 1952](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/gold/pdfs/teaching/old_literature/Turing1952.pdf).

The imitation game asks what performance, through a deliberately restricted interface, permits a machine to be judged alongside human participants. Its opening arrangement includes a man, a woman and an interrogator; the machine is then introduced into that game. The participants' physical appearances are withheld. [Turing, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, 1950](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~vaughan/teaching/889/papers/turing1950.html).

We read these together as questions about **formation and recognition**: what can become a body, and under what conditions do we encounter it as one? This is an interpretive connection for Ada, rather than a claim that Turing stated the same question in both papers.

The question can land at several levels. A foreground artifact can acquire geometry or behaviour. A surface can acquire a pattern someone wants to wear. A terrain can permit a gait, conceal a body or make a gathering possible. A player can gain a new way to act. Another agent can begin to respond. A background system can become something whose activity changes the encounter.

These levels can exchange roles. The object we examined on a table becomes the ground under our feet. What appeared to be decoration changes another agent's response. A creature supplies a passage. The unfamiliar need not arrive as an enemy before it can matter to us.

![Dressed agents share the museum catwalk, with a fan-shaped outfit in the foreground and other silhouettes farther along the runway.](/blog-images/2026-09-10-what-bodies-are-possible/catwalk.png)

*The current catwalk puts player and other wearers into one encounter. Their outfits share a builder. The four looks are authored; the pattern machines do not yet dress them with the visitor's own motif. Walking is choreographed, with proximity-based yielding.*

This lets us treat each hall as a simulation within the museum's continuing simulation. A hall establishes local conditions that can be investigated. The museum carries a visitor between them, with memory and an expanding toolbox. We must decide which changes persist in the program as well as which persist in the learner's understanding. “Carry this forward” can mean a remembered distinction, a stored motif or an unlocked capability; the text should say which one it means.

The proposed thread through the present work is:

| Sequence | What becomes available to investigate | What the next sequence receives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colour | Appearance through light, context, chosen values and receiving surfaces. | An addressed colour choice that can become part of a motif. |
| Patterns | Shared sources, repetition, symmetry and rendered surfaces. | A distinction between changing a value and changing the rule that relates values. |
| Change | Rates, accumulated contributions, trajectories and sampling. | A language for distinguishing position, velocity and acceleration. |
| Forces | Interactions and constraints that alter motion and access. | Bodies whose possibilities depend on relationships with other bodies and fields. |

Transformation remains underneath this passage. It supplies ways to move, turn and scale a construction. Arrays supply ways to address its parts. Returning to those tools in a new setting can reveal something the earlier room could not yet ask.

This thread helps us choose relevant artifacts. A primary encounter should let the visitor perform the room's central comparison, observe a consequence, or find a limit in its claim. Sometimes one artifact is enough. Sometimes two are needed to keep a difference visible. In Legs, all seven walkers can belong to one primary comparison: reducing them to an arbitrary pair would remove much of the question about possible body plans.

Supporting artifacts can remain. A room with developed prose should not be emptied to satisfy a number. Where the book asks the reader to undertake an additional encounter, we need to decide whether it belongs on the primary path or in an explicitly marked optional return. The prose and the role assignment should then agree about that decision.

The review found specific work to do. The four sequence files name 32 maps; the generated museum order adds four Change ribbon halls. Across those 36 halls, 25 have `final.md` and 11 do not. Twelve need a review of book anchors against primary assignments, including the four Change ribbons with no primaries assigned. These counts describe file state, not the quality of the encounters.

Several transitions also remember an earlier route. Flashlight's older documentation calls it a closing room, although it now stands second in Colour. The symmetry text hands directly to Change, but three Patterns ribbons intervene. In Forces, an opening claims that the vectors previously held still after the visitor has already passed Launch and Motion. Such sentences matter: they tell a solitary reader what experience the book expects them to have had.

The first next action is small enough to finish: visit **Color_Flashlight**, verify how grabbing, aiming and overlapping the lamps work, then write its missing book passage around the same surface under different illumination. The object is already placed and primary. The prose can begin with a discovery the room can support.

After that, Rainbow needs its actual colour rule checked before the text calls it a spectrum. Grid Pallet needs a verified account of addressing and changing a cell. The colour chamber needs an account of what its chromatic catalyst actually does. The recovered ribbon collections need distinct teaching jobs, or an explicit later decision about their place on the route.

Some improvements can travel. Keeping one thing fixed helps with colour context, symmetry rules, friction and gait comparisons. Tracing the receiving body helps distinguish preview hands from the wearer, painted surfaces from collision surfaces, and field diagrams from forces acting on the player. Comparing configurations before consolidating prevents a repeated token from being mistaken for a redundant encounter.

The next larger experiment would carry one asymmetric motif between a tile, an outfit and a terrain. Its source would remain inspectable. Each new use would show the adapter that lets the same stored decisions acquire another consequence. We would build and test one connection first, then decide whether it serves another room.

“What bodies are possible?” can therefore stay open while giving the work a concrete direction. Each hall adds a principle; each principle changes what we can try. The next body may be one we could not have specified before making the attempt.
