An Ada hall holds more relations than one text can follow. A moving sphere offers an encounter with chance. A seed opens a question about repetition. A book of random digits leads to machines, experiments and the work of preparing numbers. These connections can coexist in the room. On the page, a reader must meet them in some order.

That is the writing problem: how to give a network of knowledge the duration of a story. A route must make sense, but it should also leave someone wanting to follow it. The order of information changes what an object can become for its reader.

*Sixteen cells per panel, three colour draws per cell. The seed is the same; one discarded draw changes the grouping. Actual Godot screenshot.*

Ada gives each hall two texts with different responsibilities. **The tutorial teaches one representative example, built from an identified inheritance.** The point, cube, arrays, materials and earlier operations return when they remain useful. We name and link the code already learned. We print the complete new part, or an exact replacement, and explain its mechanism. The student should be able to predict a change, run it and explain the result. Referring to earlier code requires a reliable route back to it.

**The final is a literary encounter in a continuing conceptual world.** It can stay with an object, follow a historical problem, state an exact definition, become critical or strange, and return to the room with another possibility. History needs enough context to matter. Queer possibility needs a particular relation it can disturb or invent. Pleasure, embarrassment, humour and surprise also have work to do here. A passage about an algorithm can leave someone wanting to encounter it again.

Both texts teach. Their distinction lies in what they enable the reader to do next. The tutorial needs a definition to make an operation intelligible. The final may need the same definition because an apparent freedom has a precise boundary. Neither text is served by assuming that a novice understands something simply because an experienced reader recognises its name.

Palle describes the problem as a network that must become linear. An object stands among many relations, like a node in an Obsidian graph. A reader encounters sentences one after another. Writing selects a route, sets its pace and gives each arrival a reason. The unused connections remain possible. Every route makes some relations easier to encounter and others harder.

Our OOO orientation gives the objects a place from which those relations can depart. The graph records our approaches to an object; it cannot exhaust what that object is. Nor does an object need to speak in a human voice for its behaviour to interrupt a sentence. A page whose numbers fall away complicates the promise made by its resemblance to a book.

The **research pool** beside the prose holds more than the current route needs. Each connection has a specific claim, evidence and possible relations to other claims. Installed behaviour, historical evidence, editorial interpretation and an unbuilt proposal receive different labels. Selected material forms the route. Reserve branches keep material that this text cannot use well. A gap requiring research stays visible instead of disappearing inside a convincing transition.

A timeline then asks what each paragraph does. Calling a paragraph “history” is too coarse. It tells us what kind of information enters, but little about what happens to the reader.

| On the paragraph card | A decision in Random Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Information | The seed is unchanged; the right grid discarded one value. |
| Job | Complicate the expectation that a seed names a picture. |
| Attitude | Curious and exact, allowing the changed picture to be desirable. |
| Reader's gain | Distinguish the starting state from the use made of its values. |
| Bridge | Follow three successive requests into red, green and blue. |
| Size | Give the explanation room without restarting the Colour lessons. |

These are instructions to the writer. They need not become headings in the reader's text. The bridge deserves particular attention. “Add some history” gives us another section. A question about recovering chance gives a historical number table a reason to enter.

The [writing-patterns library](/writing-patterns) offers routes and smaller paragraph movements to combine. One route might begin with a discrepancy, explain its cause and follow the consequence. Another might move from an encounter into history, then return with a different question. Neither is automatically right for an Ada hall. The available knowledge, the object and the reader's inheritance determine what the route can carry.

Source studies of Haraway, Deleuze and Guattari, Ahmed, Kittler, Bachelard and Carroll examine bounded passages: what enters, what changes, and how one paragraph makes the next possible. A few paragraphs cannot establish an author's universal formula. Their philosophical commitments also differ. A movement of attention can suggest an Ada experiment without making these writers interchangeable. The [provenance record](/book-review/doc/book/course/writing-patterns/PROVENANCE.md) distinguishes these readings from inherited presets whose originating passages are not established.

Consider two openings for Random Definition. Begin with the grids and the reader meets a technical discrepancy: the seed stays while a picture changes. Begin with the ball and the reader enters a journey whose destination they have not chosen. The same explanation of a seeded procedure will arrive into different expectations.

![A dark sphere away from its marked centre in the museum.](/book-images/blog/2026-10-13-a-room-of-connections-a-line-of-text/sphere-out.png)

*Hold the sphere and its excursion carries you. Release and it continues without you. Direction and distance vary within a range; returning belongs to the rule. The sphere is paused in this Godot photograph.*

The ball's route gives the body a role before the text defines the mechanism. The seed bench offers another kind of return. RAND's table keeps numbers available on a page; the museum's page lets them fall away. An imagined sleeve then asks for a different capacity from the coloured grid. Joined neighbours, bending and weight would have to be supplied. A wish to wear the picture becomes a reason to ask how its rules might change.

Evidence keeps these movements distinct. A screenshot establishes a visible arrangement. An interaction test can check that release stops the sphere carrying the player. A primary source can support the history of the number table. None of these establishes that a reader understands the passage or wants to continue.

The ghost student helps test the tutorial's side of that problem. Starting with the declared inheritance, the imagined student reconstructs what the printed instructions make possible. An absent file or an unexplained dependency can become an observable failure. A reading trial has another task: finding an exhausted transition, a historical detour that arrives too early, or a strange possibility that makes the lesson worth continuing. We need both kinds of attention.

A size allowance gives the selection consequences. If a chapter keeps growing, it may contain two independent learning problems, an unexplained prerequisite, or too much of the research pool. Another map may be appropriate. So may a companion page or a branch left unused. Word count prompts a judgement about scope; it does not make that judgement for us.

The human checkpoint keeps Palle's judgement active. It names what the passage achieves, where evidence ends and which question would improve the next reading. An Ada alignment score is useful only with its reasons attached. It cannot certify literary quality. A more useful question points to a transition: after the changed picture, does the book feel like somewhere the reader wants to go?

The pool can remain expansive while the text makes a particular journey. The reader needs enough room at each stop to encounter something, understand why it is here, and feel a reason to take the next step.
