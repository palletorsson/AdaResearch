A row of five-digit numbers enters at the top of a page. It slides down. Another follows. Touch one group and it stays amber while the others continue past its address.

This is the latest version of the falling number page in Ada Research. We slowed its arrival to one row every two and a half seconds after discussing the chapter that surrounds it. Whether that pace feels right in the room still needs an encounter. But the reason for changing it had already become clear in the reading: keeping one thing while others disappear had begun to hold the chapter together.

The room contains instruments about pseudorandom generators, seeds, timing, physical-source models, movement and probability. Putting these in a plausible teaching order was relatively easy. Making one encounter create a need for the next was harder. A sequence can be accurate and still feel like a catalogue.

The question that changed the work was what the reader was attending to. In the number-book passage, Palle noticed the difference between a number being produced, recorded and selected. After reading the complete sequence, he identified a recurring action: “keeping something while something else disappears.” The seed keeps a beginning. A page keeps numbers. A touch keeps one group. A recording keeps measured intervals. A gesture becomes a seed, while the fuller record remains something more than that integer.

These operations were already in the room. The reading brought a relation among them into focus. That relation could guide the prose and suggest changes to the objects.

The chapter's title, “What chance is allowed to do,” acquired a more precise question. Random values have consequences through the systems that receive them. At one exhibit, the values become colours. At another, they become heights or rotations. A dark sphere can carry the visitor on an excursion whose direction and distance vary, but whose return is prescribed. Another random draw changes the journey within that rule. To change the obligation to return, we have to alter the apparatus.

That is the kind of tension we have been calling a candidate gem. Its subject may be familiar; its particular relation makes another experiment imaginable. It has to survive contact with an object and a reader. Calling something a gem does not settle whether it is interesting.

The next request was smaller and just as consequential: explain a little more of what we actually see. The writing had moved too quickly from an instrument's name to its meaning. “Frame trace” assumed a reader who already knew what the line measured. “Hash” named an operation without giving someone a way to understand the gesture becoming a number.

We revised the passages to stay with their objects. The frame study draws differences in the time between engine updates. Holding the recording and changing the vertical scale makes the same intervals look more or less agitated. No new timing event is needed. At MOVE / KEEP, six coloured traces show changes in position and rotation as a small sphere is carried and turned. The text now introduces hashing where the released gesture becomes a seed: a fixed calculation makes a compact code from the measurements, and that code is reduced to a bounded number.

An inline explanation can let the reader stay with the encounter. It gives them enough to ask a better question without sending them away to acquire a vocabulary first. It also gives the research something to check. Can someone say what the marks represent? Can they identify what changed and what remained? Does that distinction make them want to try anything?

This suggests a small working loop. Begin with an encounter and a tension. Keep a baseline. Change one condition. Preserve what happened. Replay it, and reconstruct it when reconstruction is the question. Hear what a reader or visitor notices before explaining what they were supposed to notice. Then revise the writing, the apparatus or the question.

The two texts in Ada Research enter this loop differently. The tutorial develops the capacity to rebuild and vary an operation. The literary text follows what an object opens in thought, history, attachment and possible action. Either can propose an experiment, and either can be changed by its outcome. A sentence may ask for an instrument we have not built. A working instrument may make a sentence untenable.

We need to retain several kinds of evidence without confusing them. A headless test can establish that the falling page admits rows at the intended cadence and preserves selected cells. It cannot establish that a hand finds the interaction legible or that the passage sustains attention. A reader's response can reveal a connection the writer missed. It cannot by itself prove a technical claim about a source of randomness.

The latest final is longer because it now carries the missing descriptions and explanations. That is a tradeoff to test in the complete reading. The next comparison uses a held frame recording at two display scales, followed by the same simulated Geiger events read with two clock resolutions. In one case the picture changes; in the other, a measuring procedure loses a distinction. Can that difference become something the visitor can investigate?

AI makes it easier to move between a proposed sentence, a revised scene and a test. It also makes it easier to produce plausible explanations faster than anyone can encounter them. We are recording this process so that the speed of making does not become our evidence of learning. The next direction should come partly from what the object and its reader make difficult, surprising or worth keeping.
