Four pale blue pyramids mark the corners of a grey platform. A pink spire rises from a matching block at the centre. Ochre cubes gather around it. We have learned enough geometry to begin describing every object here. The arrangement still gives us a reason to look.

![Four blue corner spires surround ochre cubes and a taller pink centre on a raised platform, with the Dürer tableau further along the museum.](/blog-images/2026-09-16-enough-to-continue/geometric-court.png)

*The opening court in Primitives Melencolia, captured inside the actual endless museum on 16 September. Its colours and proportions follow the manuscript image supplied for the room; the dimensions adapt that composition for the museum.*

This is where the first chapter of Ada Research now arrives. Ten halls lead from a point to a scene after Dürer's *Melencolia I*. The game, the artifact choices and the book have been revised together. This post takes stock of how that chapter reads, following the earlier [Bring the detour](/blog/2026-09-08-bring-the-detour).

The first words of the book are “You arrive late.” A counter has already accumulated frames. The engine has been doing work before we found its number. We stand in a room we could not yet build, borrowing decisions we have not learned to inspect.

At the other end, Melencolia offers more possible constructions than we have time to pursue. That is a thread through the whole chapter. Beginning means choosing what to examine. Continuing means carrying a question while leaving other invitations open.

The technical progression gives those choices somewhere to land:

| Hall | What becomes available |
|---|---|
| [Point One](/book?map=Point_One&section=final) | Locate a position; distinguish its address from a visible, responsive body. |
| [Lines](/book?map=Point_Lines&section=final) | Connect two positions; distinguish the segment from the journey between them. |
| [Trace](/book?map=Point_Trace&section=final) | Keep sampled positions; inspect the decisions that admit a movement into a record. |
| [Grid](/book?map=Point_Line_Grid&section=final) | Give places shared addresses; repeat a placement and find another use for a drawing. |
| [Triangle](/book?map=Point_Triangle_Context&section=final) | Close a boundary, add a face, then test what the filling method can handle. |
| [Polyhedra](/book?map=Primitives_Polythedra&section=final) | Enclose a region; distinguish what the eye sees from what a body can pass through. |
| [Animated Cube](/book?map=Point_Animatedcube&section=final) | Move a vertex, expose a diagonal, and recognise the same box vocabulary in the museum. |
| [Ignorance](/book?map=Primitives_Ignorance&section=final) | Inspect the polygonal body that a familiar name lets us overlook. |
| [Portals](/book?map=Primitives_Portals&section=final) | Approach a limit and decide what would be enough for a particular use. |
| [Melencolia](/book?map=Primitives_Melencolia&section=final) | Compose the familiar forms; stay with what an exact answer leaves open. |

Some of the connections are literal. A drawing released in Trace can arrive in Grid as a list of positions. The next room centres it, enlarges it and rounds it again. A small bend made by a wrist becomes something we might want to follow with a larger body. The book can ask what survived because there is a particular record to inspect.

Elsewhere, a construction supplies its own difficult question. The triangle tool fills an L-shaped outline by making a fan from its first vertex. Begin at another corner and the same rule sends faces across the notch we wanted to leave empty.

![Two versions of the same L-shaped boundary show a triangle fan fitting from one starting corner and spilling into the notch from another.](/blog-images/2026-09-16-enough-to-continue/triangle-fan.svg)

*A diagram of the book's six-corner experiment, rather than an engine screenshot. The boundary is unchanged. Input order changes the fan's fill.*

That discrepancy has a cause we can understand. We can choose another starting point, change the outline, or develop another filling method. We could also want the excess patch for a different work. A wing, perhaps, or a pleat. Finding a use for it does not make it a correct fill of the original outline. It makes another question possible.

This is how we want the critical investigation to meet the tutorial. Learn an operation closely enough to discover what it permits, where it fails a particular intention, and what else can be made with it. A restriction can produce a form we had not expected. The five-sided capsule becomes interesting when its next side refuses our imagined symmetry. The coarse grid gives a movement an interval to dance within.

In Animated Cube, the reader finally looks down. The floor slabs and wall blocks belong to the family of forms under examination. These meshes and their collision bodies have already been carrying and redirecting us. Understanding the exhibit begins to change how we understand the place containing it. The implementation can make a boundary consequential for a body; its consequences deserve the same close attention as its geometry.

![An elevated view of the museum shows the coloured geometric court connected by a raised route and ramp to a separate platform carrying the Dürer tableau.](/blog-images/2026-09-16-enough-to-continue/court-and-tools.png)

*The court comes first; the tools wait on a second platform. Both are actual museum structures. Their encounter order gives the closing text its movement.*

Melencolia lets this accumulated knowledge become an arrangement. The pyramids are recognisable, but their relations can suggest a monument, a court, a place we might want to enter. Further on, the numbered square gives a test we can finish. Its rows, columns and main diagonals sum to 34. That result does not finish looking at the scene.

Pleasure, uncertainty and attention keep the investigation going. The aesthetic work includes arranging, noticing and wanting to try something else. We can know how a form was made and still be drawn towards it.

The chapter has work ahead. Point One asks the reader to hold more than the later halls do. The repeated invitation to leave other questions for a return needs a less predictable rhythm. A reader away from the museum needs more diagrams and small worked constructions. These are tasks for the next iteration. The current sequence gives us enough continuity to move forward, while headset experience and learning with visitors remain to be tested.

We now enter [Trans_Pre](/book?map=Trans_Pre&section=final). A still cube begins to rise and fall. Another adds turning; another adds swelling. The parts of the mesh can retain their relations while the body occupies a different place, faces another direction or changes size. Later, those operations will carry us across a gap or alter the room around us.

The question travels with us: **what must remain for this to be the same body, and what becomes possible when something changes?**

The first chapter has given us forms and ways to question them. There is enough here to continue.
