# What the fractals chapters can learn from *Nature of Code* ch. 8

Read 25 September 2026 against the seven `final.md` of the `fractals` sequence (6,283 words: Recursion 861 · RecursiveTrees 691 · CantorSet 783 · KochSierpinski 1,261 · MengerSponge 825 · GoldenSpiral 1,076 · Synthesis 786). Source: https://natureofcode.com/fractals/ (Daniel Shiffman, 2nd ed.). No agents; the artifacts' code and the maps were read by hand.

## First fact: the halls already run on Shiffman's code

Six of the sequence's artifacts are ports of the chapter's numbered examples — `example_8_3_recursion_circles_vr` (the rings desk), `example_8_4_cantor_set_vr` / `_pagoda_vr`, `example_8_5_koch_curve_vr`, `example_8_6_recursive_tree_vr`, `example_8_7_stochastic_tree(_separated)_vr`, `example_8_9_lsystem_tree_vr` (placed as `fractal_lsystem_tree`); `example_8_8` (the A→ABA string generator) exists in `algorithms/fractals/` but is not registered and stands nowhere. Tasks .003/.004 already ask for the code credit. So the question is not "what code to borrow" but which of the chapter's *moves* the ports dropped when the code crossed over.

The spine order is the book's order: randomness → noise → cellularautomata → **fractals** → lsystems (NOC: ch. 7 CA → ch. 8 fractals, with L-systems inside 8). The bridges Shiffman builds between those chapters are therefore available to Ada for free.

## Where Ada is already ahead (keep)

- **The monster paradox is walked, not stated.** NOC: length 1 → 4/3 → 16/9 → ∞ "in a finite space". Ada: 256 segments, twelve and a half metres of path between endpoints four metres apart, and the visitor walks it. Better.
- **Box-counting dimension** (`box_counting_dimension`): NOC has no fractal dimension at all. Ada's RANGE button — the slope moves while the points stay — is a genuine edge (which fit would you have called the dimension?).
- **Removal as room**: Cantor pagoda gaps that admit some bodies and not others; Menger openings you fly through and ones the capsule cannot. NOC has nothing of the body. This is the sequence's own subject ("Room to Move") and it is right.
- **Procedure vs appearance**: the recursive chair with no self-call; the Sierpiński pyramid's six calls where the comment promised five. NOC never doubts a picture.

## What to learn (nine items, all grounded in what already stands in the halls)

1. **Say what a fractal is, once, from the hall's own objects.** NOC gives Mandelbrot's definition (a part is a reduced copy of the whole), then a cheap negative test: a straight line is self-similar and is *not* a fractal — fine structure at every scale is the second condition; "if you can say it's a line, it isn't". Ada's 6,283 words never define the word; "self-similar" never appears. The squares desk in Recursion is the place: seven squares, each a half-copy — and then the edge: a line would pass the copy test too.
2. **Deterministic vs stochastic self-similarity is standing in the halls and never named.** NOC's ladder: the deterministic tree is "too perfect"; add random angles and counts and it "goes too far"; the remedy is Perlin noise for gradual variation (Ex. 8.9) — which is Ada's noise sequence, two rooms back. RecursiveTrees stands `recursive_tree_2` (no randomness; GROW/ANGLE/LENGTH/FORKS), `recursive_tree` (seeded), and `fractal_lsystem_tree` (NOC 8.9, a grammar) and calls two of them "the downward trees, the mesh fractal". CantorSet stands `stochastic_tree_separated` (unseeded `randomize()`, branches repel level by level after growth) and gives it three words. One paragraph in each hall makes the ladder: the same rule, three ways of choosing the next fork.
3. **The order of drawing is a subject (NOC Ex. 8.6: number the branches in the order they are actually drawn).** `recursive_tree.gd:3` "A queue evaluates a recursive branching rule one tip at a time" — GROW is breadth-first. A function that calls itself (`branch()` with `push()`/`pop()`) draws one side to its tip first — depth-first. Ada's chapter has the fact ("Growth here is a queue of instructions") and misses the point: the same tree, two visiting orders — the tide and the diver from GT_Foundations, five sequences earlier. That is an encounter (repetition class): press GROW and predict *which* branch comes next.
4. **One mechanism, three times.** NOC says it outright: the Koch `segments`/`next` arrays are the Game of Life's current/next generations, and the L-system's `current`/`next` strings are the same swap. Ada's Koch chapter has the list ("The next call receives these segments as its beginning") and neither looks back to the CA hall nor forward to the string. One sentence.
5. **Cantor as a grammar is the cleanest bridge into lsystems.** NOC: alphabet A B, axiom A, rules A→ABA, B→BBB, A draws and B moves — "Look familiar?" — and the Cantor set appears. Ada's Synthesis handover reaches for the grammar lab's *Koch* preset, which is the 90° quadratic Koch (`lsystem_editor.gd:103` `F → F+F-F-F+F`), not the snowflake — the chapter already has to apologise for that. `lsystem_editor` has no Cantor preset; `grammar_operations.json` already says `delete` is "taught_by cantor_set". Add the preset (two rules, one line each) and the handover can say: the interval you cut five times is two letters and two rules in the next hall.
6. **Name the makers, and the makers before Mandelbrot.** NOC gives dates (Cantor 1883, von Koch 1904, Lindenmayer 1968, Mandelbrot 1975 and the Latin *fractus*) and a paragraph on fractal structure in Ba-ila village layouts and Islamic geometry "long before the formal study of fractals in Western mathematics". Ada's seven chapters name nobody. Since Boids got Reynolds and GT_Foundations got Euler, one footnote per hall is the pattern — and the pre-Mandelbrot paragraph is the sequence's self-colonial-recognition beat, waiting (Eglash, *African Fractals*, 1999, is the reference NOC is paraphrasing).
7. **Segments as objects so they can move (NOC Ex. 8.3: make each segment a spring).** Shiffman's reason for the `KochLine` class is that a segment can then "move independently … participate in a physics simulation". Ada's Koch is that list already, and "the sculpture has no collision". In a sequence called Room to Move, the only body that moves is the visitor's, in the sponge. A Koch segment the hand can pull is a design decision (softbodies has the springs); the chapter can at least say the segments are things.
8. **The hall's own flora is a fractal the visitor walks past.** NOC's Ecosystem Project asks for plantlike creatures, leaves, fruit that can be picked. Ada's `soft_stages.json` already grows the fractals stage (order 10) with `fractal_flora`, kingdoms flower/tree/fungus, density 0.6, terrain "growth", ambient "recursive_world" — and no chapter mentions that the trees in the biome ring and the trees on the desks are (or are not) the same rule. Check the flora builder, then one paragraph.
9. **Exercises that change one thing.** NOC ends each section with a small modification ("make the trunk thick and each branch thinner", "draw the snowflake", "leaves at the ends"). Ada's desks *are* the exercises (ANGLE, LENGTH, FORKS, PACKING, DUST), which is better — but the chapters' closing questions are mostly "what would we have to add"; NOC's are "do this now". Where a desk already has the button, end on the button.

## Not to learn

- The first-person, joking voice ("I dare you", pizza defined as slices of pizza). Ada's museum voice is a decision.
- The Mandelbrot set: NOC defers it too. The sequence is about rules, scale and room; escape-time is another sequence's question.
- NOC's reassurance that infinity "won't be a factor" in finite pixels. Ada's better line is already there: "We have not displayed that limit. We have made a finite instrument for approaching its rule."
