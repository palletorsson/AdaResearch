# ML_Neural_Networks — Critical Reflection

A corridor of chambers connected by bottlenecks. Data enters one end and emerges the other end transformed. Three wider chambers hold three artifacts — `neural_networks_vr`, `neural_network_visualization`, `learn_world_stacked` — each showing the same architecture from a different angle. The bottleneck passages between chambers are width-1 corridors through h5 walls. You are the data. The architecture is the network. The chokepoints are the hidden layers.

Hayles writes that in the computational universe, pattern and randomness replace presence and absence as the fundamental categories. The neural network is the machine that converts randomness into pattern. Each layer transforms its input — `a(l) = sigma(W(l) * a(l-1) + b(l))` — and the transformation is learned, not designed. The weights are adjusted through backpropagation, gradients flowing backward through the same layers the signal flowed forward, and the learning rate determines whether this process converges or oscillates. The network does not store answers. It learns features. Edges become textures become objects. Each layer throws away what the loss function does not reward and amplifies what it does.

The three artifacts create an arc within the chamber sequence. The first shows the full network architecture in 3D. The second shows the learning process — neurons glowing with activations, weights shimmering with gradients, the error graph descending. The third stacks learned representations, showing how abstraction accumulates layer by layer. Together they narrate the network as transparent mechanism: here is the structure, here is the learning, here is what it learned. This transparency is the deepest fiction in the map.

A neural network is not transparent. It is the paradigmatic opaque system. The hidden layers encode features no human specified, in combinations no human can parse, distributed across thousands of parameters that interact nonlinearly. The network works but cannot explain why. Hayles identifies this as the condition of the computational unconscious — processes that operate below the threshold of human awareness, shaping decisions without producing understanding. The visualization artifact makes weights and activations visible, but visibility is not legibility. You can see every neuron fire and still not know what the network knows.

Chun's work on programmed visions is precise here. Software produces habits of perception — ways of seeing that feel natural because the interface makes them automatic. The corridor architecture of this map is itself a programmed vision: you walk through layers in order, forward pass as spatial sequence, and the experience trains your body to think of neural computation as a pipeline. But modern architectures violate the clean layered metaphor — skip connections, residual layers, attention mechanisms, recursive loops. The corridor teaches a version of neural computation that was current in 1989 and is now a pedagogical convenience. The corridor is a skeuomorph of the perceptron era preserved in the architecture of a map that claims to teach the present.

The `learn_world_stacked` artifact sits in the deepest chamber, closest to the exit, and its identity is the most revealing. It stacks learned representations, making visible the abstraction hierarchy: raw input at the bottom, high-level features at the top. This is the dominant narrative of deep learning — depth creates abstraction, each layer sees more than the last, the network builds a ladder from pixels to meaning. What this narrative excludes is the possibility that the layers are not building toward anything. The universal approximation theorem says one hidden layer suffices in theory; depth helps in practice. But "helps in practice" is not "creates abstraction." The network may be memorizing shortcuts, exploiting spurious correlations, or building representations that happen to work for the training distribution but encode nothing generalizable. The stacked visualization looks like understanding. It might be coincidence frozen into weights.

The bottleneck passages force compression. You cannot carry your full spatial freedom from one chamber to the next — the corridor narrows, and you squeeze through. This is the most physically honest element: neural network layers really do compress, really do force information through narrow channels, really do discard what doesn't fit. But compression is lossy, and what is lost is not random. The network learns to discard whatever the loss function does not penalize for discarding. If the training data underrepresents certain populations, certain edge cases, certain ways of being in the world, the network learns to compress them out. The bottleneck is efficient. It is also an engine of erasure, and the things it erases are the things nobody thought to include in the loss.

Generalization lives in the narrow corridor between knowing and forgetting. Too much fitting and the network memorizes. Too little and it learns nothing. The map cannot show you this corridor because it is not spatial but statistical — it lives in the gap between training error and test error, between the data you showed the network and the data you did not. The thing that makes neural networks powerful and dangerous — their ability to generalize from the seen to the unseen, silently, without justification — is the thing no visualization can contain.
