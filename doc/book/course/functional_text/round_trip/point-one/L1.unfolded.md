# Point One: give the point a body

*Unfolded by a blind reader from the compressed lesson `L1.md` alone. Where the compressed text did not carry enough and I had to fill a gap, the passage starts with [guess].*

In the previous lesson (Point Zero) you met a scene, a node and a script, the words `extends`, `Node3D` and `func`, and the function `_ready()`. The one fact this lesson builds on is that `_ready()` runs once, at the moment the scene starts.

In this lesson you use that moment to place a point. A point by itself is not something you can see or bump into, so you then give it a body: first a body you can **see**, then a body you can **touch**. By the end you will know that "seen" and "touched" are two separate things in Godot, and that you are the one who decides whether they agree.

## 1.1 Three stations, three feelers

*(Picture: three labels reading "Point one", side by side. Above the first there is empty air; above the second and third there is a white sphere. A pink feeler sweeps past each one.)*

Open the Point One study (the folder `studies/point-one`, starting with its `README.md`) and then open the scene `study.tscn`.

The root node of that scene is called `PointStudy`. Underneath it stand three **stations**, named `Position`, `Visible` and `Solid`. A station is simply a node that has a script and a label. The three scripts are:

| station | script |
|---|---|
| `Position` | `position.gd` |
| `Visible` | `visible.gd` |
| `Solid` | `solid.gd` |

These three scripts are the only things you will change in this lesson. The floor, the light and the camera are supplied for you; leave them as they are.

[guess] The lesson does not say how a station's label is made (for example as a 3D text node); it is part of the station you are given, and you do not edit it.

Now run the study and watch:

- Above the first label there is nothing.
- Above the second and third labels there is a white sphere.
- Three pink **feelers** move from left to right, one past each station. A feeler is a small pink body whose job is to test contact.
- The first two feelers cross straight through their station.
- The third feeler stops when it reaches the last sphere.
- Then the sweep starts over and repeats.

You can control the sweep from the keyboard: **Space** pauses it and resumes it, **R** restarts it.

The feelers are moved by a supplied script, `contact_probe.gd`, which also tests whether a feeler has touched something. Like the camera, it is supplied, and you do not edit it. You never change the feeler itself. What you change, in the three station scripts, is *what a feeler is able to cross*.

## 1.2 A point has no body

*(Picture: the first station. A label, empty air above it, and a feeler passing through.)*

Here is the whole of `position.gd`:

```gdscript
extends Node3D

func _ready() -> void:
    var point_position := Vector3(0.0, 1.12, 0.0)
```

You already know `extends Node3D` and `func _ready()` from Point Zero. Here is what is new:

- `-> void` after the function's parentheses says that the function gives back no value when it finishes.
- `Vector3(0.0, 1.12, 0.0)` is a **Vector3**: one value made of three numbers.
- `var point_position :=` gives that value a name, `point_position`. A name for a value is called a **variable**. `var` starts the variable and `:=` gives it its value.

Two more words to hold on to:

- A **position** is where something is. In this script the position is the `Vector3`.
- A **point** is the place a position marks.

What do the three numbers mean? This lesson leaves that open on purpose. The next lesson, Point Coordinates, deals with them.

[guess] In the next section, changing the middle number from `1.12` to `1.7` makes a sphere rise, so the middle number seems to be the height. The lesson gives no unit; it may be metres.

### Try it

Change `1.12` to `1.7` and run the study again.

Nothing appears at the first station. The editor shows a warning: **unused variable**. You changed a value, but nothing in the script uses that value, so the change has no visible effect at all.

[guess] The warning is not caused by your edit. `point_position` is unused with `1.12` as well. The edit just makes it obvious that a value nothing reads does nothing.

The feeler still crosses the empty place where the point is. The feeler belongs to the test, not to your point, and your point has nothing it could bump into.

Change the number back to `1.12`.

**What to remember:** a position alone gives a point no body.

## 1.3 A mesh is seen

*(Picture: the second station. A white sphere above the label; the feeler passes right through it.)*

Here is `visible.gd`:

```gdscript
extends Node3D

func _ready() -> void:
    var point_position := Vector3(0.0, 1.12, 0.0)

    var radius := 0.14
    var sphere := SphereMesh.new()
    sphere.radius = radius
    sphere.height = radius * 2.0

    var marker := MeshInstance3D.new()
    marker.mesh = sphere
    marker.position = point_position
    add_child(marker)
```

The first line inside `_ready()` is the same position as before. Then the script builds two parts, and each part is made with `.new()`:

1. `sphere` is a **SphereMesh**, made with `SphereMesh.new()`. A **mesh** is what is seen: the visible form.
2. `marker` is a **MeshInstance3D**, made with `MeshInstance3D.new()`. It is a node whose job is to show a mesh.

[guess] `.new()` written after a class name such as `SphereMesh` creates a fresh object of that kind. A word after a dot, such as `sphere.radius`, reaches one setting (a property) of that object. A plain `=` changes a setting or a variable that already exists. `:=` is what creates a new variable.

The sphere's size is set from the variable `radius`, which is `0.14`:

- `sphere.radius = radius` sets the sphere's radius.
- `sphere.height = radius * 2.0` sets its height to twice the radius.

Then three lines give the marker everything it needs:

- `marker.mesh = sphere` gives the marker its mesh, so it has something to show.
- `marker.position = point_position` puts the marker at the point.
- `add_child(marker)` joins the marker to the station, so it becomes part of the scene.

[guess] `add_child(...)` with nothing before it attaches the new node to the node that runs this script, which here is the station.

`marker` is a **body**: a node you place. Notice that the position (`point_position`) and the body placed there (`marker`) are two different things. The position is a value. The body is a node that stands at that value.

### Try it

1. Change `1.12` to `1.7` and run. This time something happens: the sphere rises, because now the marker uses the position.
2. Change it back to `1.12`. Then set `radius` to `0.3` (the line `var radius := 0.14`) and run. The sphere grows, and its centre stays in the same place. Its height grows too, because the script sets the height to twice `radius`.

[guess] If the height were not tied to the radius, a bigger radius with the old height would give a squashed or stretched sphere. That is why the script derives one from the other.

Watch the feeler cross the larger sphere. It passes through, just as it did through the small one. A bigger mesh changes how much the sphere covers in the picture. It does not change what can pass through it.

[guess] The compressed lesson puts this as "You change what the sphere hides, not what passes". I read "hides" as "covers from view".

Set `radius` back to `0.14`.

**What to remember:** a mesh gives the feeler nothing to touch.

## 1.4 A shape is touched

*(Picture: the third station. The same white sphere, and the feeler stopped against it.)*

The third station looks exactly like the second, but its feeler stops. Here is `solid.gd`:

```gdscript
extends Node3D

func _ready() -> void:
    var point_position := Vector3(0.0, 1.12, 0.0)

    var radius := 0.14
    var sphere := SphereMesh.new()
    sphere.radius = radius
    sphere.height = radius * 2.0

    var marker := MeshInstance3D.new()
    marker.mesh = sphere
    marker.position = point_position
    add_child(marker)

    var solid := StaticBody3D.new()
    solid.position = point_position

    var sphere_shape := SphereShape3D.new()
    sphere_shape.radius = radius
    var collider := CollisionShape3D.new()
    collider.shape = sphere_shape

    solid.add_child(collider)
    add_child(solid)
```

The first part builds the same visible `marker` as `visible.gd`. The second part is new and adds three classes:

| variable | class | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| `solid` | `StaticBody3D` | a body that stops things |
| `sphere_shape` | `SphereShape3D` | a **shape**: what is touched |
| `collider` | `CollisionShape3D` | carries the shape |

They fit together like this:

- `collider.shape = sphere_shape` hands the shape to the collider.
- `solid.add_child(collider)` attaches the collider to the body.
- `add_child(solid)` attaches the body to the station.

So the station ends up holding two children side by side:

```
Solid (station, runs solid.gd)
├── marker   (MeshInstance3D, shows the sphere mesh: seen)
└── solid    (StaticBody3D, stops things)
    └── collider (CollisionShape3D, carries sphere_shape: touched)
```

Two facts make the seen sphere and the touched sphere line up exactly:

- `marker` and `solid` both stand at `point_position`, so they share a centre.
- `sphere` and `sphere_shape` both use `radius`, so they share a size.

The feeler stops at the shape. It does not stop at the mesh.

[guess] "Static" probably means that this body does not move by itself. The lesson only says it stops things.

**What to remember:** a mesh is what is seen, and a shape is what is touched.

## 1.5 Seen and touched, set apart

*(Two pictures: the feeler stopped short of a small sphere with a gap between them, and the feeler sunk into a large sphere.)*

In `solid.gd` the mesh and the shape agree only because the script gives them the same radius. Now you pull them apart.

### Try it: a shape larger than the mesh

Find the line `sphere_shape.radius = radius` in `solid.gd` and change only that one line:

```gdscript
    sphere_shape.radius = 0.35
```

Run again. The white sphere stays small, because the mesh still uses `radius`, which is `0.14`. But the feeler stops earlier, before it reaches the sphere. A gap opens up: the feeler is stopped by what looks like empty space.

If you want to see the invisible shape, turn on **Debug → Visible Collision Shapes** in the editor's menu. It draws the larger shape. You do not need it, though. The feeler stopping in mid-air already shows you that the shape is bigger.

Put the line back to `sphere_shape.radius = radius`.

### Try it: a shape smaller than the mesh

Now change two lines: set `radius` to `0.3` (so `var radius := 0.3`) and set `sphere_shape.radius` to `0.07`. Run.

The white sphere is now big, but the feeler sinks into it. Only once the feeler reaches the small shape hidden inside the mesh does the station's label report "stopped".

[guess] I take this to mean that the station's label shows the feeler's state, and that it changes to "stopped" when contact happens. The compressed lesson mentions this only here.

Afterwards, return both to `0.14`.

[guess] Returning `var radius := 0.14` and writing the shape line back as `sphere_shape.radius = radius` (rather than typing `0.14`) puts the script back exactly as supplied, with the two sizes tied together again.

### Why this matters

In the museum (the game this study belongs to) there is no feeler. There, it is your own movement, you walking through the space, that runs into the difference between what you see and what you touch. The study has no player walking around, so its feeler moves in your place and lets you watch the contact happen.

Seen and touched are set apart. You choose whether they agree.

## 1.6 Light off, body kept

*(Picture: the museum after the wall switch is used. The long shadows are gone; the spheres are dim but still visible.)*

In the museum there is a switch on the wall. When you use it:

- the long shadows disappear,
- a dim fill light keeps the spheres visible,
- the shapes stay exactly where they were.

The switch makes its decision in one line of code:

```gdscript
sun.visible = false
```

`sun` is the museum's directional light. Setting its `visible` to `false` turns it off.

[guess] A directional light shines from one direction across the whole scene, like sunlight, and that is why it casts the long shadows. A "fill" light is a weaker light that keeps things visible without strong shadows.

**Read this line, but do not paste it into your scripts.** `visible.gd` has no variable named `sun`, so the line would not work there. The study keeps its own supplied light, which you do not touch, and building the switch is outside this lesson.

What the line shows: turning off a light changes what is seen. It does not change the bodies or their shapes. What is touched stays.

## Why the lesson runs in this order

The three stations go from nothing, to something seen, to something touched:

1. **Position only:** a value, no body. Nothing is seen and nothing stops the feeler.
2. **Mesh:** a body that is seen. The feeler still passes through.
3. **Shape:** a body that is touched. The feeler stops.

Each step adds exactly one part, so you can tell which part does what. The order teaches two distinctions: a position is not a body, and what is seen (the mesh) is not what is touched (the shape). Section 1.5 then proves the second distinction by pulling the two apart.
