# The decision and the field note

Every lesson asks you to predict, then to look. This page is about what you do after you have looked.

It comes from the research method Palle Torsson wrote down for this project on 1 October 2026: make a configuration, look at it, change one thing, look again, and then decide whether to follow what you saw or to settle. The lessons had the prediction and the comparison. This adds the decision, and the record that keeps it.

## Decide: follow or settle

After the comparison you are at a fork.

- **Follow.** Something did not match. Your prediction was wrong, or two ways of looking disagreed: the picture and the marble, the editor and the exported build, one camera and another. Change one more thing and look again.
- **Settle.** The result is what you need. Keep it and build your body.

Either is a good decision. A difference is worth following when it gives you a question about the material, when it contradicts what you expected, when it changes what a body can do, or when it shows that your description was missing something. You do not have to follow every difference, and you do not have to find the best configuration. Stopping is part of the work.

Write the decision down, with the reason. That is the step most people skip, and it is the one that lets someone else, or you in a month, see why the body is the way it is.

## Keep three things apart

- **What you saw.** The marble came to rest at 1.71 m. The top was missing from above.
- **What you conclude from it.** The top is wound the other way.
- **What you are guessing.** Perhaps a drafter that is told less winds everything inward.

A note that mixes them cannot be checked. Write them as separate lines.

Two cautions from the same method. Do not say a system became more disordered, or that entropy rose, unless you can say which distribution you counted and how. And when one cause explains what you saw, such as a collision margin or a camera that clips, say so: an ordinary explanation does not make the observation less worth having.

## The note

Copy this into your sketchbook for each lesson's argument. Fill the first four lines before you run anything.

```
Question:        what am I asking of this body?
Baseline:        the scene, the kit, the values it starts from
Intervention:    the one thing I change, from what to what
Prediction:      written before the trial, or "no prediction"
Observation:     what I saw, and what a body (the marble, the walker) did
Another cause:   what else could explain it
Decision:        follow / settle / branch / leave open / stop
Reason:          why
Run it again:    the steps or the command that reproduce it
```

"Leave open" is allowed. A note may end with something you could not explain.
