# A pattern that returns

<!-- @seed_replay_demo -->

435. It fills the dark display above the colour bench. The number arrived from RANDOM.ORG, whose generator draws on atmospheric noise. It has been saved here; another request can bring another number.[^atmosphere] We asked for an integer between zero and 999. Before it arrived, we had already decided what it could be.

Once kept, that number can serve as a seed. It sets the starting state of a pseudorandom generator: a procedure that produces its sequence by a deterministic rule. The same generator, starting state and sequence of requests give the same values. A picture needs one more agreement. Here, three successive draws supply the red, green and blue amounts of a cube.

Below the number stand two grids, four cubes across and four high. Their colours agree, cell for cell. REPLAY recovers the arrangement, including a blue beside a violet that nobody selected as a pair. Cage’s score could be heard again. Here an arrival from the atmosphere has become something we can return to without asking the atmosphere again.

The button marked +1 DRAW breaks that agreement. It discards one value before colouring the right grid, shifting every group of three. The seed stays; the right-hand picture changes. Nothing in a draw says that it is red. The patches acquire different neighbours. You can leave them together without first deciding that they belong.

Nearby, a dark sphere carries a person away from its centre and back. Its own generator chooses a direction and a distance for each excursion. Holding it joins the journey; releasing it leaves the sphere to finish alone. There is a pause before it leaves again. Whatever happens to its passenger, it still has an appointment at the centre.

Keeping chance available for another calculation once meant preparing an enormous book. RAND’s *A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates*, published in 1955, supplied researchers with numbers for Monte Carlo calculations: sampling possible cases rather than working through every one. The digits began in an electronic roulette device in 1947. Tests found biases. Engineers refined the machine, and the table was transformed before publication.[^rand-1955]

Prepared for punched-card machines, this chance passed through considerable work. A printed table keeps its numbers where they can be found again; our generator calculates its sequence afresh. Either way, we need to know where to begin and how to take the next number. But the page standing in this museum has made another arrangement with time.

Its digits fall downward and disappear. A touch holds one cell while the others continue. Following a number to the bottom ends in its disappearance. The page resembles a book until someone wants to reopen a line that has fallen away.

Back at the grids, the cubes have kept their places through every change of colour. Here, randomness varies what the program has made variable. Another seed cannot persuade a cube to leave its cell.

Imagine taking a coloured square between your fingers and drawing it towards your wrist. Suppose we let its neighbours follow, still joined, bending as you pull. A corner folds around your arm; the rest trails across the floor. We have not built this behaviour. Some draws might choose which squares come loose. Keeping them connected would require another rule.

Now there is a sleeve to contend with. Where does it catch? Does your wrist drag it, or can its weight pull back? The colour that made you stop has become something you want to put your hand inside. Replaying the picture will not get it there.

The next hall gathers coloured symbols into counts. Its gauge can agree about two arrangements whose neighbours are entirely different. Remember the patch you wanted to keep when you meet these new patterns. What will counting keep of the relation that made you stay?

[^rand-1955]: RAND’s [introduction and production account](https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1418.html) documents the demand for sampling tables, the electronic roulette output, tests for bias, transformations and punched-card preparation. The museum page recalls the book; its falling digits are an authored behaviour of this installation.

[^atmosphere]: RANDOM.ORG describes its atmospheric source and bounded integer requests in its [HTTP interface documentation](https://www.random.org/clients/http/api/). The displayed 435 is a saved response received on 13 October 2026. The museum uses it to seed Godot’s generator; it does not fetch each cube’s colour from the service.
