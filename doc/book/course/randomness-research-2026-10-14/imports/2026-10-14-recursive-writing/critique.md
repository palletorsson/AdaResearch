In your earlier message, you wrote:
The author is not dead, the author is dissipated.
And you described writing as a collaboration across time, involving knowledge the author may not yet possess, an apparatus that changes during its own production, and a learning debt that allows the author to return to the work and discover something.
That is considerably more radical than AI-assisted writing.
I checked[ SOURCE.md](https://github.com/palletorsson/AdaResearch/blob/palm-scanner-door-entry/doc/book/course/randomness-research-2026-10-14/SOURCE.md) and[ FINAL.md](https://github.com/palletorsson/AdaResearch/blob/palm-scanner-door-entry/doc/book/course/randomness-research-2026-10-14/FINAL.md). Neither contains the concepts dissipated authorship, learning debt, or unauthored material explicitly. SOURCE preserves the earlier randomness research map, but not this additional philosophical proposition.
Instead, FINAL says:
AI changes the time available for this work.
That is true, but it is a much weaker claim.
Your proposition isn't merely that AI accelerates programming and allows us to spend more time observing. It is that the temporal structure of authorship itself changes.
The traditional model often assumes that writing is something the author understands sufficiently when it is published. Your proposed apparatus allows writing to precede some of the author's understanding.
A text may incorporate knowledge from another agent, another historical moment, or another implementation. The author can return to it, investigate it, disagree with it, modify its conditions, and learn from what was already written.
This creates an unusual relationship between authorship and reading.
The artist can become a researcher of a book they helped produce.
And that possibility has not yet been given a concrete place in Astra's specification.
The current specification has a strong experimental cycle:
WORLD EXPERIMENT
Build → Encounter → Observe → Record → Replay → Reconstruct
I would put another cycle beside it.
WRITING EXPERIMENT
Generate → Read → Question → Test → Revise → Rediscover
And connect them in both directions.
For example, Astra discovered that curl_noise_particles contains claims its current code does not fully justify. The program uses uncontrolled random respawns, while the text describes a more coherent and reproducible flow than has been established.
That discovery could remain a technical audit item.
But it could also become literature.
The text had promised one kind of world. Reading the code revealed another. The artist now has a choice: rewrite the text, change the program, or retain the disagreement as an experimental question.
A future edition could preserve both descriptions and explain what happened between them.
This is precisely the recursive relationship we identified earlier, now applied to authorship itself.
The book doesn't just describe the apparatus. Its own changing account becomes part of the apparatus being investigated.
I would add a small learning-debt ledger, recording a claim or passage that has not yet been fully understood, what generated it, what evidence is missing, and what future experiment could resolve or transform it.
That doesn't mean publishing unverified technical assertions as facts. It means making unresolved knowledge visible without treating uncertainty as an editorial failure.

Astra's own method gives you the means to evaluate this. Build or simulate both short routes, record encounters, compare the questions they produce, and let those observations affect the sequence.
There need not be one universally superior route. They may address different forms of attention.

But I think the decisive artistic opportunity remains ahead.
Imagine that you return to Ada in six months. You encounter a passage that you do not remember writing, explaining an unexpected behaviour in a particle system. You investigate the code, discover that the behaviour changed during development, and find an observation made by an earlier AI collaborator. You reconstruct the experiment and discover something the passage did not anticipate.
Now the book has served several roles simultaneously. It has been an archive, a collaborator, a source of questions, and an object that needed to be corrected.
The author remains responsible for its development, but is not the sole origin of everything it contains or everything that can be learned from it.
This is what I understand by dissipated authorship: not the disappearance of the author, but the distribution of intellectual activity across agents, materials and durations that no individual author can fully contain.
It also offers a powerful response to the accelerating complexity of technological systems. The artistic method need not pretend to have achieved complete understanding before beginning. Instead, it can construct situations in which understanding is produced, interrupted and reconstructed over time.
My assessment: Astra has produced a serious technical and curatorial research specification. The remaining challenge is to make the book itself an equally experimental system.
The central test should eventually be not only “Can a student reconstruct this apparatus from the book?” but also:
“Can the apparatus produce something that changes what the book knows—and can the book produce something that changes what the apparatus is capable of becoming?”
That's the point at which your method would become more than a sophisticated curriculum. It would become a recursive artistic research practice.
