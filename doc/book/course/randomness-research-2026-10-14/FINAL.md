# What could happen here?

*Sequence-level final-text draft, 14 October 2026. Developed from the supplied research map and the later texts on a recursive writing apparatus. The preceding draft is retained in history/2026-10-14-before-recursive-writing/FINAL.md. Existing encounters are described as existing; unbuilt encounters remain conditional. This is editorial material for the proposed route, not a replacement of the current hall texts.*

Two small grids keep the same arrangement of colours. Four cubes across, four high. The seed is the same, and the procedure takes its numbers in the same order. A blue has arrived beside a violet without anyone choosing that pair. You might stop there for a while. The program has produced an arrangement; it cannot tell us why these neighbours made you stay.

Pressing REPLAY brings the colours back. It does not bring back your first encounter with them. You now know something you did not know before. The picture has returned under different conditions of attention.

The button marked +1 DRAW discards a number before making the second picture. The seed stays the same. Every later group of three values receives another assignment, and the neighbours change. A number was not red before the program used it as red. What looks like an arrival is also a meeting between a source and a decision about what that source may affect.

For now, the cubes remain in their cells. Thousands of other seeds could produce thousands of other pictures without giving a single cube permission to leave.

Imagine drawing a corner towards a wrist, letting its neighbours follow while they remain connected. The square arrangement begins to suggest a sleeve. We have not made it. The sentence asks for something the present instrument cannot do, and leaves much undecided: what connects, what bends, what can separate, what counts as wearing it.

The writing has proposed another apparatus. One interpretation might make the connections visible; another might make them bear a force. Neither would exhaust what the sentence means. The difference between them would give us something to investigate.

That permission is where this investigation begins to change.

Randomness reaches the room through several kinds of preparation. A generator calculates a sequence from its state. A previously acquired number can be kept and used to begin that calculation again. A new acquisition from a secure source has another relationship to prediction. A measurement of the headset's timing tells us something about that particular measurement and its conditions; its irregularity alone does not establish a trustworthy entropy source. We need to keep these accounts distinct even when their pictures look alike.

The distinction also changes what we mean by returning. We can preserve an arrival without being able to make its source repeat. We can replay the recorded consequence of a physical throw without making the hand, the contacts and the body take the same path. We can save the values that coloured a grid while forgetting the order in which they were used. A retained result needs an account of what was retained with it.

As we move through the halls, chance acquires different jobs. A draw can select a member of a set, but another rule has already decided who belongs to that set. It can choose a height, with one law favouring a narrow neighbourhood and another spreading values more evenly. It can decide how long a body remains available to stand on. These choices make different experiences possible. Calling all of them random is only the beginning of an explanation.

A distribution is one way of organizing expectation. Most of the values may gather near a centre, while a few travel far from it. We can change the bins and alter the shape we see without changing a single stored value. We can also change the law that makes the next sample. Both operations change the display, but they ask different things of the world. The first rearranges our account. The second changes what is likely to arrive.

Then the values begin to share something. Nearby columns rise together. A field can be sampled at the same address again; its value need not be a fresh independent draw. Variation can have a scale, and the scale can become a fold, a ridge or a passage. Smoothness is a relationship we have made available, not the disappearance of randomness into order.

The receiving body matters just as much. A value can colour a surface while leaving its shape intact. The same value can displace a point. A threshold can admit a cell into a volume or leave it empty. Rendering can make a boundary visible while collision makes a different boundary available to the person walking there. The source has not decided on its own whether its variation will become an image, an obstacle or a place to enter.

There is a reason to stay with these differences before increasing the scale. An entire room can conceal a small decision more effectively than a tabletop can. Entering it makes that decision bodily consequential; it does not automatically make its mechanism clear. We may need to leave the room, keep a sample, and make a smaller version to understand what happened inside it.

A particle gives us another way to follow the consequence. At first it has a position and a velocity. Later it belongs to a population. An emitter determines its birth; a lifetime determines when it is retired. A curve can make it fade without changing where it goes. A field can turn its path. A boundary can let it leave, return it elsewhere, absorb it or make it rebound.

The population may seem to persist even when none of its first members remains. We can watch that continuity, record the replacements, and ask what we have been calling the same thing. We can also change a boundary while keeping the births fixed. The resulting difference might tell us more about the population than adding another thousand particles would.

The path becomes more difficult to account for when it begins to alter its surroundings. A walker leaves a trace. If the next decision reads that trace, the record has entered the procedure. A place already visited may recruit another visit. A forgotten place may become less likely to be found. We can disconnect that return path and compare what changes. Memory here is an operation with consequences, not simply an image of the past.

We will need measures, but every measure keeps some things and leaves others behind. Two arrangements can contain the same numbers of coloured cells and have very different neighbours. A histogram retains frequencies. It does not retain every relation that made an arrangement worth looking at. Shannon entropy concerns a declared variable and distribution; it is not a general reading of a work's complexity, surprise or value.

This matters to the ambition of the museum. A highly variable picture can be entirely familiar as a kind of picture. A small change to a familiar rule can disturb an expectation we had not noticed. The number of alternatives available to a system does not settle the question of which differences matter to someone encountering it.

Nor does unpredictability always require a fresh random input. We can investigate a deterministic rule from two nearby starting states and observe their separation. We can ask how the numerical method participates in the result. We can keep a field unchanged while moving the threshold that turns its values into occupied space. These experiments deserve their own accounts. Sensitivity, threshold, noise and numerical error cannot be made interchangeable by the fact that each can surprise us.

The later encounters would let us examine persistence more closely. A population can vary while an evaluator keeps selecting for the same property. Changing that evaluator might reorganize the population's future without changing the candidates in front of us. Keeping a candidate the evaluator rejects would place another judgment beside its score. We could then ask what the score failed to notice, and whether that omission should change the next implementation.

This is one place where QFEP gives Ada a research question. How much organization needs to be retained for transformation to remain possible? When might preserving a form protect a capacity, and when might it prevent a new relation from appearing? We approach these as questions for a proposed theoretical and artistic framework. They are not answered by calling a slider entropy or by finding a setting that produces an appealing animation. Variational free energy, physical entropy and the entropy of a sampled variable require different definitions. An inhabited boundary also requires further work before it can stand for a statistical Markov blanket.

The grid returns here. Suppose a cell could come loose. Suppose two cells could connect, and the relation could persist after they moved. The random values might remain exactly as they were while the program gained another kind of possibility. We could preserve the old rule and compare it with the new one. A drawn line would first establish a visible connection; making that connection bear a force would be a further decision, with further consequences.

The sleeve imagined at the opening is still a question. What would have to hold together for a square to follow a wrist? Where would it catch? What would its weight do? We do not yet know whether that reconstruction would produce the encounter we want. The point of building it would be to find out, and to let what happens alter the next version.

A sentence can arrive before we know how to make it operational. A collaborator may turn it into a small model, and the model may reveal decisions the sentence left open. Perhaps a connected grid would collapse. Perhaps it would become a net that refuses the wrist. These are imagined outcomes, not observations. An encounter would give us a reason to revise the passage, the construction, or our interest in pursuing either.

The book would then do more than receive the result of an experiment. It would help initiate the next one. A paragraph could have several corresponding worlds; a world could have several incompatible accounts. We could keep the failed construction beside the sentence it interrupted. We would not need to smooth those differences into a single history of progress.

AI changes the time between these acts, and the moments at which understanding becomes available. An implementation can arrive before its maker understands the method used to construct it. A passage can introduce a relation its nominal author has not yet investigated. The person who proposed the sentence, the agent that developed a model and the visitor who encounters it know different things. The author returning later occupies another position again.

Writing has always depended on given time: the work of other observers, the languages and instruments they left, the conditions under which their words could reach us. Here those contributions can enter a book while the world it concerns is still changing. The author is not dead. The author is dissipated. Responsibility remains, while the origins of the work and the occasions for understanding it extend beyond a single act of composition.

This produces learning debt: a distance between what the apparatus has made available and what we presently understand. A working solver may still be unfamiliar. A convincing explanation may lack evidence. A strange relation may be worth returning to without knowing yet how to test it. These debts ask for different kinds of attention. They should not all be marked settled because a program runs.

I might return to a passage I did not personally compose and find something worth reconstructing. Such unauthored material, from the returning author's position, still has contributors and conditions of arrival. Where its origin is uncertain, that uncertainty belongs in the record. It gives no permission to present an unsupported claim as fact. But a passage need not remain within what I understood when it first entered the book in order to become useful later.

Reading becomes another experiment. We can ask which apparatus a sentence describes, what version supported its claim, and what a later implementation has changed. In the curl-particle study, source inspection already gives us a precise question about replay: some respawn draws and frame-dependent updates fall outside the simple seeded account. That is not a newly observed headset result. It is a reason to test the claim, revise its wording, or construct a different procedure. The discrepancy can enter the writing without pretending it has been resolved.

We therefore want a tutorial that can lead beyond the result it already knows how to demonstrate. It should make an experiment reconstructable: what was brought in, what was supplied, what changed and what was observed. Someone might use AI while reconstructing it. That assistance belongs in the account. The important next question is what they can now change, inspect and explain, including what the apparatus cannot yet express.

A visitor's observation may disagree with our reason for making the work. They may keep something we expected them to discard. They may find no useful difference. They may discover that two controls we treated as independent alter the same hidden condition. These are possible research outcomes. The apparatus needs enough stability for us to investigate them, and enough openness for their consequences to reach its design.

Those consequences should reach the plan as well. If the kept picture opens a question about bodily memory, the next useful task may no longer concern the source of its random numbers. We should be able to preserve the old proposal, explain the departure and build a different next question. Otherwise exploration would only decorate a route whose destination had already been fixed.

The final record would therefore keep more than a successful configuration. It would keep a baseline, an intervention, a comparison, the conditions of replay, and a question that remains. It would distinguish a new arrival from a recovered one. It would give the next person a way to reconstruct the experiment and leave them free to change what the reconstruction is for.

At the seed bench again, the blue can still return beside the violet. This time we might ask what else those values could be allowed to do. We might also return to the sentence that first asked them to leave their cells. What has the room made that sentence unable to say? What new relation could its revision ask the room to attempt? The book is one of the instruments through which both questions can continue to change.
