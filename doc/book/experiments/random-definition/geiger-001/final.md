# What does randomness sound like?

A click is brief. A silence can feel long enough to become a question. When several clicks arrive together, it is tempting to hear intention: a rhythm, an acceleration, a signal meant for us. The counter lets that expectation become part of the experiment.

Here the event times are simulated. Keeping the seed and recipe lets the same irregular sequence return. The ability to repeat it is useful for listening carefully, but it is also information about its origin. It does not turn a software model into a physical source of radioactive randomness.

The archived HotBits apparatus used a Geiger–Müller detector to obtain events from radioactive decay. Its radioactive service was retired at the end of 2022. Our exhibit draws on that history without pretending to be a live connection to it. [Fourmilab's retirement notice](https://www.fourmilab.ch/hotbits/)

The clicks are electronic representations of detections, not sounds emitted by individual atoms. The clock and comparison rule make another representation: bits. Make the clock coarser and some distinct intervals become indistinguishable, even while the sound remains exactly the same. A source has not changed, but what the apparatus can say about it has.

The question can move in either direction. We can begin with the tutorial and ask whether its rule reconstructs the recorded trace. We can begin with listening and ask why a cluster sounded suspicious or a quiet stretch felt like failure. Neither reaction has been established as a visitor finding yet. Preserve what happens, then decide whether to change the explanation, the sound, the clock, or the question itself.
