# ML_Sequence_Memory — Critical Reflection

A long narrow corridor. Time flows from spawn to exit. Two artifacts at regular intervals like timesteps: LSTMs first, transformers second. The width varies — walls close in then open, creating rhythm. You walk forward and cannot go back. The corridor is time made spatial, and the map's thesis is that sequence processing is a one-way passage through a narrowing architecture.

The LSTM artifact sits early in the corridor. Its architecture is gates — forget, input, output — three learned filters that decide what to keep, what to discard, what to pass on. The cell state runs like a conveyor belt through time, modified but never fully overwritten. Hayles recognizes this as the computational unconscious operating in its temporal mode: the gates decide what the network remembers without any external agent specifying the criteria. The forget gate is the most revealing. It learns, through gradient descent on task performance, which parts of the past are irrelevant to the present. Forgetting is not loss; it is curation. And the curation criteria are determined by the loss function, which means the network forgets whatever failing to remember would not increase the error. Memory is shaped by what the system was trained to care about. Everything else leaks through the forget gate like water through a sieve shaped by someone else's priorities.

The transformer artifact sits deeper in the corridor. Attention lets every position in a sequence look at every other position simultaneously. No conveyor belt. No sequential processing. Time collapses into weighted relationships. Chun would identify this as the shift from habitual memory to sovereign vision — from the LSTM's patient accumulation to the transformer's panoptic glance. The transformer does not remember by carrying state forward; it remembers by looking backward from every position at once. This is not memory in any human sense. It is simultaneous access, the computational form of omniscience applied to a finite sequence.

The corridor's narrowing architecture contradicts the transformer's logic. You walk through a tight passage that enforces sequence — this step, then this step, then this step — while the artifact inside demonstrates an architecture that abolishes sequence. The transformer attends to position 1 and position 100 with equal ease. The corridor does not. You cannot jump from the LSTM chamber to the exit; you must pass through every intermediate cell. The map's body-logic is recurrent even when its content is attentional. This is not a flaw. It is the map's deepest admission: that embodied experience is sequential even when computation is not, and that teaching temporal architectures through spatial traversal always imposes a linearity the most powerful models have learned to escape.

Hayles' concept of intermediation — the recursive feedback between human cognition and computational processes — finds precise expression in the corridor's rhythm. The walls close in then open, creating a breathing pattern that mirrors the LSTM's gating mechanism. Tight passage: forget gate closed, information compressed. Wide chamber: input gate open, new state absorbed. The architecture teaches through somatic rhythm what the equations describe through linear algebra. The intermediation goes further. The learner walking the corridor is performing a sequence task — moving through a temporal environment with memory of previous rooms and anticipation of the next — using exactly the kind of recurrent, state-carrying cognition that the LSTM models computationally. You are the hidden state. Your memory of the previous chamber is the cell state. Your decision about what to attend to in the current room is the attention mechanism.

Chun's analysis of software temporality applies to the gap between the two artifacts. The LSTM processes time honestly — it carries state forward, and the vanishing gradient problem means distant memories genuinely fade. The transformer processes time without temporal experience — it flattens the entire sequence into a single attention matrix, abolishing temporal distance. There is no vanishing gradient in a transformer because there is no temporal chain. Every position is equally close to every other position, weighted only by learned relevance. This is what Chun calls the enduring ephemeral: a structure that preserves everything not because it remembers but because it never experienced sequence as passage, only as static structure to be queried.

The corridor ends. Two architectures for memory — one that carries and one that looks — and the space between them is the distance between two ways of being in time. The LSTM lives in time and pays the cost: vanishing gradients, decaying memory, the struggle to connect the distant past to the present. The transformer escapes time and pays a different cost: quadratic attention complexity, the assumption that the whole sequence is available before processing begins. One architecture remembers by suffering through sequence. The other remembers by surveilling it. Neither remembers the way a body remembers — through rhythm, through habit, through the corridor that your feet have learned.
