# Q: An Entropic Morality

*QFEP development essay · 14 September 2026. Developed from the user's [Swedish and English draft](source-entropic-morality.txt). This is a philosophical proposal within AdaResearch; its ethical commitments are stated explicitly rather than presented as consequences proved by physics. [Project connections and development notes](README.md).*

A finite model makes some differences available for action and leaves others unresolved. A map selects features; a classifier selects distinctions; a controller responds to some changes and remains insensitive to others. These reductions can make action possible. The problem begins when the reach of the representation is mistaken for the reach of what exists, and whatever cannot be represented becomes available for dismissal or destruction.

For QFEP, the question is how to make necessary reductions while remaining answerable for their consequences.

This does not require a self-contained subject looking out at an external world. Modelling happens within material relations: bodies, instruments, environments, institutions, and histories participate in what can be sensed and acted upon. A boundary distinguishes activities within those relations. It can have real effects without constituting an absolute division between independent substances. The modeller is involved in what is being modelled, and can be changed by the encounter. What exceeds a model is not a mysterious territory outside reality; it is the relations and differences that this particular arrangement does not register.

Boundaries can also sustain the ability to participate. A membrane, a protected habitat, a private conversation, or a space of refuge can make relations possible by limiting other relations. Openness therefore needs a political question: who can cross, who can refuse, and who bears the consequences? Requiring every boundary to dissolve would make exposure compulsory, including for those whose survival depends on protection.

The first distinction to preserve is between simplifying a representation and simplifying the conditions of life to fit it. Leaving a species out of a diagram does not itself remove that species from an ecosystem. But a management system that allocates protection only to listed species can turn an omission into a material loss. Q attends to this passage from description to intervention. It asks when a useful abstraction begins to enforce its own incompleteness on others.

**Q names the practice of keeping the terms of a model revisable, and protecting the capacities through which what it leaves out can continue to live, act, and change those terms.** This develops the Queer-Q and Self-Q already present in AdaResearch. It includes awareness of limitation, but requires more than an intelligent agent reporting uncertainty. There must be material opportunities for correction, refusal, encounter, and repair.

Ashby's law of requisite variety gives this concern a precise starting point. Regulation depends on a repertoire of distinguishable responses adequate to the disturbances relevant to the outcome being maintained. The requirement concerns a specified regulatory task, not exhaustive knowledge of the universe. More available responses help only when the regulator can distinguish when and how to use them; constraints and environmental buffering also affect the task. Ashby's result establishes a limit on regulation. It does not tell us which outcomes deserve protection, or whose survival should count. [Ashby, *An Introduction to Cybernetics*, chapter 11](https://ashby.info/Ashby-Introduction-to-Cybernetics.pdf).

That limit permits opposed political projects. One project expands the ability to respond: additional perspectives, accessible channels, shared resources, and room to revise the objective. Another makes its surroundings easier to control by suppressing differences that trouble the controller. Both may make regulation easier under some conditions. Cybernetics alone cannot select between them. QFEP therefore needs an explicit ethical commitment: others' continued lives and capacities to act matter beyond their usefulness to the system doing the regulating. The ability to make a world predictable does not confer authority to determine everyone else's future.

The phrase *entropic morality* names an inquiry into the distribution of possibility and loss. It does not make entropy a measure of goodness. Shannon entropy measures uncertainty in a probability distribution over specified alternatives; its value depends on the distinctions the measurement uses. It does not measure the worth of a life or the freedom of a community. Two sequences with the same symbol counts can have the same single-symbol entropy while arranging those symbols very differently. Thermodynamic entropy, information entropy, and the political possibility of living otherwise cannot be exchanged without an account of how they are related. [Shannon, *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf).

Accordingly, noise is not inherently liberating, and order need not be oppressive. Reliable shelter, continuity of care, and enforceable protection can enlarge what someone is able to do. Unpredictable violence can destroy that capacity. A queer reading of QFEP asks which arrangements make different lives possible, and which require a preferred kind of life to become compulsory. It must also leave room for people who want continuity, quiet, or a stable identity. Becoming cannot become another demand to which everyone must conform.

Mutual aid develops the affirmative side of this argument. Dean Spade describes a political practice in which meeting survival needs belongs to collective organizing and the transformation of social relations. This supplies a different question from how a central controller can extract more information from its surroundings: how can people organize the conditions of survival together? [Spade, *Solidarity Not Charity: Mutual Aid for Mobilization and Survival*](https://www.deanspade.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Mutual-Aid-Article-Social-Text-Final.pdf).

Within QFEP, mutual aid can sustain distributed capacities that no participant possesses alone. But difference is not automatically knowledge, coordination is not automatically just, and a group is not necessarily more capable merely because it is larger. Relations need time, resources, ways of resolving conflict, and opportunities to decline participation. Sharing capacities without sharing authority can reproduce domination. A project that preserves others only because they might become useful has retained the controller's standpoint. Their worth cannot depend on demonstrating a contribution to its model.

The desired preservation is therefore of lives, relations, and possibilities, not ignorance for its own sake. Learning more about an ecosystem can reduce uncertainty while improving the conditions for its inhabitants. A community can make a harmful process legible without making every member transparent to an institution. Q keeps these questions distinct: what should we learn, what should remain private, and what must remain able to exist regardless of whether we understand it? There is no duty to preserve avoidable confusion, and no entitlement to eliminate another's opacity.

Intelligence matters here through the capacities and powers it makes available. Better anticipation may reveal dependencies and harms that a narrower model missed. The same ability may enable more effective extraction or control. Recognizing a limitation and caring about those affected by it are different achievements. Neither follows automatically from faster computation, better prediction, or a higher score on an intelligence test. Even the ability to criticize one's own model can coexist with refusing others any authority to challenge it.

The Marxian comparison in the supplied draft helps frame this distinction: enlarged productive capacities may make greater freedom materially possible, while the organization of production determines who can exercise that possibility. QFEP can make a parallel proposal about intelligence. More capable modelling expands opportunities for care and for domination. Its ethical significance depends on how resources, decisions, and consequences are organized. A system cannot certify its own benevolence by displaying its sophistication.

For this reason, greater regulatory power brings greater obligations to those exposed to it. The obligation should fall especially on whoever can impose large or lasting changes, rather than demanding ever more explanation from those already carrying the costs. A powerful institution's admission that it cannot predict every consequence should motivate smaller interventions, shared decisions, and provision for repair. Uncertainty must not become its excuse for unaccountable experimentation.

Ecology gives this argument material stakes. Research on response diversity concerns differences in how species contributing to a common ecosystem function respond to environmental change. Such differences can support resilience when conditions change, but species richness alone does not guarantee resilience. The relevant lesson is that present apparent redundancy can conceal differences that matter under other conditions. [Elmqvist and colleagues, *Response diversity, ecosystem change, and resilience*](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1890/1540-9295%282003%29001%5B0488%3ARDECAR%5D2.0.CO%3B2).

QFEP's ethical extension is to resist treating an incomplete account of those relations as permission for irreversible loss. We need not prove that every possible intelligence must fail to model the biosphere, or that its value is a mathematically infinite quantity. The more grounded claim is that an intervention can destroy relationships and histories before its model has established what depends on them. Being unable to calculate a value does not make that value zero. Nor does calculating some benefits settle the standing of those who will lose their conditions of life.

The resulting principle can be stated as follows:

> Preserve the conditions through which different lives can continue and futures can remain revisable. An incomplete model is not sufficient justification for irreversible destruction of what it leaves out.

This is a presumption that requires judgment, rather than an instruction to preserve every existing arrangement. Some arrangements actively destroy others' possibilities. Ending coercion, responding to an immediate threat, or restoring damaged relations can require decisive change. Inaction can also impose irreversible losses. The task is to compare the consequences of acting and not acting, include those affected, and favour repairable interventions where they can meet the need. What counts as sufficient retained variety cannot be settled once for every system and every future.

This changes how the QFEP formula should be read. The existing expression, `QFE = F − λE(S) + φΔE(S,t)`, provides a vocabulary for discussing prediction, variation, weighting, and change. The ethical reading asks who chose those terms, what each measurement omits, and what happens when a low score is achieved at another's expense. Q does not need to become an extra scalar term purporting to measure everything the others missed. Any such measure would itself make another selection. The practice of revising a model and its authority must remain possible beyond adjustments to its parameters.

This also makes room for a more accurate relationship to Friston's Free Energy Principle. Free-energy minimization is not simply a command to eliminate stimulation or remain in a dark room. The account of surprise depends on the organism's model and expectations, which can make staying in a dark room surprising. QFEP's proposed ethical contribution therefore concerns the authority and consequences of modelling; it does not depend on claiming that the original framework cannot accommodate exploration. [Friston, Thornton, and Clark, *Free-Energy Minimization and the Dark-Room Problem*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00130/full).

AdaResearch already asks, through Self-Q, what a maker's choices make thinkable, what they foreclose, and what remains hidden. This essay extends that inquiry to the conditions under which others can answer back. The maker is also shaped by institutions, tools, dependencies, and previous decisions; recognition is not an escape into an independent observing self. A decision becomes more answerable when those relations can change it. The hidden remainder might be an excluded participant, an unmeasured dependency, a refuge, or damage made difficult to see. Calling it generative in advance would be another way of deciding its meaning without an encounter.

For the project, this suggests a laboratory where learners can encounter the difference between improving a reading and improving the conditions it describes. They might make a display easier to predict while discovering that a partner has lost the ability to respond. They might preserve an apparently redundant route that becomes usable when another route fails. They should also be able to change what the instrument counts, question the objective, and discover when a protective constraint makes more action possible. These are proposed teaching encounters, not claims about existing gameplay.

Q then develops beyond a preference for more disorder or faster transformation. It becomes a commitment to sustaining relations in which a model can be corrected without first requiring the world to become its likeness. Intelligence with Q would be recognizable in the possibilities it helps others retain, including possibilities it has not learned to name.
