# What remains uncertain

The chairs are pink and black, at a height familiar to the knees. Groups of eight stand on both sides of a low divider. A full-size pink chair rests on a shelf against the entrance wall. It is the same model used in the groups. Their resemblance offers somewhere to begin. Before the numbers, there are seats, backs, spaces between legs, a place where a body might settle.

At the first group, four chairs are pink and four are black. The wall proposes a draw: choose one chair with equal chance, record its colour, and return it. The next recorded colour has two equally likely possibilities. Shannon entropy gives this uncertainty a value of one bit. A bit is the unit associated with two equally likely alternatives. The neighbouring version rearranges the colours among the sites. The pattern changes while the reading stays still.

Another group has seven pink chairs and one black. Here, a pink prediction succeeds more often. The entropy falls. We may find the single black chair more conspicuous than before; the measure does not become larger because our attention gathers around it. It reads the declared probabilities of the colour outcomes.

Across the divider, four pink and four black chairs occupy eight marked sites. Here the question concerns the whole arrangement. The left four sites can be entirely pink, leaving the right four black. Under this description there is only one matching colour assignment. Two neighbouring versions each have two pink chairs on either side. Each permits thirty-six assignments. Their visible differences fit the same brief.

This brief is called a macrostate: a coarse account that leaves some details unspecified. Boltzmann's expression, S = kB ln Ω, relates entropy to the number Ω of compatible microstates under appropriate statistical assumptions. Our chair version makes those assumptions small enough to inspect: fixed sites, fixed colour totals, and no distinction between chairs of the same colour. Its display gives S/kB. We have made a counting model, rather than discovered the thermal entropy of furniture. The colour-draw reading remains one bit in either arrangement.

At the threshold, a framed account shares a wall with a room image, a blank drawing surface and loose notes. A comparison can remain there as a sketch or a question while we move on.

Beyond the wall, the chairs spread along another wall in four groups. A regular grid gives way to one altered chair, then to a repeated disturbance, then to a scatter of turns and offsets. The eye encounters a growing difficulty in carrying the arrangement away as a pattern. A description might have to remember more exceptions.

The recipes on the wall interrupt that progression. The last group was generated from a seed by a short rule. A visually unruly arrangement can have a concise construction. Algorithmic complexity asks for the length of the shortest program that produces a specified object, relative to a decoding language. We can offer programs and compare descriptions; there is no general procedure that always finds the shortest. Here the common generator remains part of the account. The appearance of disorder has not answered the question of how much must be written to rebuild it.

Three doorways are cut into one solid wall. Two door pairs share a repeating clock. In each pair, a translucent leaf begins almost at the same angle as the solid one. Under the closing rule, the difference contracts. Each update brings both leaves nearer the frame. Waiting makes their future less surprising.

Another rule drives the neighbouring pair. A normalized angle doubles and wraps back into its allowed interval. Nearby starting values can then separate. The apparatus records whether an angle lies in the lower or upper half at each step. Across a declared set of starting bins, longer records keep acquiring distinctions under this expanding rule. The closing rule soon supplies no new branch. These are two authored dynamics made visible by a door; the ordinary act of closing was never sufficient evidence of chaos. Between the two studies, a third door stays open and leaves a passage through.

Behind it, five cups share a table with a plate and a ring-shaped donut. Looking into the cups, white milk stretches through brown coffee. The first has a distinct pale region; the next draw it into finer swirls; the last has an even colour. Each surface keeps the same proportion of milk. What changes is its distribution. The moving image makes a process available to the eye, without supplying its thermodynamic measurement.

The labels give a separate, simpler calculation: a cooling cup at zero, five, fifteen, thirty and sixty seconds. Heat leaves it and enters a finite surrounding body. The cup loses entropy while the surroundings gain more. The total increases as heat crosses the temperature difference. The units are joules per kelvin: this model concerns energy and temperature. Neither the milk pattern nor the pale steam wisp is used to calculate that number. All five moments remain available on the table; this coexistence belongs to the digital reconstruction, not to a physical way of returning heat unaided.

Another wall introduces another loss. The same pink chair stands behind five panes, from clear glass through increasing frost to an opaque surface. Blur spreads the edges of the chair. The accompanying records progressively leave details out: first the complete identity, then colour and arms, then colour alone, and finally no distinction among the chairs. Eight identities were equally possible beforehand: two colours, with or without arms, with or without a lower back rail.

The two middle colour records deliberately keep the same information despite their different degrees of frosting. Four chairs still fit either record, leaving two bits unresolved. Conditional entropy names the uncertainty remaining after a specified observation. A more blurred picture need not change this declared account. Our recognition may follow a different course; it has not been measured by the shader.

At the last encounter, three tables each hold sixteen balls of the same size. The first has eight pink and eight teal; the second has twelve pink and four teal; the last is entirely pink. The question stays the same: choose a colour before one ball is drawn with equal chance and returned.

At the first table either colour succeeds on eight of sixteen chances. Pink wins on twelve at the next, and on all sixteen at the last. Nothing about the size of a ball or a change of naming intervenes. The safest guess becomes progressively safer.

Min-entropy expresses that best guess's chance in bits: minus the base-two logarithm of its probability. The three readings are one bit, about 0.415 bits, and zero. Zero belongs to the table where the answer is certain. Shannon entropy averages surprise across the possible outcomes; this question attends only to the most likely one.

We have carried one word through a room of familiar things. Each encounter required another account of what could happen, what was kept, or what was allowed to vary. An arrangement can become harder to read while its colour entropy holds. A door can move while uncertainty contracts. A cooling cup can lose entropy while the combined system gains it. Before asking for more or less entropy, there is this work of finding what the question includes—and what its answer leaves outside.

The next hall keeps this need for a precise question, then puts it under pressure: what can change while a measurement remains still?
