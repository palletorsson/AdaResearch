# The wall between the bars

The first work seems to be a rectangle of colour. Closer, each stripe has a side. Between the stripes is the same wall that continues beyond the work.

Open the intervals. Twenty-four bars occupy more of the room's width without acquiring another bar. The wall comes forward between them. How much of what you were calling the work was the distance you had stopped noticing?

At the next structure, horizontal and vertical colours cross. Bring the verticals towards you. The front view keeps much of its order; the shadows begin to disagree with it. From the side, there is a gap where a joint seemed to be.

Make every member pink. The crossings persist. So do the shadows. Colour had been helping to sort the parts, and now depth has more of that work to do.

Farther along, a line turns, stops, begins again. It has the confidence of a diagram. A narrow groove runs along each coloured face, recalling a component waiting to be fastened to something else.

Remove the upper rail. The wall is still there. The outline has lost an agreement.

These studies return to Liam Gillick's wall structures: repeated industrial elements, compact assemblies, lines that suggest the organisation of a process. We can identify every part of our diagram and still have no process for it to explain. The neatness is persuasive. It has not told us what it is persuading us of.

From the bench, the three works become small again. Their shadows remain attached to the room.
