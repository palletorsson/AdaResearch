# What the next room keeps

6 October 2026 · Endless Museum · Research journal

In the Trace tutorial, the point you learned to hold becomes a drawing tool. Move it, and selected positions enter a list. Release it, and the program saves the record together with its visible segments. In Grid, that drawing arrives as a sculpture. There is no need to invent another gesture to begin the next lesson.

A pink version keeps the saved work. A separate green copy takes its positions from rounded grid addresses. Change the spacing and the copy changes. The source remains available.

This small passage helped us find the shape of the first chapter. A lesson can leave something that the next room has a reason to receive. What travels might be a drawing, a class, a way of comparing two constructions, or a question that has become more precise.

The distinction between those things matters. The same point construction returns across several tutorials, but that does not mean one live node survives every scene change. Trace carries an actual saved record into Grid. Later lessons reuse classes or load selected positions into a new study. A convincing story of continuity needs to say which kind of continuity it has built.

The book follows an existing museum. Its halls contain more than the tutorial needs to teach. Some artifacts belong to the reflective text, some to the tutorial, some to both, and some remain part of the surrounding field. Choosing one operation to teach does not require explaining every object in the room.

That selection has been difficult. An attractive example can teach the wrong thing at the right location. A tutorial about a held point can drift into distance calculations when its useful new work is pickup and reference frames. A complete script can also contain more machinery than the lesson intends to teach. Making all of it visible is necessary for reconstruction; giving every line equal explanatory weight can bury the encounter.

Reading the complete Primitives tutorial alongside its reflective sequence made another principle visible: **what changes, and what is kept?**

This is a question for a particular comparison. In Lines, moving one endpoint changes the span while the point's construction stays the same. In the sphere study, changing rings while holding radial segments fixed lets us inspect one direction of resolution. Its spherical collider does not become faceted with the visible mesh. In Columns, keeping the profile and height while changing the number of sides makes sampling legible. A later comparison can hold sampling fixed and change the profile.

What is kept depends on the question. There is no permanent list of invariants attached to each hall. Naming the comparison prevents an image of several simultaneous changes from standing in for an explanation of one.

The same discipline protects the writing. `tutorial.md` gives the situation, the complete reduced code, the supplied dependencies and an action whose consequence can be checked. `final.md` stays with the encounter and its implications. A visible face can leave the body free to pass through. A column's height, lighting and placement can invite reverence without its generating rule requiring it. Knowing how the surface was made opens these questions; it does not finish them.

We have been calling the connection between these kinds of work “type-safe” writing. It is a working analogy. A position value is not the sphere that displays it. A mesh is not its collider. An artistic association is not a demonstrated historical influence. A function appearing in a listing is not a skill the student has acquired. The edges need to remain legible so the different kinds of knowledge can meet without silently replacing one another.

This also changes how we describe progress. A concept may be supplied for use, introduced through explanation, practised through a variation, or demonstrated independently by a learner. These are different statuses. The held-time controller in Godot Primitives supports a lesson about resolution. Its presence should not count as evidence that the student can design a timer. The closing composition task asks for independent work; until somebody attempts it, that independence is an intention.

The Ghost Student helps with another part of the problem. The printed programs can be assembled, run in Godot and checked against behavioural assertions. Deliberately breaking the neighbour loop or making a copy alter its source should make those checks fail. This gives us evidence about the runnable construction and the sensitivity of its tests. It cannot tell us whether a beginner understood the explanation or found a reason to continue.

Short instructions give a future writer a way back into these decisions:

> **BRING** the saved trace. **CHANGE** the copy's grid spacing. **KEEP** the source record and its geometry. **LEARN** how addresses derive another representation. **TEST** the predicted address and source preservation. **CARRY** the original positions into Triangle.

That brief is an address into the work. It needs the source files, full lesson and evidence behind it. If the teaching intention changes, the brief should change too, followed by the affected lessons and checks. Compact memory is useful when it helps us recover and revise a decision.

By Melencolia, the student has several ways to make a body. A function can return positions; indices can connect them; a class can make another instance. None of those operations chooses where the object belongs. The closing exercise asks for an arrangement using learned constructions and for an account of the choices made. Composition is part of learning: it asks what the available capacity is for.

Primitives begins beside a counter already running. It returns to that counter after the rooms have made some of the world's construction available to inspection. The continued process does not certify mastery. It gives a place to notice what has changed in our ability to act, and what still needs another person, another trial, another room.

What we take into Transformation is concrete: the sphere and cube constructions, their independent instances, a known reference frame, and the distinction between altering construction and changing placement. Motion can give these bodies another capacity. The next chapter should earn that change while keeping enough familiar material for the student to recognise what happened.
