The right-hand picture changes. The left stays as it was. Above them, the seed still has the same number. You pressed +1 DRAW, and the small patch you were watching has lost its arrangement of colours. The cubes remain in their cells, as though nothing of consequence has happened. Yet the two grids no longer agree.

A moment earlier they did. Perhaps you had found an orange beside a violet that you wanted to keep. REPLAY brought the arrangement back, including that unplanned pleasure. The score from Cage’s lesson could be heard again; this bench makes a narrower promise. Keep one number, and the picture can return.

The number begins a procedure. A pseudorandom generator moves through states by a deterministic rule; the seed sets its starting state. With the same generator and sequence of requests, it produces the same values again. But the picture requires another agreement: each value must be given the same job. Here, successive draws supply the red, green and blue amounts of each cube. +1 DRAW discards one value before those assignments begin, shifting every group of three. Nothing in a draw says that it is red.

Nearby, a number-book page recalls RAND’s *A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates*, published in 1955. Researchers needed samples for Monte Carlo calculations. An electronic roulette device produced digits in 1947, but tests found biases. Engineers refined the machine, and the resulting table was transformed before publication. Prepared for punched-card machines, this chance passed through considerable work.

A printed table keeps its numbers where they can be found again. Our generator calculates its sequence afresh. Both can support a return, provided we know where to begin and how to take the next number. But the museum’s page does something else: its digits fall downward and disappear. Touch can hold a cell while the others continue. The page resembles a book until you try to reopen a line that has fallen away.

Look again at the changed patch. You might prefer it. An orange has acquired another neighbour; a stubborn corner has become unexpectedly pleasing. The bench reports a lost match, but you have begun making a different comparison. The generator has not chosen between two pictures. Nor can the test decide what deserves to be kept.

At the textile panels, follow one cell from display to display. The same draws are assigned different pairs of marks: diagonals, upright and horizontal bars, large and small blocks. Something that leaned now stands or fills more of its square. Stay until a figure emerges in one panel that you cannot find in another. Each has supplied a vocabulary before chance gets to choose.

The cube grid has a vocabulary too. Its colours may change; its cubes cannot leave their cells. Randomness varies what the program has made variable. Suppose we gave some draws another job: deciding which cubes could slip out of the panel. This is an experiment we could build. A corner might lengthen into a sleeve, dragging its last square over the floor while you look for a place to put your hand.

Then we would have to decide how the sleeve follows a wrist, where it catches, whether its weight can pull. Rules accumulate around a simple wish to wear the diagram. A pattern that pleased the eye has become a possible relation with the body. The bench can reproduce the pattern, but it cannot decide to make that move.

Return to the patch you wanted to keep. In the next hall, colours will be gathered into counts, and a gauge will read their shares. It may give the same reading for two arrangements whose neighbours are entirely different. Your pleasure may have depended on precisely that relation. Carry it with you when the number appears.
