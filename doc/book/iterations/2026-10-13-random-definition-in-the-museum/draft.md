Take hold of the dark ball in front of you. It carries you away from its centre, slows, then brings you back. There is a short wait before the next excursion. A direction and a distance have been drawn; you have not chosen either. Keep your grip for the return, or let go halfway and watch the ball finish without you.

It comes back to the same place. The next departure can be different. You might begin waiting for the return before the outward journey is over. Or stay where you released it, watching from a place the ball will pass through. It still has an appointment at the centre.

At the colour bench, returning takes another form. Two grids, four cubes across and four high. Perhaps an orange beside a violet makes you stop. REPLAY brings the arrangement back, including that unplanned pleasure. Cage’s score could be heard again. Here you can keep the number above the grids and ask for another look.

That number, the seed, sets the starting state of a pseudorandom generator: a procedure that produces its sequence by a deterministic rule. The same generator, starting state and sequence of requests give the same values. A picture needs one more agreement. Each value must be given the same job. Here, three successive draws supply the red, green and blue amounts of a cube.

Press +1 DRAW. The seed stays; the right-hand picture changes. One value has been discarded before colouring begins, shifting every group of three. Nothing in a draw says that it is red. Your orange has acquired another neighbour. The bench no longer matches itself. Perhaps you prefer it this way.

Keeping chance available for another calculation once meant preparing an enormous book. RAND’s *A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates*, published in 1955, supplied researchers with numbers for Monte Carlo calculations: sampling possible cases rather than working through every one. The digits began in an electronic roulette device in 1947. Tests found biases. Engineers refined the machine, and the table was transformed before publication.

Prepared for punched-card machines, this chance passed through considerable work. A printed table keeps its numbers where they can be found again; our generator calculates its sequence afresh. Either way, we need to know where to begin and how to take the next number. But the page standing in this museum has made another arrangement with time.

Its digits fall downward and disappear. Touch can hold a cell while the others continue. Try following one number to the bottom. The page resembles a book until you try to reopen a line that has fallen away.

Back at the grids, the cubes have kept their places through every change of colour. Here, randomness varies what the program has made variable. Another seed cannot persuade a cube to leave its cell.

Imagine taking a coloured square between your fingers and drawing it towards your wrist. Suppose we let its neighbours follow, still joined, bending as you pull. A corner folds around your arm; the rest trails across the floor. We have not built this behaviour. Some draws might choose which squares come loose. Keeping them connected would require another rule.

Now there is a sleeve to contend with. Where does it catch? Does your wrist drag it, or can its weight pull back? The colour that made you stop has become something you want to put your hand inside. Replaying the picture will not get it there.

The next hall gathers coloured symbols into counts. Its gauge can agree about two arrangements whose neighbours are entirely different. Remember the patch you wanted to keep when you meet these new patterns. What will counting keep of the relation that made you stay?
