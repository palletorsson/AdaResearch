## Thesis (for the student)

You start where Klee's Pedagogical Sketchbook starts: a point that goes for a walk. You write an instruction, give a body a time, decide with one 'if' whether it is solid, carry it, join two points with a line, keep your walk, and close it into a floor you stand on. By the end of the first week a machine has drafted one of your bodies, you have caught it, and you are standing among made and scanned bodies in the headset. From there the parts are named by what code does to a body, so you always know where you are. You REPEAT a body until many become one, colour it by a rule, let a seeded draw replace the rule, give it noise to stand on and run one function for every pixel. You PARAMETERIZE it: the families you have been making by hand become a type with exported fields, a sheet in the editor, a budget and a file. Then you TRANSFORM it (joints, scale, a weather), put it under FORCE (a gravity you write, a balance that fails, a ray that reaches, legs that drive from the foot, a swing you hear, a game), let rules GROW it, and finally LEAVE THE LAB, where the approximations fail in public before you make a body others may change. Each idea arrives when a body needs it: a line needs a function, a walk needs a list, a family needs a class, a fall needs a force. The order is related to Ada's spine but not forced by it: chance, noise and colour come earlier than the spine puts them because you need them in the first month and because regularity must exist for chance to be seen; Parameterize has no spine place at all; every departure is named where it happens, and the spine index below shows each one as a jump. In every chapter you build a body, vary one axis, write down before you render which two will look most alike, defend one sentence with the picture, say what the approximation owes or hides, and take the body where a person meets it. One thread is queer in the code itself: the scene tree's parent-commands-child is Klee's active-and-passive chain, and later you make the passive end drive. That kind of geometric and bodily critique has almost no precedent in critical code studies; this course is trying it.

## How a chapter runs

Each chapter is an assignment in Code as Creative Medium's shape, run with PRIMM:

1. **Brief** (one sentence) and **precedents** (works that answered it before, with at least one from the Queer 3D lineage where it fits).
2. **Walk the hall first and guess** how its primary work is made (Predict). The halls are stand-alone challenges; several chapters may visit one.
3. **Closed exercise** on a runnable kit or a small scene: run, investigate, modify one thing (Run, Investigate, Modify). One **planted bug** per chapter whose message the student reads (listed last in each chapter's Godot line).
4. **Make** the body, at two depths: the short track makes the smallest body that carries the argument; the long track takes the project step into the student's own hall.
5. **Argument**: the body becomes a family along one axis (3-5 values). Before rendering, write which two will look most alike and why, and date it. Render the sheet. Write one claim sentence the picture supports. A prediction the render contradicts is worth the most.
6. **Debt**: one reading of what the approximation owes or hides, tied to the code idea that made the body, after the making.
7. **Place**: take the body where a person meets it: stand on it, walk through it, carry it, become it; desktop first, the headset reachable (the walker's skin is separate from its controller from ch19 on).

## Parts

- **One body: the point goes for a walk (Klee I.1-4, Kandinsky)** (ch1-9). You can write an instruction, build one body in code, give it a time, decide whether it is solid, carry it, relate it by a line, keep its walk, close the walk into a floor, check code a machine drafted for you, and stand among found and made bodies in the headset from a build you exported.
- **Repeat: structure, colour, chance, noise, pixel (Klee I.5-8; Nake, Nees, Molnar)** (ch10-14). You can make many bodies by a rule, colour them by a rule, let a seeded draw replace the rule, give them noise to stand on, and run one function for every pixel at once, and you can say what each repetition hides.
- **Parameterize: the family as a type (an addition to the spine, declared)** (ch15-18). You can write a body as a class whose exported fields make a family, sweep it into a comparison sheet in the editor, measure what each value costs, and save a body as a file someone else can build.
- **Transform: joints, scale, weather (Klee I.9-12, II)** (ch19-21). You can build a jointed body and name who commands whom, open the player body and read scale as a relation, and move many bodies by a field.
- **Force and rhythm: to stand although it could fall (Klee II-IV)** (ch22-27). Your bodies fall by gravity you wrote, balance and topple, reach by a ray, walk from the foot up, swing and sound, and play: a small complete game you export.
- **Grow: rules that make bodies and worlds (NoC 7-9; WP2-3)** (ch28-32). Bodies made by neighbour rules, recursion and grammars, bred by a fitness you write, a generated world searched as a graph, and a flock with no leader: procedures you can stop, save and argue with.
- **Leave the lab: failure before futures (WP4; Queer 3D debt, then ontology)** (ch33-38). Bodies that deform, fuse and learn, a model's limits made walkable, the course's own error measured, and a public body others may change.

#### ch2. Point Zero: a clock, and the engine calls you

*Spine:* primitives. *Halls:* Point_Zero (carrier), WaveFunctions_Pendulum (encounter). *Tracks:* short, long.
*New:* variables, lifecycle, delta. *Returns:* statements, output, errors.

- **Brief.** Give a body a time of its own.

## Self-sieve (three questions)

1. **Does it thicken the cognitive water?** Yes where it matters for a beginner: every part has a one-word handle (repeat, parameterize, transform, force, grow, leave), every chapter a body you can stand in, and the spine index lets a teacher enter at a hall and find the chapter. The drag thread gives the queer lens something to do in code, not only in the debt notes.
2. **What is foreclosed?** A student who wants motion by cause early waits until ch21-22; the Parameterize part is the price. Klee's colour-last ending and his gravity-before-pendulum order are both dropped. The short track never meets a shader or a surface built vertex by vertex. Three late chapters introduce no new idea: fine for a synthesis, thin if a teacher runs them as code weeks.
3. **What lives in the dark spot?** The prediction-and-claim ritual can harden into a scoreboard, most of all at ch37 where the course measures the student; the open question kept beside the number is the guard. The verbs are Form+Code's formalism: they can hide that 'repeat' already carries the grid's politics, which is why ch10's debt says so. Several planted bugs are [mem] and must be run once before a class meets them.
