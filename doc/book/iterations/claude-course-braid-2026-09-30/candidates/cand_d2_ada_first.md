## For the student: why this order

This course walks the way Paul Klee's Pedagogical Sketchbook walks, the book Ada's own research plan says it wants to put 'in drag, in VR'. A point goes for a walk and becomes a line; the line closes into a plane you can stand on; the plane repeats and becomes structure; chance disturbs the structure and, given a memory, becomes ground; the body gets joints, becomes a family, stands, falls along a curve, swings, sounds and glows, and the book ends in colour. Where Klee stops, the course follows Ada's spine out of the sketchbook and says so. Every chapter also carries one of five verbs (repeat, transform, parameterize, visualize, simulate), so you can always say what your code is doing to form, and each verb needs the one before it: disorder is invisible without a grid, a family needs a class, a simulation needs all of these. The programming ideas arrive when a body needs them: a point needs a variable, a line a function, a walk a list, a floor a loop, a family a class, a balance physics, a curve your own gravity, a swing sine. In every chapter you build a body in GDScript, vary it along one axis, write down before rendering which two variants will look most alike, defend one sentence with the picture, ask what the approximation owes or hides, and then stand on the body, carry it, walk through it or become it. 'In drag' is not a costume here: Klee maps active, medial and passive onto brain, tendon and bone and onto male and female, and the scene tree does the same with parent and child. You will learn that hierarchy, and then make the passive end drive.

## Parts

- **Point, line, plane (Kandinsky; Klee I.1-4)** (ch01 to ch08). From a point you place to a plane you walked and can stand on, and a first build that runs outside your editor. You learn that the engine calls your code, that everything is an object in a tree, that a line is a record of a walk, and that a machine drafter fills your gaps with its defaults.
- **Structure: repetition, disorder, articulation (Klee I.5-12; Nake, Nees, Molnar)** (ch09 to ch13). Repeat a body and it becomes a floor; move the rule into the surface and it becomes a pattern; disturb it and chance appears; give chance a memory and it becomes ground you can walk; articulate the body and it moves by itself.
- **The family (Klee I.7-8; Albers; Molnar): Parameterize, declared as an addition** (ch14 to ch18). You describe a body in a single class whose exported fields make a family, sweep one axis into a comparison sheet, measure what each value costs, save a body as a file, and bring a found body into the family's room. The short track pivots into its mini project here.
- **Dimension and balance (Klee II)** (ch19 to ch21). The eye and the standing body: perspective as a fact about eye height, balance as a fact about the centre of mass, and legs that let the passive end drive. The walker you have used since day one is opened.
- **The gravitational curve (Klee III)** (ch22 to ch25). You write gravity yourself: a curve from steps, regions with other laws, a stair that is a function, and a small complete game about staying upright.
- **Kinetic and chromatic energy (Klee IV; Albers)** (ch26 to ch30). Motions that return: pendulum, circle, spiral, sound, the arrow and the field of arrows; then colour as a system and the shader. Each makes something invisible visible, and each picture hides a choice.
- **Out of the sketchbook: bodies in drag (spine 13-25)** (ch31 to ch41). Where Klee has no page: rules in time, recursion that must be stopped, grammars, generated worlds, soft, implicit and collective bodies, bodies that learn, the crisis of foundations, entropy measured on your own predictions, and your hall in the collective museum.

## The lesson rhythm inside every chapter

Each chapter is a **brief with a precedent** (the lineage line names it), a **closed exercise**, and an **open make** (after Code as Creative Medium, and Albers's cut-paper problems before free studies).

1. **Decode the hall** (PRIMM Predict; Aesthetic Programming's Decode): walk the carrier hall first and guess how its work is built.
2. **Run, investigate, modify** the kit or the supplied body; change one line.
3. **The closed exercise is the ARGUMENT**: a body with one exported axis, 3-5 values. Before rendering, write the PREDICTION in the sketchbook README: which two will look most alike, with the arithmetic when there is one (the chord gap of 16 segments, the 6 cm corner of a 5 degree cube). After rendering, the comparison sheet and one CLAIM sentence it supports. The prediction is a floor, not a forecast: when the sheet comes back less different than predicted, stop and look for the fault (the Ada DNA programme's rule).
4. **The planted bug**: each chapter has one real error, and the student reads its message before fixing it (listed for the teacher below).
5. **Make** your own body (the project step), carried into your room.
6. **Debt, after the making**: what the approximation owes or hides, in one reading or one code-critique snippet of Ada's own code; from ch14 on it is also a debt comment in your code.
7. **Place**: a body meets yours: stand on it, walk through it, carry it, become it.
