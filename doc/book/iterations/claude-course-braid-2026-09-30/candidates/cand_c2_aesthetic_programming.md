## For the student: why this order

This course borrows its shape from Winnie Soon and Geoff Cox's Aesthetic Programming (2020): every chapter pairs one programming idea with one critical question and ends in a small work in two halves, the body you run (RunMe) and the argument you write (ReadMe). Here the work is always a body built in code, the argument is a family of that body with a prediction you write before you render it (which two will look most alike, and why), and the question is what the approximation owes. The order is the order in which you need things to make bodies. First one point that begins, resists, is held, is joined to another and leaves a trace (variable geometry). Then time and repetition turn one body into many and many vertices into a surface you can stand on (infinite loops). Then bodies come in from outside - a scan, your own tracked body, a touch - and the machine starts generating them by chance, noise, flow and rule (capture and auto-generation). Then you own a body type as a class, give it mass, balance and flesh, and let rules speak as recursion, grammar, sound and a recipe that travels (object abstraction and vocable code). Then the skin becomes data: pixels, shaders, patterns and fields (que(e)ry data). Last come the procedures that navigate, flock and learn, and the places where computation cannot close. Ada's halls are your field trips. The spine is the map you walk, but you will meet some halls earlier or later than the spine does, and each time the chapter says why.

## Palle's question: are there courses like this to learn from?

Yes, and one is almost a twin in method. **Aesthetic Programming** is a ten-chapter, fourteen-week course for students who have never programmed, taught at Aarhus. Each chapter ties one piece of syntax to one critical theme: coordinates to the politics of faces, loops to computational time, random to control and care, classes to abstraction as a political choice. Every week ends in a MiniX with a RunMe and a ReadMe, and gets peer feedback [V in research_ccs.md, from the book's chapter pages]. Around it: Marino's *Critical Code Studies* (close reading of real code, including Kittler's raytracer), Montfort's *Exploratory Programming* (modify before write, errors in week one), *10 PRINT* (regularity before randomness, ports), Fuller's *Lexicon* (one reading per code idea), Chun (the critique of 'seeing the source'), and the CCS Working Group, whose 2024 round had a Queer(ing) Code week and a teaching thread [V in research_ccs.md]. SFPC's *Code Societies* (winter 2019) is a precedent for the three-week format with a different guest each session [V in research_creative.md].

What none of them has is the part Ada is about. They are 2D and web-first, with no engine that calls your code, no persistent 3D objects, no bodies to stand on. Their critique is mostly social (data, platforms, language), and their queerness is linguistic and data-centred. Only three footholds critique geometry (Kittler's raytracer, 10 PRINT's regularity and randomness, the Lexicon's Pixel, Glitch and Elegance). The queer and feminist courses found make in Twine and paper; the art-school 3D and VR courses make without a queer lens [V in research_queer.md; the search was not exhaustive]. So this candidate takes AP's chapter anatomy and order, and says plainly where Ada must invent: the body, the family axis visible in a still, the prediction that can be wrong, and a geometric queer lens.

## Parts

- **Getting started: variable geometry** (c01-c06). You can make one point, give it a surface and a separate boundary, hold it, connect it to a second point and keep its trace, and you can say what the starter scene assumed a body is. (Aesthetic Programming ch. 1-2.)
- **Infinite loops: time, repetition, surface, colour** (c07-c12). Time and repetition become form: one body spins, one figure is built from parts, one becomes many, many vertices become a surface you can stand on, an index becomes a colour and a motif becomes a floor. From here every chapter ends in a family sheet with a written prediction. (AP ch. 3.)
- **Capture and auto-generation** (c13-c20). Bodies come in from outside (a scan, your own tracked body, a touch), then the machine starts generating them: chance with a seed, chance with a shape, noise, fields and rules. You can say what the capture takes and what the pseudo-random owes. (AP ch. 4-5; a midterm generative hall closes the part.)
- **Object abstraction and vocable code** (c21-c28). You own a body type as a class with exported axes (a family), give it mass, balance and flesh, then let rules speak: recursion, grammars, sound and recipes that travel between halls. (AP ch. 6-7, with The Nature of Code's Mover supplying the physics AP lacks.)
- **Que(e)ry data: pixels, shaders, fields and cuts** (c29-c32). A body's skin becomes data: painted pixels, shaders that answer light, a reaction-diffusion skin ported from CPU to GPU, and bodies that are fields rather than parts. (AP ch. 8.)
- **Algorithmic procedures and machine unlearning** (c33-c38). Procedures that navigate, flock and learn, and the places where computation cannot close: impossible bodies, entropy, bias. The final project is planned as a flowchart at the start of the part and shown in the class museum at its end. (AP ch. 9-10.)

#### 2. Variable geometry I: a point that begins and continues (`c02`; tracks: long)
