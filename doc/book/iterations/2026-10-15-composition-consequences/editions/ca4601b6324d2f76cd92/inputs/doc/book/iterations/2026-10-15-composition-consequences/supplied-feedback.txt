I see what this composition pilot is testing: three complete versions of What chance is allowed to do, with the 926-word witness trial as the reference, followed by a 950-word patient observation version and a 967-word limits of observation version.

My main finding is that the comparison is useful, but the literary operations have not yet produced substantially different readings.

## Comparing the three versions

| Reference — 926 words             | Clicks, repetition, explanation          | Strongest economy and rhythm                                   |
| Patient observation — 950 words   | Additional time spent listening          | More duration, but little new discovery                        |

### 1. Patient observation

The addition is:

> We stay through another click. The screen still reads 435.

This is consistent with a Perec-like strategy, but it doesn't yet do much. The narrator stays longer, while the observation remains essentially unchanged.

Perec's method becomes interesting when continued attention accumulates particulars, omissions or discrepancies. Here the passage has more duration without acquiring much more information.

### 2. Limits of observation

This adds:

> The returning gap gives us something to recognise. It does not disclose how the gap was made.

This is more productive. It distinguishes the observable replay from knowledge of the generating procedure.

But then:

> The screen supplies a number; the sound supplies a sequence. Neither is the calculation itself.

I feel the narrator moving back toward philosophical exposition.

The observation has already opened the problem. The second sentence resolves it into an epistemological statement.

### 3. Reference

I still prefer the original:

> Click. A gap. Another click.

> This machine detects no radiation. Its waiting times are calculated. We press RESTART.

> Click. The same gap. Another click.

The repetition actually performs something that the two alternatives discuss.

But it is not yet a convincing test of map-level composition.

We have changed the literary operation applied to one encounter, without substantially changing how the encounters influence one another.

And this is where I think the dynamic writing system needs a more demanding principle:

A strategy must produce a new observation, narrative relationship or formal consequence—not merely a new way of expressing an existing interpretation.

For example, a patient-observation strategy might require looking at the 96 events long enough to notice a particular interval returning. An archival strategy might move the acquisition receipt before the first click. A Beckett-inspired composition might allow several identical restarts before any explanation. These would yield materially different reading experiences.

## My proposed next experiment

Test three genuinely different compositional operations:

Repetition — Beckett

The Geiger clicks recur, with explanations withheld. The writing itself repeats, and a small discrepancy in the reader's attention becomes significant.

Montage — Eliot

The receipt, screen display, recorded intervals and participant's observation appear in distinct registers. Their juxtaposition establishes the relation without a continuous explanatory narrator.

Recurrence — Joyce / Borges

The number 435 appears in different apparatuses, each time producing something unlike its previous appearance. The literary structure follows the number through its transformations.

These would be three meaningfully different treatments of the same evidence.

And once compared locally, the map could decide whether the chapter needs one of them. Perhaps the RAND book already establishes archival montage, making another archival intervention at the Geiger unnecessary. Perhaps repetition at the Geiger prepares the bodily return at the end.

That is the kind of editorial intelligence the map could contribute.

## One other point: distinguish the edition from the experiment

The pilot rightly labels these versions as unadopted proposals. I would preserve this discipline.

I would also record the observed effect separately from the intended effect. Calling a candidate "patient observation" documents an editorial intention; it does not establish that a reader experiences it as patient or that it rewards sustained attention.

Likewise, the source asserts a shared hall seed and particular replay mechanisms. The literary comparison can hold these assertions fixed, but their implementation status should remain tied to the code and integration record.

## My conclusion

The reference remains my preferred edition. Neither alternative makes enough of a difference to justify replacing it yet.

But the experiment is useful because it reveals something about the method: merely adding an authorial operation to a passage doesn't necessarily transform the literature.

I think we now need two explicit tests:

- Artifact test: Does this strategy make us notice, investigate or experience something we would otherwise miss?
- Map test: Does the resulting passage change the significance of something elsewhere in the sequence?

A strategy that passes neither test should be discarded, however interesting its literary source.

A strategy that passes both is especially valuable.

That gives us a way to make writing dynamic without making variation itself the objective.
