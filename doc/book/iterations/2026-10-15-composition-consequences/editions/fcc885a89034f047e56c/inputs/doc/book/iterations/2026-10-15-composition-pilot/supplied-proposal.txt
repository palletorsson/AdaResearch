Yes. I think we can turn this into something much more interesting than a style guide: a compositional system in which every artifact has literary possibilities, every map has a dramaturgy, and the writing changes as the relationships between artifacts change.

This could become another research instrument inside Ada Research.

The crucial distinction is:

An author is not a voice to imitate. An author offers a way of operating on an encounter.

Perec gives us enumeration, patient attention and the ordinary. Ahmed offers orientation, lines and reachability. Beckett offers repetition and exhaustion. Borges offers recursive rules and paradox. Haraway offers situated, partial knowledge. These are different procedures for writing, not decorative styles.

If we assign those procedures to the actual material qualities of the artifacts, we can create variation without producing arbitrary stylistic changes.

## 1. Three scales of literary composition

Artifact level — Encounter

How does the narrator attend to this particular object? Which authorial strategies reveal something in its behaviour?

Example: Geiger counter → Calvino's difficulties of observation + Barad's attention to measurement.

Map level — Dramaturgy

How do encounters accumulate, interrupt, echo and transform each other? Which literary structures shape their relationships?

Example: Randomness hall → Eliot's montage + Le Guin's gathering + a recurring number, 435.

Book level — Transformation

How does the narrator's capacity to perceive, describe and understand change across the larger work?

Example: From coordinates and points to memory, forces, complex systems, incomplete knowledge and agency.

The artifact can have its own literary identity without the entire chapter adopting that identity. The map composes the differences between them. The book determines what happens to these forms across time.

That separation is important. Otherwise, every chapter becomes another recognizable application of the same literary formula.

## 2. Match an author to what the artifact actually does

Here is how I would score your current randomness sequence.

| Artifact       | Primary operation                     | Secondary tension                     | Literary effect                               |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| RANDOM TOUCH   | Ahmed: Orient and follow              | Perec: Observe differences            | The body discovers directions and constraints |
| RAND BOOK      | Le Guin: Gather and keep              | Hartman: Respect the archive's limits | Preservation without complete knowledge       |
| GEIGER COUNTER | Calvino: Investigate measurement      | Beckett: Repeat                       | Precision creates a peculiar situation        |
| MOVE / KEEP    | Flusser: Translate into a program     | Ponge: Attend to the object           | A complicated gesture becomes a small number  |
| RANDOM RETURN  | Beckett: Repeat the prescribed action | Borges: Follow the rule to paradox    | Variation inside an inescapable return        |

These pairings would be proposals, not permanent classifications. Hartman's archival work, for example, has specific historical and ethical stakes that should not be reduced to a generic analogy with data loss.

Most importantly, the same author need not always produce the same literary effect.

Beckett at the Geiger counter could mean exact repetition of a sound. Beckett at the return sphere might mean the exhaustion of a bodily action. The operation changes with the material.

### The rule I would introduce

Every artifact receives:

- A material fact: what the apparatus demonstrably does.
- A primary literary operation: what the writing attempts to do with that fact.
- A counter-operation: what interrupts or complicates the primary approach.
- A refusal: what the narrator must not automatically explain, conclude or invent.

The refusal is especially important. It prevents every authorial strategy from turning into a predictable rhetorical gesture.

A text influenced by Borges does not need to end in a paradox. A text influenced by Ahmed does not need to conclude with a discussion of orientation.

Sometimes the strategy is present only in what the narrator chooses to notice.

## 3. Put the literary strategy in the artifact header

I would add metadata to the artifact definition, but keep it separate from the verified technical account.

For example, the Geiger counter could carry:

```

{
  "artifact": "geiger_counter",
  "encounter": {
    "action": "listen, restart, compare",
    "observable": [
      "96 stored detection times",
      "replay preserves the intervals",
      "seed initializes a deterministic generator"
    ],
    "provenance": "simulated detections"
  },
  "literary": {
    "primary": {
      "author": "Italo Calvino",
      "operation": "observation becomes problematic"
    },
    "counterpoint": {
      "author": "Samuel Beckett",
      "operation": "exact repetition"
    },
    "registers": [
      "situated witness",
      "sound",
      "instrument readout"
    ],
    "motifs": ["435", "interval", "return"],
    "avoid": [
      "explaining the philosophical meaning",
      "equating simulation with physical radiation",
      "ending with a rhetorical question"
    ]
  }
}

```

This is a writing contract, not a finished prompt or prose template.

The writer gets to choose how to enact Calvino's operation. It might do so through a contradiction, an unsuccessful experiment, an observation that requires another observation, or a narrator who begins to doubt the adequacy of the instrument.

The header tells us what the passage should investigate, not what sentences it should contain.

I would also add `implementation_status` and evidence references to the actual scene/code. That would prevent literary revision from quietly converting a proposed interaction into one the game already supports.

## 4. Give the map a different kind of header

The map should not merely inherit the average voice of its artifacts.

It needs a compositional score: its own structure, temporal development, recurring elements and transitions.

For the randomness hall, one possibility is:

```

{
  "map": "randomness",
  "composition": {
    "form": "montage with recurring motif",
    "reference": [
      "T. S. Eliot: juxtaposition",
      "Ursula K. Le Guin: gathering"
    ],
    "trajectory": [
      "follow",
      "keep",
      "repeat",
      "encode",
      "return"
    ],
    "recurring_elements": [
      "hand",
      "435",
      "stored movement",
      "bounded possibility"
    ],
    "registers": [
      "physical observation",
      "historical document",
      "instrument output",
      "technical explanation"
    ],
    "ending": {
      "operation": "enact a constraint",
      "explanation": "minimal"
    },
    "variation": {
      "avoid_adjacent_same_operation": true,
      "allow_silence": true,
      "allow_unresolved_observation": true
    }
  }
}

```

Here Eliot is a reference for the composition of heterogeneous materials, not an instruction to make each passage sound like The Waste Land.

The map can interrupt an intimate bodily encounter with an archival receipt, follow it with instrument sounds, and eventually return to the body.

This also suggests a fourth important data structure: the relationship between two artifacts.

For example:

RANDOM TOUCH

Numbers → movement

MOVE / KEEP

Movement → number

Relationship: inversion

The map-level writer discovers that these distant objects reverse one another. It can create a connection without requiring either artifact's text to explain the relationship.

Other relationships might be echo, contradiction, transformation, shared source, difference, recurrence, interruption, or unresolved question.

This is particularly relevant to the magnetic refrigerator wall you were envisioning. The magnets represent artifacts; the directed lines represent meaningful relationships; the reading order is one path through that graph.

Rearranging the magnets would not only change the sequence. It would change which relationships can be revealed by the writing.

## 5. How do we prevent repetition?

I would avoid using a rigid instruction such as “each artifact must have one author, one theoretical insight and one question.”

That would immediately become mechanical.

Instead, I propose a variation score that looks at the sequence as a whole.

Illustrative distribution of literary operations

Observe

Touch

Keep

RAND

Repeat

Geiger

Transform

Move

Constrain

Return

Each encounter makes a different principal demand on the writing. Secondary operations can overlap.

I would check manuscripts for local monotony rather than enforcing total novelty. Consecutive artifacts should not automatically have the same rhetorical shape, but deliberate repetition must remain possible—especially in a chapter about replay.

A useful editorial audit would ask whether the prose repeatedly opens with spatial inventory, ends with philosophical questions, resolves ambiguity into an aphorism, or explains a discovery immediately after the visitor experiences it.

The system should also notice absence. A stretch of careful observation may be more effective because no theoretical interpretation intervenes.

So the rule becomes:

Vary the operation, not merely the wording. Repeat only when repetition itself has a function.

## 6. Writing as a dynamic research process

This is where I think your proposal becomes genuinely exciting.

We can separate the evidence of an encounter from its possible literary realizations.

Artifact + source code + observations

What can actually happen, what was measured, what remains uncertain

Artifact strategy

Primary method, counterpoint, constraints

Map composition

Order, relationships, motifs, rhythm

Writing studio

Editorial process

Field notes

What was observed

Tutorial

How it operates

Final text

What it becomes

Compare → Critique → Revise → Adopt

The evidence stays fixed while the writing can change

A dynamic writing system would not necessarily mean that every visitor receives a newly generated story.

It could mean that the research and authoring interface lets you continuously test different literary arrangements of the same material.

For example, we could take the Geiger passage and deliberately test two incompatible operations.

Geiger counter — two candidate writing operations

Perec — Exhaust the observation

Calvino — Complicate observation

The machine clicks.  A pause.  Another click.  Ninety-six detections have been stored. We restart the machine. The first interval returns. Then the second. The sequence continues. Nothing has been added to the recording.  We listen for a difference.

Neither passage needs to be declared superior universally. One might work better depending on what preceded the Geiger counter, what follows it and how much explanation has already been given.

This is what makes the map an active participant in authorship.

## 7. The magnetic wall becomes a literary instrument

I would add four views to your proposed 2D/1D artifact map.

| View       | What the magnets and connections reveal                       |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Spatial    | Artifacts, rooms and actual traversal                         |
| Conceptual | Shared ideas, inversions and dependencies                     |
| Literary   | Authors, writing operations, registers, repetitions           |
| Editorial  | Field notes, tutorial text, final text, outstanding questions |

Imagine selecting the literary view.

The magnets now show their primary operations rather than their technical subjects. Connecting RANDOM TOUCH to MOVE / KEEP with an inversion edge reveals a writing possibility. Moving the Geiger counter between two other artifacts changes the surrounding rhythm. The system could then propose revised transitions, without automatically rewriting the underlying scientific descriptions.

You might even introduce a literary friction indicator: not a score of literary quality, but a warning when five consecutive passages use the same rhetorical operation, when every artifact ends in a question, or when the same theoretical explanation is being repeated.

Importantly, the author would always be allowed to override it. A machine that mechanically enforces variety would merely produce another recognizable style.

## 8. The most radical possibility: the narrator develops

At the book scale, I would avoid permanently assigning a particular voice to the entire work.

Instead, give the narrator a changing repertoire of capacities.

Early in Ada Research, perhaps the narrator can locate a point but cannot describe movement. Later it can follow a line, register duration, compare differences and preserve traces. In the randomness hall it learns that recorded repetition and experienced unpredictability can coexist.

In subsequent halls, its language might acquire the capacity to describe feedback, emergence, distributed action, instability and loss.

The narrator's evolution would then be linked to the computational concepts it encounters.

This is not merely a narrative conceit. It could be represented as metadata:

```
{
  "narrator": {
    "available_capacities": [
      "locate",
      "follow",
      "compare",
      "remember",
      "distinguish_record_from_event"
    ],
    "new_capacity": "identify_constraints_on_variation",
    "unresolved": [
      "what changes a rule",
      "what cannot be recovered from a seed"
    ]
  }
}
```

The book acquires knowledge without requiring the narrator to become omniscient. Earlier encounters might even become newly readable when revisited after later discoveries.

## 9. A practical first implementation

I would test this on the current five-artifact randomness sequence, rather than building an elaborate engine immediately.

Create literary metadata for each artifact, a map-level composition file, and a small directed graph of their relationships. Generate alternative passages only for one or two artifacts. Then assemble the full chapter and review it at the map level.

The writing process should preserve a reproducible record of which source material, metadata and instructions produced each candidate. Criticism should inspect two different questions: Is this true to the implemented experience? and What does this passage make possible as literature?

Once a version is adopted, it becomes a stable edition. Further experiments can branch from it rather than silently overwriting it.

### The larger idea

I think this approach gives you something more substantial than an AI-assisted writing workflow.

You are developing a method in which:

The artifact determines what can be observed. The literary operation determines how we attend to it. The map determines what relationships become possible. The book determines what these encounters accumulate into.

And there is one further possibility I find particularly important.

An artifact need not have a single definitive literary interpretation. Its metadata can contain competing hypotheses about how to write it. The research becomes a process of testing those hypotheses against the experience of the actual simulation.

In that sense, the literary strategy is itself experimental.

The AI should not simply decorate observations with the language of Haraway, Ahmed or Calvino. It should be capable of proposing an unexpected relationship between an artifact's operation and a literary method, demonstrating that relationship in prose, and having the proposal rejected when the writing becomes predictable or unsupported.

That would make the writing dynamic while preserving authorial responsibility.

And it fits the underlying principle of Ada Research: the goal is not to maximize the number of possible outputs. It is to build a system in which differences can be investigated, retained, recombined and sometimes allowed to change the rules of the next encounter.
