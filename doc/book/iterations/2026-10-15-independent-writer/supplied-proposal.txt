## Three examples could establish the literary voice

I would suggest that you select and refine three passages that genuinely satisfy you. Not necessarily three versions of the same encounter, but three different examples of what the writing can accomplish.

For instance:

1. An embodied encounter — the spheres, movement and orientation. Where spatial precision becomes strange without an explanation being added.
2. An epistemological encounter — the Geiger counter, measurement and incomplete knowledge. Where evidence, uncertainty and the narrator's limitations coexist.
3. A conceptual or poetic encounter — perhaps a fractal, cellular automaton or impossible geometry. Where the writing can become more speculative, associative or formally experimental.

These become calibration examples, not templates.

I would study what makes each passage work: its relationship to the material, its rhythm, its level of explanation, what it withholds and how it produces a new way of attending.

Then I could write new material using those principles without mechanically reproducing their style.

## A possible division of responsibility

Astra — The situated researcher

Knows the game, checks the code, documents the encounters, records what is implemented and what remains speculative. Supplies technical and historical evidence.

Me — The independent literary editor and writer

Reads the evidence, considers the chapter as literature, makes editorial choices, experiments with narrative structures and produces a complete final-text candidate.

You — The artistic director and researcher

Selects the exemplary passages, evaluates the literary consequences, challenges the interpretations and decides what belongs in the work.

There is one important condition: Astra should not supply me with a fully interpreted literary argument for each artifact. That could lead me simply to restate the project's existing intentions.

Instead, it should supply the material conditions of the encounter: what happens, what changes, what can be observed, what the algorithm does, and what is not yet known.

I can then develop a literary interpretation independently.

## Yes, the text should stand by itself

I would distinguish two forms of independence.

Literary independence: The chapter must work for someone who has not played the game, has not read the source code and does not know the research project. It needs its own spatial world, rhythm, discoveries and consequences. It must not read like documentation that requires the installation to become interesting.

Epistemic independence: The text must be able to make its own observations, rather than merely illustrate the theoretical argument that motivated the artifact. It can discover things that were not planned when the artifact was designed.

However, independence does not mean inventing the apparatus. If a sphere only moves the visible hand, I must not describe the whole body travelling. The scientific and implementation facts remain constraints on the literature.

And there is another advantage to this separation: I can identify when an artifact seems uninteresting from its description. That is useful evidence, even if the game itself is more compelling. It may reveal that the writing has not yet found what is distinctive about the encounter.

## I would establish one editorial rule

The final text must not be obliged to explain the artifact's intended meaning.

It must remain faithful to what the artifact does. But it may be funny, disorienting, quiet, difficult, inconclusive or unexpected.

An artifact about pseudorandomness might produce a passage about waiting. A coordinate system might produce a passage about being lost. A cellular automaton might produce a passage about persistence or disappearance.

These developments must arise from the encountered material, not from generic philosophical associations.

This also means I should have permission to reject a proposed strategy, remove explanatory paragraphs, change the order of the account or identify an unexpected connection between two rooms.

## The workflow I would test

Once you fix the three literary examples, we can begin working at the map level.

Astra supplies a compact evidence dossier for the map, including the artifact sequence, verified behaviours, technical distinctions and field observations.

I produce a complete literary chapter, not five individually optimized descriptions stitched together.

Then we conduct two separate readings:

- Literary reading: Does the chapter stand alone? Does it surprise us? Does it create curiosity, associations or a desire to remain with something?
- Technical reading: Are the descriptions true to the actual system? Have I confused a proposed experiment with an observed event? Has a metaphor distorted the scientific account?

The literary critique should come before the technical critique, so the project does not immediately pull the writing back toward documentation.

The final chapter remains a candidate until you choose to adopt it.

There is also something I would preserve about the difference between Astra and me. I would not try to eliminate it by giving both systems identical instructions and complete access to the same project.

You might benefit from two differently situated intelligences producing friction. One knows too much about the work's construction; the other knows too little and must make sense of what has been presented.

Neither position is automatically superior.

And the most interesting results may emerge precisely where my reading disagrees with the original intention.

That disagreement could lead to rewriting the passage, revising the artifact or discovering another possibility in the research.

So I would treat the three fixed examples as the beginning of a literary practice, not the definition of a house style. The objective is not to make every chapter sound alike. It is to develop a recognizable intelligence that can encounter very different computational worlds and remain capable of surprise.
