The force lesson begins with a promise: press here and this body will move. But the body meets a floor, a tether, another body. The action reaches further than the instruction. We can understand each contribution and still be surprised by the situation they produce together. That is a strong subject for Ada: the passage from making something happen to living with what has happened.

The earlier Forces atlas follows the point as it gains abilities. These three new comics follow what happens when an ability meets other conditions. They are original proposed museum encounters, informed by the references below. They are not reproductions of the artists' works, native museum captures, or results of running a physics simulation.

Three different artistic connections

Nikita Diakur — directing through a rig. His project description combines puppeteering with dynamic simulation. His site also identifies simulated marionettes, a ragdoll driven by animated springs, and physical rig tests. The useful connection is an instruction passing through a body whose joints and surroundings affect the result. Our trolley comic takes up that working relation; it does not copy a scene from his films. Artist's project and process descriptions.

Theo Triantafyllidis — inhabitants meeting a world. In Ritual (2020), he repurposes a game-engine demonstration environment and introduces inhabitants that respond to it, producing unexpected connections. This brings another scale: the event belongs to several actors and their setting. Our three-cart encounter proposes that relation inside the museum. Its local rules can work individually while making a collective impasse. Artist's account of Ritual.

Katie Torn — a body made from attachments. Torn describes digital assemblages made from the remains of consumer and internet culture. That gives Ada a way to think about the sphere's growing inventory: added parts carry histories as well as uses. Our assemblage comic extends this into a physical question about weight, leverage, contact and wind. That extension is ours; her artist statement does not establish that every work is an emergent physics simulation. Artist's statement.

A pause before correction

Keats's December 1817 letter describes remaining in “uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact & reason.” He was writing about literary capacity, with Shakespeare as an example. Applying this to simulation is our interpretation: stay with an encounter long enough to notice what a quick explanation or reset would erase. It does not require abandoning causal investigation. Letter and transcription history, University of Victoria.

The risk in a simplifying interface is that it can hide the difference between a request and its consequences. “Make it work” may remove the very relations we need to read. A useful interface could instead let us alternate between acting, watching and inspecting. It can make the unexpected approachable without making the world obedient to every gesture.

Chaplin's feeding-machine sequence in Modern Times is a useful companion: the apparatus's promise of assistance becomes bodily inconvenience and comedy. This is a staged film situation, not evidence of emergent computation. Official Chaplin film excerpts.

Your Half-Life scientist reference supplies another dramatic figure: the participant whose experiment exceeds its brief. We should retain that association while distinguishing an authored catastrophe from an outcome produced during play. The comparison concerns the experience of lost mastery; it does not by itself establish how a particular game scene was implemented.

Tinguely offers a further material precedent. Homage to New York (1960) assembled discarded machinery into an event whose self-destruction did not fully follow its plan. The museum's account makes the divergence part of the work's history. Ada could learn from that gap without making catastrophe the compulsory ending of every invention. Museum Tinguely.

What this changes in Forces

Keep the small, intelligible force lesson. Then let it meet one more body or constraint. A student who has learned a spring can inspect a clumsy helper; one who knows contact can read a blockage; one who knows force direction can see why an attachment turns the object. We do not need to teach the entire engine to make a consequence legible.

Give each encounter two moments: make a prediction, then stay with the event. Ask what continued after the input stopped, which other body joined the action, and what the student chose to preserve. The test should check the rule the tutorial promises, not demand one exact comic ending.

For a later implementation, record starting conditions, parameter changes and input timing. Compare replays as closely as the engine permits. A stable interaction producing an awkward pile, a misconfigured collider and a numerical instability are different findings. They can all be interesting, but the text should name which one was observed. These illustrations establish none of them as runtime facts.

The queer opening can be small and concrete: a body fails its assigned role and becomes useful in another relation. The sphere designated as an enemy might support a crossing; two bodies obstructing each other might need another way to approach. The question is who names the failure and who is required to adapt. Strangeness by itself is not a political achievement.

A possible reflective passage

You wind the spring to move the point. The trolley rolls beneath it. The cloth catches a corner. For a moment, the demonstration has become a small, awkward event that nobody meant to make. You can explain the spring without explaining away the pause. The machine has followed its relations more faithfully than it has followed your intention. Before you reset it, watch what it leaves beside your feet.
