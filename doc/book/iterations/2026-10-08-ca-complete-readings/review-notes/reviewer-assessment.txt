# Cellular Automata — Assessment of the complete eight-hall sequence

Ada Research · Tutorial and reflective sequence · 8 October 2026

The new package contains all eight tutorials and their corresponding reflections, plus screenshots and validation evidence for each hall.&#x20;



My judgment is that Cellular Automata is the most coherent learning progression of the three sequences I've examined: Forces, Fractals, and CA. Its conceptual ambition is comparable, but its educational structure is particularly disciplined.

The sequence develops one question through increasingly complex forms: How does a local rule become a collective phenomenon, and how do we know what we are seeing?

The progression is compelling because each lesson adds a new problem without discarding the operations established before it.&#x20;



## 2. Three particularly strong pedagogical decisions

First, you separate state from its appearance. A cell can hold a value that is not directly visible; an occupied address is not the same as a moving object; a recorded history is not the present state. These distinctions recur in increasingly sophisticated forms.

Second, bodily participation changes meaning across the sequence. In the early halls, the black point reads a cell or advances time. Later, its position becomes an intervention. In Expanding Space, the visitor's location participates in changing the surface that supports them.

Third, the final halls introduce comparative experimental reasoning. Soft Rules compares reaction with and without neighbourhood exchange. Agents/Circuits compares different generating mechanisms. Edge of Chaos isolates one changed initial value. Students increasingly learn to ask which operation caused a difference, rather than merely describing what appeared.

This third quality makes CA especially strong as a research curriculum.

## 3. The strongest lesson may be Expanding Space, not Edge of Chaos

[academy.nightcity.games](https://academy.nightcity.games/category/tutorial/immersive-town/)

In The surface includes you, the student's relationship to the automaton changes fundamentally.

In the previous lesson, a pattern moves across fixed addresses. Now the visitor's position can influence the condition of the cells, and the cells can determine whether a tile remains physically supportive.

This creates a feedback relationship:

The body becomes part of the conditions that change its environment. This is an important development of the research methodology, and potentially more interesting artistically than the mathematical complexity of the automaton itself.

There is an opportunity to push this further: allowing a visitor to discover that an intervention has altered the route they expected to take, and then having to respond to that consequence.

## 4. My most important criticism: the experiments remain unusually controlled

You have carefully designed the eight lessons to isolate particular mechanisms.

That is pedagogically effective. But CA is a domain in which students could also encounter behaviours that are not easily understood through a single-variable comparison.

There is a tension between two objectives:

Scientific legibility: Change one variable, keep the others constant, measure the result.

Artistic experimentation: Combine mechanisms, allow unexpected consequences and investigate whatever becomes interesting.

The sequence is currently stronger in the first.

The rear-gallery artifacts, unusual rule variations and open-ended physical encounters partly address this, but the central tutorial path still emphasizes predetermined comparisons.

I would not remove that discipline. Instead, I would make room for students to move between the two modes of investigation.

The question is not always What happened when I changed X? It can also be What is happening here that I did not know to ask about?

## 5. The second criticism: the middle of the sequence risks becoming too explanatory

The eight reflections are carefully written, and their technical precision is unusually high.

But some passages carry a large explanatory burden. The text identifies an observation, gives its causal account, distinguishes its representation from the underlying state, explains the implementation's limitations and provides the next comparison.

The risk is not primarily that students cannot understand the material. At this stage they have substantial experience.

Rather, it is that the prose sometimes anticipates nearly every interesting conclusion.

The best moments are shorter and more observational:

> More places carry it. Less of it remains at any one place.

Or the distinction between a fixed cell address and a pattern that appears to travel through it.

These moments allow the underlying mechanism to be discovered before its significance is fully articulated.

I would prefer less interpretation at the precise moment when the physical encounter is most surprising, while retaining the detailed explanation elsewhere in the tutorial.

## 6. The third criticism: the finale is deliberately narrower than the sequence's title

I maintain my earlier assessment of Edge of Chaos.

The final experiment is excellent for understanding the propagation and attenuation of differences, the effects of finite storage and the distinction between numerical state and visual amplification.

But neighbour averaging is not a convincing demonstration of the transition between ordered and chaotic cellular dynamics.

The new reflection acknowledges this clearly. That intellectual restraint is preferable to claiming a result the experiment does not establish.

Nevertheless, if Edge of Chaos is intended to be the conceptual culmination of all eight halls, I would consider an optional comparison using rules that exhibit qualitatively different regimes: fixed or periodic behaviour, propagating structures, and more irregular dynamics.

This would allow the student to investigate whether certain systems can maintain and transform information rather than merely spread and attenuate differences.

## 7. Comparison with Forces and Fractals

| Dimension                         | Forces    | CA        | Fractals  |
| --------------------------------- | --------- | --------- | --------- |
| Experiential variety              | Excellent | Very good | Very good |
| Cumulative instructional design   | Good      | Excellent | Excellent |
| Isolation of causal mechanisms    | Good      | Excellent | Very good |
| Open-ended consequences           | Excellent | Moderate  | Moderate  |
| Physical participation            | Excellent | Excellent | Very good |
| Connection of code and reflection | Uneven    | Excellent | Very good |
| Explicit transfer between lessons | Good      | Excellent | Excellent |

These remain editorial assessments; I have not observed learners using the complete sequence.

What distinguishes CA is that the sequence is not only teaching students how to generate patterns. It progressively develops the capacity to interrogate the conditions of an observation.

The student learns that a pattern might not move even though it appears to move; a difference might not disappear even though it becomes invisible; two similar images might result from different operations.

That is a substantial form of computational literacy.

## Final judgment

I would preserve the eight-hall structure and most of the writing.

The sequence succeeds at something I think is central to Ada Research: it transforms computation from an invisible explanation of what appears on screen into something students can investigate through space, action, measurement and comparison.

My one major recommendation would be to introduce an optional experimental synthesis that connects the cellular rules to an earlier system, just as the revised Fractals sequence now begins to do.

For example, a cellular field could control where a structure is physically supported, while the resulting change in geometry influences where the visitor can move and therefore which cells are affected next.

That would combine CA, physics, procedural construction and embodied participation without sacrificing the clarity of the eight existing lessons.

The larger curriculum is becoming interesting for a reason that goes beyond accumulating technical concepts:

Students are learning not simply to make increasingly complex worlds, but to understand why worlds behave differently depending on how they have been constructed, how they are observed, and which bodies are permitted to participate in them.

That is a strong foundation for both an educational project and an artistic research methodology.
