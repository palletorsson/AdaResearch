Same rate. Different time.

Look first. Then change the right rate, let the difference grow, and bring the rates together again. ALIGN is a separate action.

The controls in the hall

DRIFT RIGHT · SAME RATE · OFFSET +45° · ALIGN · HOLD / RUN · READING · RESET

Two pale faces touch at their dark rims. Each carries a hand with a pink tip. Watch one pass the top, then watch the other. For a while, either could have told you where to look.

Press DRIFT RIGHT. At first the difference is small enough to miss. Stay for another turn. The right hand has gained something it keeps gaining.

Choose SAME RATE. The hands still disagree. They will carry this distance together for as long as the rule holds. Matching their speed has not recovered the time between them.

ALIGN brings the right hand to the left. If you leave the rates different, the agreement immediately begins to go. Try aligning once without first making the rates equal. How long do you need to watch before the difference belongs to what you see?

Gonzalez-Torres's paired clocks make shared time available through two ordinary objects. MoMA places the work in the context of his partner's AIDS diagnosis.1 Here, both hands receive the same pulse from the game loop. Their disagreement has to be written in. Neither battery will fail; the program has given us an unusually easy power to bring them together again.

We can keep that power visible without pretending it is something we could have offered the lovers. The two rims remain in contact throughout.

  1. MoMA, Felix Gonzalez-Torres, “Untitled” (Perfect Lovers), 1991. Ada’s accelerated dials share one engine clock. Their programmed rate difference is a comparison, not a simulation of illness, battery failure or the historical work. ↩

The reference and the limits of this comparison

Whitney: Felix Gonzalez-Torres. The Whitney research led to this artist. This particular paired-clock reference is not presented as a Whitney collection work.

Foundation: “Untitled” (Perfect Lovers), 1991. The Foundation records two wall clocks and wall paint, ideally installed above head height. Ada uses enlarged single-hand dials and explicit rate controls; it is an original application, not an authorized installation or reconstruction of that work.

MoMA: “Untitled” (Perfect Lovers), 1991. MoMA describes initially synchronized battery-powered clocks, possible drift or stoppage, resetting, and the context of the artist’s partner being diagnosed with AIDS. The digital study models none of that bodily or material vulnerability.

The clocks have been replaced by enlarged single-hand dials making a turn in twelve seconds. They share one engine update. The controls expose a chosen rate difference; this is neither hardware drift nor the passage of a life.
