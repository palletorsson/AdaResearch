# What the gauge forgets

In the preceding hall, familiar objects separated several questions carried by the word entropy. Here we can change the objects and watch what their readings fail to register. Two neighbouring colours give us something small to keep in sight.

Two hundred coloured tiles stand on a desk beneath a glowing number. Ten bars collect their occurrences, wherever they fell. The bars keep how often; the row keeps where.

The first forty tiles also appear enlarged across the desk's front, labelled “as drawn.” We keep one pair in view and press SORT. Above it, the tiles glide into ten blocks. The bars stay. The number stays. Our pair remains together in the enlarged excerpt while its members acquire different neighbours above.

The plate prints the sorted copy's H beside the original reading: *equal*. Yet finding every occurrence of a colour has become easier. Pressing SORT again restores its former neighbours. The gauge cannot choose between these uses.

The desk uses the colour-frequency question from the chair hall. Its **Shannon entropy** does not ask which tile followed which. A rhythm could pass unremarked. Shannon's theory also has ways of asking about sequences; this instrument has chosen a narrower question.

CONTRAST changes the sample. One colour now occupies much of the row and H falls. The shares changed; no tiles disappeared. Even the bars require a closer look: a tiny visible mark can stand for zero, and the tallest bar reaches the same height in both samples because the display fits its ruler to the largest count.[^ledger]

DISCLOSE reveals the formula and source on the upper panel, then withholds some of its workings. Below, the desk still carries the tiles. Information absent from one display remains within reach on another.

Beyond the desk, four jars rise 2.3 metres from a shared pale plinth. Lavender and coral beads begin with layers, an overlapping interface, mixed colours, and a lavender core surrounded by coral. For eight seconds they hold. One reading asks about colour given a height band, H(C|Y); the vertical bar means “given.” The other asks about colour given core or rim. In the last jar, knowing height leaves colour uncertain; knowing core or rim determines it. The readings disagree before anything moves.

They begin to mix slowly. Lavender crosses the first column's middle; the last column's coral enters its core. One motion rule, four beginnings. After a while, the names on the plinth describe a past that is harder to find in the beads. Forty of each colour remain in each. Their positions continue changing even when the columns look similarly mixed.

PAUSE holds an arrangement for both readings. RESTART brings back the beginnings and their seeded paths. Clusters can return; readings need not rise continually. We can lose sight of a distinction before it has vanished from a record. How much difference is enough to recognise where a column began?

Beside the plinth, a glass jar holds red beads below and blue ones above. Its reading uses six horizontal bands. Here we can change the motion ourselves.

We shake it. Red and blue enter the same bands; their weighted colour reading changes. Counting the whole jar would still give forty red and forty blue, one bit.

This reading does not measure the heat of shaking. RESET restores the specimen. That intervention, too, belongs to the apparatus. We have changed the positions inside a partition. How many arrangements could its record describe?

A narrow tube delivers four cubes onto a pale table: pink, black, pink, black. Then it stops. Six little pictures show how two of each colour can occupy the four places. We add a divider through the middle without moving anything. Four pictures remain. Each now agrees with a more particular record: one pink and one black in each box.

Exchanging the middle cubes gathers pink on the left and black on the right. Only one picture fits. The four objects have survived every change. A partition made their whereabouts matter; sorting changed what that partition could report. With these few objects, we can inspect every possibility before accepting a number.

<!-- @entropy_compartments -->

The next instrument expands the tray: two glass-sided compartments on a dark steel cabinet, eight places in each. Orange A spheres glow on the left; blue B spheres fill the right. Above them, two round dials face the same arrangement. The left says one bit. The right says zero.

The left counts colours across the whole table. The right counts how many arrangements would fit a shorter description: **k**, the number of A spheres on the left. A full list of sixteen occupants is a microstate here; k names its macrostate, a description shared by several possible lists.

At k = 8, only one list fits. We press A LEFT −. One orange sphere exchanges places with a blue one. After four exchanges each tray holds four of each colour. Now 4,900 lists would satisfy the same description. The colour reading has not moved.

**Boltzmann entropy**, S_B = k_B ln Ω, uses the number Ω of compatible microstates. The table shows S_B divided by Boltzmann's constant k_B: zero for one arrangement, about 8.497 for 4,900. This is a small configuration model, with one occupant per site and a fixed inventory. It has no temperature or heat exchange. Its two gauges ask about different things; their disagreement does not make either calculation false.

One more exchange takes us past the balance. The right reading falls. SAME k then moves the colours within their trays and leaves both readings untouched. A difference remains in front of us that neither number can restore.

RUN chooses pairs with a seeded generator. Some swaps exchange equal colours and leave no visible difference. The recorded trial also includes falls in the configuration reading. A finite sequence does not have to climb at every step. KEEP preserves the site lists and generator states; REPLAY reads those lists back. Retaining only the two gauges would not have been enough.

Beyond the table, a square glass enclosure holds points. At the blue end they sit in rows; farther along they stray from their lattice addresses. Walking sideways changes what lines up. The same field looks ordered from one position and tangled from another.

Its random offset grows along z. Even this apparent loosening has a direction. No reading here counts the configurations compatible with a macrostate. The displaced points offer another kind of observation: what looks irregular, from where, and at what scale?

<!-- @entropy_ruin -->

Four stone columns carry a lintel. A lower brick wall runs behind them. ONE STONE draws a supporting drum out towards us and releases it. Above the gap, another drum drops. Others tip and strike the bed. We asked for one stone to move; the contacts decide how far that request travels.

The counter distinguishes stones pulled from stones moved. The inventory remains 116. Taking a loose drum in hand makes the same intervention directly. The stone can leave its old place without leaving the room. Walking around the surviving columns, we find that a former boundary now permits a view across it.

The bottom two courses are fixed; the other stones have weight and colliding surfaces. Gravity continues when automatic selection pauses. RESTORE rebuilds the authored arrangement and restarts the selection seed, but the seed alone is no longer a record of where each stone lands. This is a rigid-body model of loose masonry, with no calculation of thermodynamic entropy.

Beside the columns, a section of the museum wall follows a different procedure: its top pieces leave for arranged landing places. Through its thinning edge, the view continues beyond the room. The floor stays and a low course keeps the boundary. Here the fall has been composed; at the columns, one removal leaves a consequence for contact and gravity to resolve.

RESTORE closes the openings with stones and collision. An arrangement decides where a body can go. The unchanged inventory could never tell us what still holds, what becomes visible, or which passage might open.

Through the doorway after the ruin, the count returns as a room. Black and white continue from the floor onto the far wall and overhead. ROWS becomes CELLS. Both counts stay at 2,048.

A small four-by-four witness lets us inspect the record at another scale. H stays at one bit per symbol while the number of unlike neighbours changes. In this response to Ikeda, immensity is made from a finite stored array. The dark pixels never remove the floor; both sides remain ways out.

We have kept colours, changed neighbours, counted compatible arrangements and watched an opening appear without losing a stone. Each account made a difference available and left others aside. The next experiment could change a partition or preserve a longer history. It might also ask what the instrument has not allowed to happen. In the next hall, the eligible set is laid out where we can count it. Before anything is removed at random, a rule has decided what can be chosen.

[^ledger]: Ten equally likely symbols give a ceiling of log₂(10), about 3.32 bits per symbol. Finite observed shares need not be equal. CONTRAST selects a cached, separately seeded sample from a law that favours early symbols; sorting changes only a display copy. Bars have a two-millimetre minimum and scale to the sample's largest count. DISCLOSE's expected counts describe a law, not promised outcomes.
