Unit Circle — a circle becoming a graph

The point reaches the top and keeps going. Its height begins to fall. Beside it, a path grows across the room: the same rise, the same fall, laid out where you can follow it with your eyes.

After two turns the path stops growing. The point continues. Something has finished being drawn while the movement it describes goes on.

Take the angle control. At the rightmost point, the horizontal offset is one and the vertical offset is zero. At the top they exchange their roles. Another quarter turn makes the horizontal offset negative. There is no privileged beginning on the circle; the rightmost point becomes a beginning because we agree to measure from it.

Two lengths change together. At radius one we call them cosine and sine. Increase the radius and both offsets grow; their ratios to the radius remain the same. The construction is exact even though its visible outline is made from finite pieces.

An angle can be measured by the arc it cuts off divided by the radius. That ratio gives radians. A larger circle has a longer circumference, but one complete turn still has the same angular measure.

The graph makes another agreement. Here one turn occupies six metres along the floor. That distance belongs to this display. It is neither the circle's circumference nor the distance the point has travelled. We have given angle a horizontal address so that height can be read beside it.

Watch the point cross the same height twice: once rising, once falling. A height alone cannot tell you which movement is happening. The other coordinate restores a distinction the single reading lost. What seemed like a complete description was sufficient only for a particular question.

Farther into the hall, separate steps oscillate and another curve keeps a short trail. A drawing can suggest a route. Whether it supports a body is a further decision: these coloured graph pieces are a display, while the floor carries your weight.

The circle gives us a way to calculate a return. The next room asks what happens when a returning body is attached to others. Then its movement must answer to more than its own angle.
