# Sound is an infrastructure — latency, quantisation, and the politics of what can be heard

The waveform on the scope and the sound in the room are not the same thing. The mathematical expression is evaluated at discrete times; the scope draws selected samples with lane-specific gains, while the audio buffer is converted into a physical signal by the playback system. Speakers or headphones and ears take part in an encounter the formula alone does not contain. Between the function and the experience is a full stack of infrastructure, and this hall does not hide it.

Wendy Chun argues that software's power is the power to make infrastructure invisible. When a system works, the learner hears the note; when it fails, they hear nothing, or noise, or a glitch. Chun's insight is that the invisibility is not neutral. It is a political achievement: a claim that the translation from waveform to sound is a trustworthy process, and that the translation's costs can be ignored.

The hall's primary unhides one stretch of that stack. The FM rig on its two desks does not play a recording; a new note is computed when movement passes its trigger conditions or PLAY is pressed; HOLD preserves a rendered reference for replay — 44,100 samples for each second, a decaying sinusoid inside the carrier's phase, an attack, a decay, a tanh that keeps the result within half of full scale — and the low readout prints the time spent generating a new sample buffer. That number measures one stage. Movement detection, scheduling, playback buffering and the listening equipment also contribute to latency. Real-time is a claim, not a property. The scope above the desks selects two carrier periods near the attack: a rescaled modulator, a bare carrier, a rule, and an enlarged output. Its image can hide a change in modulation depth by normalising that lane. Making a cause visible still involves choosing what its picture preserves. The colour cube on the shelf shows only three of the six values as a hue nobody can decode, and the hall says so; it is a sign, not a drawing.

The racks along row five extend the argument the other way, toward a studio's density of gear. The sine rack is the atom; the Moog carves harmonics away until a bass remains; the DX7 lets sines modulate sines into a piano that never had strings; the 303 bends a filter into melody. The audio monitor draws whatever plays; the timbre sculptor stacks integer multiples of one fundamental. That restricts its partials to harmonic relations; it does not decide whether someone will describe the result as bell-like. A mapping can discard a difference, preserve it, or generate further components through a nonlinear operation. We need to inspect which operation this instrument performs before assigning every change the same story of loss.

The audition panel is the hall's politics of listening. HOLD keeps the synthesised note itself, not a memory of it; COMPARE replays it beside the note you just made; the readout lists values that differ beyond its reporting tolerances. A preference has to survive hearing the reference again. That is a small discipline, but it is the one that the rest of the chapter's sound rooms assume: fix one thing, change one thing, listen through the tail.

Within the sequence, Effect Sound is the pivot from visible oscillation to audible oscillation. The rooms before treated the wave as something to see or walk; this one treats it as something to hear, and shows the machinery that hearing depends on. AirMusic follows on the active route with a struck resonance; Bernini's twisted columns and Cage's silence lie further along, and all of them depend on the infrastructure this hall makes visible. The learner leaves knowing that hearing is as constructed as seeing, and that the construction is always political.
