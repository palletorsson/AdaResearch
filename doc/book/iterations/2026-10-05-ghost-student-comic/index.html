The student who hasn't arrived

Eight-panel comic synopsis · Endless Museum · 5 October 2026

An imagined student walks through a world whose code can already run. Their teacher is also a figure of anticipation: the author preparing for a role whose conditions are changing. The student's needs are assumed; the sphere's construction and behaviour can be tested. Neither person is reducible to that test.

The student wears rose; the teacher wears blue. Faint doubled contours give them an unsettled presence. The small white sphere has weight of line, a definite surface and a consistent size. It is the same point carried through the teaching sequence. A large, polished black sphere awaits the student at the end: the object of the future. The characters are fictional figures, not portraits or recorded participants.

The revised rooms follow the museum's straight off-white partitions, rectangular passages, terrazzo floor and simple plinths. Point One begins with three podiums: an empty position, a visible white point, then a matching physical point. Each room bears the same spaced serif wall title, ENDLESS MUSEUM, with always another room underneath.

The sequence dramatizes an actual editorial correction: stop rebuilding the sphere in each lesson, and test what must be added to carry the student's earlier work forward. The code in panel 5 is real. The native result in panel 6 belongs to the bounded continuation pilot, not a human learning trial. The incoming file is an explicit reconstruction from Point One's lesson and source. It is not a recovered student's program.

The comic treats the point as one continuing work. The pilot checks preserved geometry, placement, material and node/resource identity through the interaction; it does not establish persistence of one live instance across all museum halls or sessions.

The teacher's uncertainty concerns the value and direction of their continuing labour. The comic leaves open what the student will need, what the teacher will become, and what learning programming will mean when they meet.

A future already making work

Companion to the comic synopsis · 5 October 2026

Before a student arrives, a hand reaches for the point in our planning. We decide what that hand should already know, which lines of code its owner should carry forward, and where another explanation becomes necessary. This ghost student has practical effects: a repeated construction leaves the lesson; a saved incoming program takes its place; an omitted connection becomes a test.

The ghost teacher concerns another absence. The author is preparing to teach while anticipating a changed relation to their own knowledge and labour. Being able to generate more code does not settle what deserves teaching. It makes the acts of selecting, explaining and judging more visible in this particular project. The teacher keeps working without knowing exactly who will need that work or what form the encounter will take.

We use anticipatory hauntology as a description of this experience, following the project's existing Folding future note and preface. It is an interpretation of Ada's practice, not a forecast that students or teachers will disappear. Derrida's publicly accessible Spectres of Marx excerpt attends to inheritance and the need to remain answerable to changes in knowledge, technique and politics that cannot be programmed in advance. That offers a connection for our reading; it does not establish Ada's specific account of AI or teaching. Jacques Derrida, “Spectres of Marx,” New Left Review I/205 (1994).

Jo Albin-Clark's 2024 conference abstract describes imagined dialogues with data-ghosts through 3D digital artworks, asking how absence and possible futures trouble ethical responsibility and educational research. It provides a relevant research-creation precedent. The accessible source is an abstract; it is not evidence that our student model predicts learning. “Data-ghost dialogues,” Edge Hill University.

The evidence here lies in decisions we can inspect. The continuation pilot mechanically applies three printed edits to an assumed incoming program. Its 62 native checks pass, and deliberately omitting the reparent operation makes the surface fail to follow the moving boundary. Anticipating a student's difficulty changed what the lesson keeps visible. These checks establish a bounded software result. Whether a person can explain it, question it or use it for another purpose remains open.

The same caution applies to the image. These are drawn possible participants, not footage of a student or teacher. A stable digital object makes it possible to inspect continuity while the relationship around it changes. Stability also has a scope: we test one construction and interaction, not an eternally identical object or an entire course.

In the museum revision, the author's large glossy black sphere becomes the object of the future awaiting the student. Its reflections bring the surrounding room onto its surface. The small white point remains the continuing exercise object; the black sphere extends the comic's imagined encounter. The three entrance podiums and the repeated wall title, with “always another room” beneath it, tie that encounter more closely to the museum.

For QFEP, the comic holds onto the user's question: what capacities should this world preserve when the relationship it was built to support is itself changing? A next version might follow a student who refuses the expected exercise and makes another use of the point. The ghosts should leave room for that person to disagree.
