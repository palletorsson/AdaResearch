# The folding future

I began from the programming courses I had taught at university. I wanted to help artists learn small operations, combine them, and make visual work of their own. The course would give them a route from understanding a few lines of code to building a world.

While I was preparing that route, my own way of making changed. I wrote instructions to an AI and received code, objects and rooms. I was no longer programming much myself. A working artifact could arrive before I understood how to make it. I still needed to inspect what it did, change its conditions and decide whether it belonged in the work. But the order had shifted: I could begin with a result and work back towards its construction. If a student could arrive by a similar route, what would they need from the course I was writing?

At this stage, I realised I was building for a ghost student. I had imagined someone sitting before the work, learning to program through the sequence I was preparing. Now that encounter began to feel as though it might never happen in the form I expected. I imagined myself as a ghost teacher too, preparing to pass on knowledge while my relationship to that knowledge and labour was changing. The tools helping me finish the course were also unsettling its premise.

One practical response was to ask what must remain understandable between two lessons. In Point One, a sphere acquires a surface and a physical boundary. In the next hall, the hand can take it. We kept an assumed version of the student's code and tested whether the new instructions were enough to make that same sphere move with the hand. The test can expose a missing connection. Whether someone understands it remains an encounter to come.

I call this the folding future. An expected change reaches back into what we explain, delegate and preserve today. We condense instructions for another AI reader and retain the code behind them, so a later reading can reopen a decision. The intended recipient is moving while we are constructing the address. Hauntology helps me attend to this presence of those who are not yet here.[^preface-folding-future]

The ghost student can also become too convenient, learning in precisely the order I prefer. A person who hesitates, disagrees, or wants to make something else must be able to change that order. Preparing for them means leaving something they can answer back to.

[^preface-folding-future]: Jacques Derrida, *Specters of Marx* (French 1993; English translation by Peggy Kamuf, 1994), “Exordium,” relates responsibility to those no longer present and those not yet here; see the [publisher's preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781040559963_A63760427/preview-9781040559963_A63760427.pdf), pp. xxiii–xxiv, and [“Spectres of Marx,” *New Left Review* I/205 (1994)](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i205/articles/jacques-derrida-spectres-of-marx). *Anticipatory hauntology* describes Ada's emphasis here; it is not presented as Derrida's own term or a prediction that teachers or students will disappear. A related educational research-creation approach appears in Jo Albin-Clark's [“Data-ghost dialogues”](https://research.edgehill.ac.uk/en/publications/data-ghost-dialogues-playing-and-questioning-future-directions-wi/) (2024). The available conference abstract describes 3D digital artworks and imagined dialogues that question absence, ethical responsibility and future research directions. Ada's ghost student and ghost teacher are our interpretation of this project's changing pedagogical relationship, not the same apparatus as Albin-Clark's data-ghosts.
