### From tutorial to physical story

At the moment many entries still read primarily as good demonstrations:

> adjust parameter → observe physical principle → understand concept.

I think they should increasingly operate as:

**promise → prediction → release → reveal → residue**

The crucial moment is **release**. Before release, the player is the scientist and believes they control the apparatus. After release, the physics engine owns part of the event.

For example, your spring encounter already contains this beautifully. The point returns toward equilibrium, but because restoring force does not erase velocity it passes through the resting position. Pasted text That is not merely an explanation of Hooke's law. It is a tiny story:

> *I pulled it here. I expected it to return there. I let go. It came back—and continued.*

The second spring then makes the event social: one body answers another, and a tiny difference in spacing can create a miss, an additional collision, or a different return. Pasted text

That is already your **mad scientist** structure.

## The strongest existing scenes

I think there are about ten encounters in this proposal that should become the spectacular physical spine of the sequence.

**The launch.** You predict a trajectory toward a cup. The satisfying outcome is perfect arrival; the equally satisfying failure is hitting the intervening ring and watching a perfectly reasonable prediction become invalid because the conditions changed. Pasted text

**The jump pads.** Now the player's own body becomes the projectile. The interesting failure isn't missing the destination—it is reaching the correct destination with too much velocity and continuing past it. Pasted text That is a wonderful physical joke.

**The drain/orbit.** The apparatus promises collection. Add tangential velocity and the “drain” suddenly produces orbit or congestion instead of arrival. Pasted text This is almost a cybernetic parable: the regulator tries to gather and accidentally generates a new stable behavior.

**The friction tray.** The object refuses to move, then suddenly crosses the static threshold and travels farther than intended. Pasted text There is a natural comic beat there: *nothing, nothing, nothing—GO.*

**The transparent pipe.** This may be one of the clearest YouTube-short-like setups. The viewer sees the curve and instinctively extrapolates it. Then the ball exits and its tangent plus gravity determines the actual trajectory. Pasted text The reveal is visual and requires almost no explanation.

**The support experiment.** Remove a leg and the object falls, but the fallen object may become a ramp or obstruction. Pasted text This moves beyond failure: the failure produces a new affordance.

### The deeper structure

I would make the halls slowly move through three attitudes toward physics:

**1. “I can describe it.”**  
Vectors, projection, prediction.

**2. “I can control it.”**  
Launch, thrust, friction, springs, attraction.

**3. “I have changed the world and now have to live with it.”**  
collision, accumulation, loss of support, backlog, displaced obstacles, aftermath.

Your document already moves approximately this way—describe → motion → room → propagation → other bodies → composition. Pasted text I would make that conceptual transformation much more explicit in the spatial design.

By the final halls the player should no longer be standing in front of pristine resettable demonstrations. They should be walking through a laboratory containing the **history of previous experiments**.

That is where the artifacts become important.

### Let each event manufacture an artifact

The conveyor produces a **jam pile**.

The spring collision produces a **tangled double oscillator**.

The pole leaves a **bridge**, or a pole wedged uselessly against the architecture.

The friction experiment leaves a **track** across a surface.

The attractor leaves a **cluster of things that were never meant to belong together**.

The support experiment leaves a **fallen body that has become architecture**.

The launch leaves an object lodged somewhere unreachable.

The bowling/impulse experiment leaves a rearranged field of obstacles.

These do not need to be formally awarded as game inventory. They could simply persist as **physical evidence**.

## Precision and failure are not opposites

One very important thing in your proposal is the warning not to use numerical instability, tunnelling, broken joints, or fake deformation as evidence of the forces themselves. Pasted text I agree strongly.

The interesting “failure” is usually **not the engine malfunctioning**.

It is:

- the correctly simulated box that jams another box;
- the accurately simulated pole that knocks away its own support;
- the spring that legitimately overshoots;
- the perfectly functioning attractor that attracts the wrong thing;
- the conveyor that correctly preserves a backlog;
- the player who correctly lands but cannot stop.

That gives you the formulation from our earlier discussion in a technically rigorous way:

> **Everything worked. That is why this happened.**

That is much stranger than a glitch.

### The YouTube-short grammar

I think every major physical encounter could therefore be designed as a 5–20 second **micro-narrative that would work even if somebody watched it with no explanation**:

**1. Establish the apparatus.**  
Ball, cup, pipe.

**2. Make its intended logic obvious.**  
Surely the ball follows the tube into the cup.

**3. Give one controllable intervention.**  
Angle / velocity / spring extension / timing.

**4. Release control.**  
The simulation runs.

**5. Reveal.**  
Perfect fit, near miss, chain reaction, overshoot, jam.

Then give the player control back.

That last point is crucial. During the reveal, perhaps the player often **cannot intervene**. For two or three seconds they simply have to watch what they started.

That is where Keats enters mechanically: negative capability not as wall text, but as an interaction pattern.

### And it gives the scientist a character arc

At first:

> **The scientist measures the world.**

Then:

> **The scientist applies forces to the world.**

Then:

> **The scientist discovers that other bodies answer.**

Finally:

> **The scientist works with what happened.**

The last phrase may actually be the conceptual endpoint of the whole sequence.

Not **control the world**.

Not even **understand the world**.

**Work with what happened.**

That is what makes the final proposal particularly strong: the missed delivery can leave behind something useful for the next attempt. Pasted text At that point failure stops being the opposite of knowledge. It becomes part of the player's repertoire.

And that is very close to the larger idea of *coding in deviation*: **the system does not become good by preventing every unintended state; it becomes interesting when it can continue from states it did not plan.**
