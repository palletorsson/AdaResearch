Latour’s point in “Why Has Critique Run out of Steam? From Matters of Fact to Matters of Concern” (2004) was not that critique had become unnecessary. It was that a particular style of critique had become politically and intellectually exhausted.
The style he was worried about was the familiar critical move of debunking:
“You think X is real, neutral, natural, objective — but actually X is constructed by interests, ideology, institutions, discourse, power, etc.”
For decades, this was a powerful operation. Critical theory, sociology of science, poststructuralism and related traditions had shown that apparently self-evident facts were produced through institutions, instruments, classifications and social arrangements.
Latour's alarm was that these techniques had become too successful. The same rhetorical machinery could now be used by climate-change deniers, conspiracy theorists and political propagandists:
Scientists tell you this is a fact?
Don't be naïve. Who funded them? What interests are behind it? Isn't this just a construction?
So critique had helped create a situation in which simply saying “facts are constructed” no longer necessarily produced emancipation. It could just as easily produce generalized suspicion.
His argument is therefore approximately:
Critique became very good at taking things apart, but not sufficiently good at helping us understand what should be assembled, protected, maintained, or cared for.
That is why he proposes moving from “matters of fact” to “matters of concern.”
A matter of fact presents itself as a finished object:
AI produces this result.
A matter of concern asks you to follow everything necessary for that result to exist:
training data
GPU manufacture
research institutions
electricity
human annotation
benchmarks
corporate incentives
interfaces
copyright law
users
values
classification systems
etc.
The critic therefore changes role.
The critic is no longer primarily someone who says:
“Aha! What you thought was objective is actually constructed.”
Instead, the critic becomes someone who asks:
How is this thing assembled? What holds it together? What does it depend upon? Who and what participates in it? What should we preserve, alter, or oppose?
That is very close to your problem with AI, although it isn't exactly the same argument.
Your “best-before date of critique” adds a temporal dimension that Latour wasn't primarily discussing.
Consider:
“AI can't write convincing literature.”
That can be a perfectly accurate criticism in 2023.
Six months later, perhaps it is much weaker.
“AI can't reason.”
Same problem.
“AI doesn't understand images.”
Same problem.
These are critiques whose force depends upon a temporary technical state of the object.
You could therefore extend Latour:
From matters of failure to matters of concern
A critique based on:
what the model currently fails to do
can disappear when the model improves.
A critique concerned with:
what assemblage makes the model possible, what it optimizes, what relations it creates, what it makes visible or invisible, and what institutions form around it
doesn't disappear when GPT-9 arrives.
In fact, improved capability can make those questions more urgent.
Take concentration of power.
If AI becomes useless, concentration of AI ownership is not particularly consequential.
If AI becomes extraordinarily capable, concentration of ownership becomes more consequential.
That is a critique with an interesting property:
Its force increases as the technology improves.
This gives you a potentially very useful distinction for the NAVET project:
Fragile critique
Durable critique
AI cannot do X
What happens when AI can do X?
Output is bad
What determines what is produced?
AI isn't creative
How is creativity being reorganized?
AI has no human body
What material assemblage constitutes AI?
Model is biased
What systems determine categories, relevance and optimization?
AI makes mistakes
Who has authority when automated systems become reliable?
AI threatens artists because it makes bad art
What happens to artistic labour when it makes excellent art?

And this gets very close to Latour's most interesting provocation: don't make the object disappear by explaining it away.
Classic critique can sometimes say:
AI is really capitalism.
AI is really extractivism.
AI is really statistical prediction.
AI is really corporate ideology.
Latour would probably tell us: No. Follow the thing.
The model is simultaneously mathematics, GPUs, culture, language, capital, electricity, research, human labour, probability, institutional aspiration and actual technical behaviour.
Don't reduce it to one hidden explanation.
That connects strongly with what you have been calling non-essentialism.
Instead of asking:
“What is AI really?”
you can ask:
“What relations allow this strange thing to exist, and what new relations does its existence produce?”
And I think there is an even stronger formulation that combines your argument with Latour:
Critique runs out of steam when it succeeds only by making its object smaller.
The interesting AI critique might instead allow the object to become larger, stranger and more interconnected.
For your presentation, I'd be tempted to put Latour immediately after your question about the half-life of critique:
2004 — Latour:
What happens when critique becomes primarily a machinery for debunking facts?
2026 — Critical AI:
What happens when the object of critique changes faster than critique itself?
Then the methodological proposition:
Don't critique AI by freezing it at its present limitations. Follow the assemblage through its transformations.
That feels extremely close to what you are already trying to articulate.

A useful test for a critique is: would this question still matter if the model became much more capable, reliable and inexpensive?
Questions about who controls the system, whose purposes it serves, who can change its rules, and where its costs fall often survive that test. Greater capability can make them more consequential. A highly accurate system can still pursue a damaging objective; excellent prose can still concentrate authority.
Rapid improvement is well documented, although capabilities develop unevenly. The evidence supports fast change more clearly than a single exponential curve encompassing every capacity. A critique should therefore declare its assumptions and the evidence that would lead it to change. hai.stanford.edu
