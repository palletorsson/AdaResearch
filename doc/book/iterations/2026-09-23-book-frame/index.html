Before the first point

Preface · working draft, revised 29 September 2026

We arrive after the light.

Someone has already taught this machine how to make a point visible. The counter has advanced. We move a hand, and work that took generations answers. A small gesture can contain an immense inheritance.1

We cannot unfold all of it before beginning. We choose one operation, follow it for a while, and leave other doors unopened. What looks like the beginning is the place where we have decided to pay attention.

Even “Hello, world” needs somewhere to happen. Before the first room, we give the work of preparation its own time: Day Zero. Downloading an engine, opening a project, making a change appear—these are part of learning to arrive. We begin with a supplied environment and, in the museum, a player whose construction we have not yet learned. That borrowed work lets us turn our attention towards the point. It also gives the point a world in which to matter.

What bodies are possible with the tools we have? The question follows us through this book. We begin by attending to a position, a line, a few ways to give something a surface. Later we can make a field, a branching structure, a body that responds to its neighbours. Each addition changes what we can imagine building. It also brings decisions we may only notice when something refuses to behave as we expected.

Ada Research is a museum built to investigate those decisions. Its exhibits include the room around them: the floor that carries us, a wall that interrupts a route, the rules through which another body approaches. Learning to construct an object gives us a way to look again at the conditions in which we encounter it. Eventually we can ask the same questions of the museum itself.

I want to know how programming can help us make worlds we do not yet know how to inhabit. There is pleasure in that desire: dressing a body, following an improbable passage, wanting to touch a form before understanding it. There is also work. A shape has to be represented, a movement calculated, a surface made visible. The computer has finite time. So do we. We follow a path because we cannot pursue every possibility at once.

While we learn to use these tools, they are being revised. With AI, I feel the change in what we can attempt together. A question in the manuscript can become code, a room we enter, and a reason to rewrite the question. We can carry an idea through these forms while it is still unsettled.

Work accumulates; what becomes possible can arrive as a leap. A change in how we write code reaches into how we make a room, then into what we can learn by being there. One capacity becomes a tool for another. This spillover is part of the exhilaration: the next opening may change the work we thought we were doing. It also changes what we need to learn. How much of our time can go into catching up? When do we get time to play?

We arrive among systems we did not choose, and find our bearings while their possibilities are changing. I sometimes feel knowledge gathering around us, exerting a new gravitational pull. An answer arrives beside a question, with code we can run and another direction to follow. The ease of following gives that answer weight before we have understood whose assumptions it carries. We need ways to test it, turn it, make something else of it. Learning includes finding a position from which to do that.

Like Alice after the White Rabbit, we want to see what happens next. The reward can be a possibility we could not have imagined asking for. The risk includes the time, energy and labour we commit before we can judge it. Where does that desire become a death drive, an insistence on continuing that makes the cost harder to see? We can ask this from inside the pleasure of making. It is one reason to keep the work open to interruption.

First light, or the light at the end of a tunnel? An opening can draw us forward before we know where it leads. We begin this book in that light.

This book is for artists, students, programmers, and curious readers who want to understand how code gives a world its possibilities. It begins with small operations and carries them forward. A line can become a record of movement; the record can be given another size or another use. The tutorial supplies something to hold on to while the questions change. A short piece of code can make a consequence legible enough for someone else to test, alter, or disagree with it.

Whenever possible, an encounter comes before its explanation. Try something. Look at what happened. Predict what a small change might do. Then open the part of the program that bears on the question. Other code will remain unread for now. Naming what we borrow helps us return to it later; pretending to begin without it would make the first lesson impossible.

The writing moves between the gesture and the instruction, between what happened and what the program can account for. Sometimes an apparent mistake opens a use we had not considered. Sometimes a dependable restriction gives us room to act. We will need both kinds of encounter. There should be opportunities to keep a form we like, repeat an operation, refuse an invitation, or find another way through.

A tool's intended use is one arrangement of its capacities. A microwave enters a makeshift laboratory; a camera sold for games becomes part of a 3D scanner.2 Their earlier lives remain legible in the new work. To tinker is to follow a connection that the product's name did not teach us to expect. The museum asks this of its own tools too: what else can a point follow, a trace carry, a surface become?

Queer desire enters this inquiry through the bodies, pleasures, and relations we want to make possible, and through the expectations we are willing to trouble. Drag, ornament, awkwardness, comedy, and the digital grotesque belong among its materials. A strange appearance can draw us closer. Understanding how it was made gives that interest somewhere further to go. We can ask whose movement a rule accommodates, what it asks a body to become, and who can change the terms.

The book and the museum offer two approaches to this work. In the museum, distance, reach, and duration enter the lesson through your own actions. On the page, code and illustrations let us hold a comparison still and return to it. The written account must give a reader enough to follow the operation away from the headset. A description of an encounter and a person's experience of it can differ; that difference belongs to the continuing research.

Footnotes open further paths into technical and intellectual sources. Follow them when an encounter gives you a reason. The main journey carries its own explanations, while the notes keep room for questions that would otherwise take us away from the object too soon.

Towards the end, the project names a developing framework: the Queer Free Energy Principle, or QFEP. Its central concern here is how models can remain open to revision while the lives and possibilities they leave out retain the means to act and answer back. The afterword returns to this proposal with the encounters behind us. It asks what they let us claim, where they complicate the argument, and what remains to be tried. The reader can reach that discussion with questions of their own.

Our route is one arrangement of attention. Other objects stand beside it; other histories could change its order. What matters is that following it gives us enough understanding to begin making such changes, together or alone.

Day Zero — Hello, world is the practical chapter of this preface. It prepares the working environment once. The making passages that follow assume we can already open, change and run the supplied project.

We have to begin somewhere. The first room is already running.

Day Zero — Hello, world

Part of the preface · preparation before Point Zero

“Hello, world.”

Two words are often offered as the beginning of programming. Before they can appear, a computer must have somewhere to run the instruction. We need the right program, a project it can open, files it can find, a place to see the result. The sentence is small. The arrangement that lets it happen is not.

We give that arrangement a day of its own. Day Zero is time set aside for downloading, configuring, waiting, asking for help and trying again. Some people will need less time; others will need more. Having the tools ready is work, and the book should not quietly ask the first point to carry it.

By the time we enter Point Zero, we have already accepted a considerable inheritance. Someone made the editor. Someone wrote the renderer, the player controller and the code that follows a hand. We can begin using these things before we can explain their construction. “You arrive late” also means this: even our first successful instruction arrives in a world of instructions already at work.

Borrowing gives us time to investigate something smaller. It also gives that investigation conditions. A supplied camera chooses a view. A player controller decides how movement is received. A pickup system determines what can be held and how it follows a hand. We name these parts so that beginning with them does not make them disappear.

Get one project running

For the first exercises, use the desktop base supplied with this book. It lets us check a scene without setting up a headset at the same time. The Quest route below belongs to this preparation too; it adds another device to the arrangement.

The test edition uses Godot 4.6 Standard and GDScript. Keep the version named by the study while following its examples. The Godot 4.6 archive provides the editor for your operating system; these exercises do not require the .NET edition.

Download and extract the Day Zero project. Keep the folder somewhere you can find again, and keep a clean copy before experimenting.

There are two files to recognise:

  • project.godot identifies the project and its settings, including which scene starts.
  • base.tscn holds the prepared scene: camera, light, floor, environment and greeting. This particular scene contains its own resources, so it can also be copied into an existing project.

A scene is part of a project. The small project download supplies both. If you already have a suitable project, you can also take base.tscn by itself.

Open Godot's Project Manager, choose Import, and select the extracted project.godot. Open the project and let its initial import finish. This is the point at which the editor finds and prepares the project's resources. Godot's Project Manager guide covers importing an existing project.

Open base.tscn. Run the project with the editor's Run Project button, or F5 with the default keyboard shortcuts. The room should show “Hello, world.” The Output panel should receive the same words.

Stop the project. Select Welcome in the Scene tree. In the Inspector, change its Text to words of your own. Save the scene and run again. Both the displayed greeting and the Output panel should now contain your words. You have changed something in the saved project and seen that change reach the running scene.

For this project, F5 starts base.tscn because it is the main scene. Run Current Scene, normally F6, starts whichever scene is open. Later studies may have more than one scene. Learn that distinction here, along with stopping and saving, so it can become an ordinary part of working. The Godot first-script lesson is a further introduction to the editor and running code.

Know what has been supplied

The base gives us a lit floor and a fixed view. We have not yet constructed a player. This desktop scene contains no walking, hands or XR add-ons. Point One's studies use the same base.tscn and hide the greeting. They supply small point comparisons, so the lesson can follow the point's changing conditions and then its coordinates.

The full museum supplies much more. In the current course project, project.godot enables the bundled Godot XR Tools 4.5.1 add-on and starts commons/scenes/vr_staging.tscn. The player, movement and pickup systems are working material to inherit. Their construction is not an entrance examination for the point.

Godot's OpenXR support connects the application to an XR runtime. XR Tools supplies reusable interaction components above that connection. Installing a toolkit and designing a player's behavior are different pieces of work. When using the course project, keep its supplied add-ons together rather than replacing them individually while learning. The XR Tools project documents the library and its version compatibility.

The Quest route

Putting the work on a headset is another setup task for Day Zero. Use the full XR course project for this route. Exporting the fixed-camera desktop base by itself does not give it tracked eyes or hands.

First establish that the XR scene responds to head movement and that its intended controls work. The scene needs its XR origin, tracked camera and configured input. The Godot 4.6 XR setup guide explains their relation and the OpenXR settings. We are learning to recognise and test this supplied arrangement before undertaking its construction.

For standalone Quest deployment, follow the versioned Android export setup: install the matching Godot export templates, Java and the Android SDK, then give Godot their locations. The guide lists the required tool versions. These are tools on the development computer; installing them does not alter the lesson's algorithm.

Then follow the XR deployment guide for the Android export preset, OpenXR mode and device connection. Enable the headset's developer access and authorise the connection as its vendor documentation requires. The Godot 4.6 guide treats the OpenXR vendor plug-in as optional but recommended; the course project's selected configuration should be kept together.

A successful export is only one part of this first test. Open the application on the headset, look around, use the intended movement and pick up a supplied test object. Seeing an image proves less than having these relations work. Resolve setup problems here before asking the first hall to explain them.

This chapter provides the setup route; the small download is the desktop base. A standalone Quest starter and a fresh headset deployment have not been verified as part of this edition.

Leave Day Zero with a place to work

You should now be able to locate your project, open the intended scene, change a value, save, run, stop and return to the saved change. If you are following the Quest route, the supplied player should also respond on your device. Keep that working state.

The later making passages assume these actions. They can begin with a point or a rule, and spend their attention on what is new. If a setup problem returns, come back here.

What must a world change so difference can do more than fit?

Afterword · Working draft, revised 28 September 2026. Follows the full spine; development tasks follow the essay and source notes.

Return to the first arrival

The first room was already running. Its counter included process frames from before our arrival, and its apparent beginning depended on work we had not opened. The point gave us a place to begin without making that place the beginning of everything. After the journey through this book, we can return to that modest arrangement with a more demanding question: what did the room require us to become in order to enter it?

The question concerns the maker too. A point, a path, a grid and a classification make certain actions easier to imagine. Repeated use can train attention until their omissions stop appearing as choices. QFEP, the Queer Free Energy Principle, names AdaResearch's inquiry into these conditions of modelling, prediction and persistence. Its ethical proposal is that models and their authority remain revisable while those they leave out retain capacities to live, act and change the terms.1

This commitment cannot be established by an attractive simulation. The chapters give it places to be examined: a hand that arrives, a line that directs without policing, a record that loses a pause. Their limits matter as much as their invitations.

Tolerance needs somewhere to go

In Point One, the target accepts a position within 0.45 metres of its centre. Exact coincidence is unnecessary. Different arrivals can satisfy one test, and a detour can change the rhythm of the falling balls without entering the distance comparison. There is room to arrive, but the centre and the criterion remain authored.2

A numerical tolerance specifies an acceptable deviation for a stated purpose. It helps answer whether this arrival, approximation or result is sufficient. Political tolerance introduces further questions: sufficient for whom, with what support, and with what power to refuse? Increasing a permitted interval supplies no answer by itself. A visitor might be admitted while the controls remain unreachable or the available time makes participation impossible.

Facilitating difference therefore requires more than allowing it to pass a test. It needs conditions in which difference can remain perceptible, develop and affect the encounter. Those conditions may include time, resources, assistance, privacy and authority to change the rule. Sometimes the useful revision will concern the target; sometimes it will concern the assumption that everyone must aim at it.

This distinction also protects technical precision. Mechanical freedom and numerical error are different matters. A joint can permit rotation while a solver accurately maintains its other constraints. Making the calculation less accurate would not demonstrate greater generosity. The task is to identify which restriction matters and what changing it enables.

What a measurement keeps

Trace makes selection visible in a small gesture. Its movement gate can leave a still pause out of the position list. Spatial rounding can send several readings to one retained address. Yet the coarse recorder also offers something to make with: a corner that can be found again. Loss and usefulness belong to the same operation.3

Grid carries the question onward. Shared addresses can help pieces meet and people coordinate. Its plan still needs a supporting surface. A location that can be named has not thereby become a place a body can reach.

Entropy readings make another selection. Shannon entropy concerns a probability distribution over specified alternatives. A symbol-frequency measurement can remain unchanged when the symbols are rearranged. The arrangement may nevertheless matter to someone reading, hearing or using them. Thermodynamic entropy, information entropy and the possibilities of living otherwise require distinct accounts; a shared word does not make them interchangeable.4

An entropic morality therefore cannot be a morality of maximising an entropy display. Its concern is the distribution of possibilities and losses, including those the chosen measure does not register. Better measurement can help. It cannot independently decide what deserves protection.

A tool has other futures

In Trace, a drawing outlives the gesture that made it. Its saved positions enter another program, where they can be enlarged, rounded and offered to another movement. A record made to look back has become something to make with. Its new use begins from what survived the recording.

I think of technology as haunted by its other possible uses. Its name and interface make one task easy to recognise; connections to bodies, materials and other machines continue beyond that task. In tinkering, a different connection becomes workable. The camera need not stop being a camera to become part of a scanner. Its history persists in what the new work can do.10

By entropy as difference, I mean here a concern for possibilities that an arrangement leaves available, including uses it has not named. Some depend on keeping an old connection; some require building a new one. They need time, skills, materials and access to the device. A closed interface can decide which possibilities someone else is allowed to discover.

Ashby's law of requisite variety puts a limit on regulation: the responses available must be adequate to the disturbances relevant to the outcome being maintained.11 Tinkering can enlarge that repertoire, but it can also change what the system is for. Who gets to propose another use, and who inherits its consequences?

The will to spill beyond an assigned use names that unfinished relation between material capacities and our desires. Whether it becomes another way to live or another way to extract depends on the connection we build.

When a description becomes a demand

The F Term room offers a precise reversal. EXPECT 0 can perform badly on the displayed bits; EXPECT 1 can improve the prediction while leaving those bits in place. The model changes to meet what it encounters.5

That small operation clarifies a larger ethical distinction. Simplifying a representation and simplifying the conditions of life to fit it are different acts. An omission becomes materially consequential when an institution uses the account to allocate space, protection or resources. Then the omitted difference can lose the conditions through which it might have challenged the description.

QFEP makes an explicit commitment here: others' continued lives and capacities matter beyond their usefulness to a controller. This does not follow as a theorem from predictive success, intelligence or requisite variety. Those can describe capacities without determining who should have authority over their use.

The project's entropic-morality essay develops mutual aid as one possible practice of sustaining shared capacities. That proposal requires organization, resources and ways of contesting decisions. Merely gathering more perspectives does not redistribute authority. A system may learn from everyone while still allowing only its maker to decide what the learning means.

A boundary can make room

The E Term fan can close branches and reopen the same tree. Its count records the available endings under its assumptions. It cannot decide whether a particular closure prevents harm or destroys a needed possibility. Nor can its restore button recover a history outside the demonstration.5

This is why QFEP cannot prescribe openness everywhere. Shelter, a private conversation and a protected place depend on limiting some access. The capacity to refuse is itself a condition of meaningful participation. Compulsory exposure would turn difference into an obligation to explain oneself to whoever asks.

Needs also conflict. One person's desired noise may prevent another from resting; one person's access may threaten another's refuge. No entropy score settles that conflict. The practical questions concern who bears the cost, who can contest a decision, what alternatives exist and what repair remains possible. Preserving every existing arrangement would preserve arrangements that prevent others from living. Inaction has consequences too.

An answerable boundary needs a purpose that can be examined, people with standing to challenge its effects, and conditions under which it can change. Revisability requires somewhere from which to speak.

Pleasure is part of the work

WALK THIS LINE does not score compliance. Its words can still invite following, hesitation or dancing. The grid's intervals can become a rhythm; a coarse trace can become a drawing one wants to repeat. These possibilities matter because people do more with a structure than satisfy its stated task.6

Desire and aesthetic pleasure belong in the account. A system that permits only justified, productive departures has already narrowed what difference may become. There should be room to linger over a form, make something strange, return to a familiar shape or decline the invitation to transform. Continuity can be wanted; becoming must not become compulsory.

Complexity also costs. More choices can demand attention, more detail can consume computation, and more controls can make an encounter harder to approach. A richer representation is not automatically a more hospitable one. Simplification may release time and capacity for something else.

The relevant question is where the effort goes and who supplies it. Does a new option help someone act, or require them to learn another apparatus before they can participate? Can the maker absorb some of that work? Pleasure is a reason to build, while care asks how its conditions are shared.

Keep the formula answerable

QFEP takes its name from a conversation with the Free Energy Principle. In Friston, Thornton and Clark's account, variational free energy bounds informational surprise relative to a model. Their discussion of the dark-room problem explains why minimizing this quantity need not mean seeking the least stimulating surroundings: what counts as unexpected depends on the organism's expectations and conditions of existence. A familiar, stimulating world can be less surprising to such an organism than an empty room.8

Ada's proposed extension concerns the authority and consequences of modelling. Whose persistence does a model serve, and who can contest the conditions it helps maintain? This ethical question is not resolved by a prediction becoming more accurate. Nor does it require claiming that the Free Energy Principle excludes exploration. The relation needs sustained discussion between the mathematical account, the simulations we build and the commitments through which we judge their consequences.

The project's teaching expression gathers several questions:

QFE = F − λE(S) + φΔE(S,t)

It remains an exploratory construction. A quantitative use must specify states, probabilities, units or normalisation, the interval of comparison, and whether the objective is minimised or maximised. A finite difference becomes a rate only when divided by an interval. Under minimisation, with other terms fixed, positive phi increases the cost of a positive entropy change. Its sign alone cannot establish a welcome for transformation.7

The Introduction console lets different arrangements approach the same total while KEEP preserves their visible distinction. Its supplied change value is fixed. The Sandbox maps received controls to authored reactor responses; Synthesis maps its controls to mechanical properties. These are inspectable implementations with different meanings. They do not measure a universal scale of queerness, and the reactor's preferred lambda interval establishes no universal band for life.

Q need not become one more scalar claiming to capture everything the others missed. It concerns the possibility of challenging the variables, the purpose of the measurement and the authority to act on it. Adjusting a weight can be useful while leaving those larger decisions untouched.

Self-Q reaches the book

Self-Q returns the inquiry to the maker, museum and spine. The sequence that makes learning possible also gives it a direction. Choosing one principal encounter gives a chapter clarity while placing other questions at its edges. This afterword is another such arrangement: it selects examples and asks the reader to recognise them through QFEP.9

The framework must be available for revision too. Evidence that an added choice excludes the people it was meant to support should change the design. An account of care that requires more unpaid work from those already carrying the costs should change the account. If participants need stable constraints where the maker expected liberation through variation, their experience should be able to alter the ambition. These are proposed tests of the argument, not reports of completed studies.

No maker can retain every possibility. The obligation is to make choices whose consequences can be encountered and contested, while protecting capacities for refusal, repair and another attempt. Some losses will be irreversible. Naming that limit should affect the scale and authority of an intervention before the loss occurs.

We return to the point with something more than a larger tolerance. A world that facilitates difference must sometimes move its centre, share its means and let others change what counts as arrival. The work continues where those changes become possible.

Evidence and source work

Distinguish current implementation, observed encounter, reader interpretation and proposed experiment. A scene check does not establish understanding, comfort or political freedom.
