How many parts
make a curve?

A cylinder, a capsule and a sphere stand beside each other. Change one count and watch where each body changes. A sharper corner, a rounder cap, another row of triangles: the same setting does different work in different forms.

The film samples a few settings. In the museum, the controls let you try every integer segment count from 4 to 64 and every ring count from 0 to 32. The sphere starts at 1 ring; its label shows that minimum when the other two are at 0.

Two counts, three constructions

BodySegments: around the bodyRings: along its height
CylinderMore sides make its circular outline finer.R rings add R cuts along the straight wall, making R + 1 bands. The outline stays the same.
CapsuleMore sides divide its circumference more finely.Each cap and the straight middle have R + 1 bands. The caps become rounder as their curved profiles receive more samples.
SphereMore sectors run around its circumference.R + 1 bands span pole to pole. More bands change the vertical approximation of the curve.

The counts describe how each generator builds its surface. They do not measure beauty. Four sides can be a deliberate form; sixty-four can be an unnecessary expense. The question is what the next division makes possible.

Stop at your own combination

S− / S+
Decrease or increase segments by one, from 4 to 64.
R− / R+
Decrease or increase rings by one, from 0 to 32. The sphere remains at its native minimum of 1.
PLAY / PAUSE
Pause the film. After a manual count change, PLAY turns the selected forms while keeping your counts fixed.
REPLAY
Return to the beginning of the automatic comparison.
STEP
Inspect the next stage of the comparison.
SURFACE
Show or hide the black triangle edges.

Changing a count pauses the animation so the result stays available to inspect. The two counts are independent: all combinations within the monitor’s range are available. These upper limits belong to this exhibit; Godot’s setters do not impose the same upper bounds.

Inside the Endless Museum

Look at the screen and the film runs. Look away and it holds its place. Manual pause stays in force when you look back. The screen is a camera view of a separate Godot world; its changing meshes leave the original museum artifacts untouched.
