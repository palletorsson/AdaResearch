The colour between the pillars

Stand where the colours are pale. Follow one line of pillars farther into the field. The hue stays recognisable while its saturation increases. Now walk across that line. The hue changes too. Two directions, two different ways to change a colour.

There are thirty-five pillars, but the floor does not stop at thirty-five colours. Look between the columns. The smooth field continues where no pillar stands. Each pillar is a sample of that field at a particular position.

The same field, seen from above and from between the columns. A colour continues across the ground where no pillar stands.

var u := clampf(p.x / 12.8, 0.0, 1.0)
var v := clampf(p.z / 19.2, 0.0, 1.0)
return Color.from_hsv(u * 0.85, lerpf(0.25,1.0,v),0.95)

p is a position measured from the field's first sample. Dividing by 12.8 and 19.2 turns the distances across its two spans into fractions: u and v. clampf keeps each fraction between zero and one.

Stay in one column of samples and follow it from the pale end to the far end. Its u, and therefore its hue, stays fixed. At the first row, v is zero and saturation is 0.25; at the last, v is one and saturation is 1.0. lerpf supplies the values between them. On the way back, the same hue grows pale again.

Value stays at 0.95 throughout the field. You have varied one part of HSV while keeping two still. The gaps between columns contain colours the collection has not raised into pillars. They are also where you can walk. Adding a column in one of these gaps would give another colour a body and take away somewhere you could stand.

Five columns by seven rows, with 3.2 metres between samples: the field gives the rule room to be walked. Each pillar rises 4.6 metres. Walk close enough to read a sample's address and colour code. Compare its surface with the floor at its foot. The floor displays its colour without the lighting applied to the pillars; the same computed value can meet you differently. Step back until several pillars overlap. Their values remain fixed. What their company makes visible keeps changing.

Beyond the field, twelve collections hang on a framed wall. Their colours have names, and their groups have names too: Bauhaus School Colors, Frida Kahlo's Palette, Harlem Renaissance Jazz. Someone has chosen which colours will stand here under those titles. The names suggest histories that these small lists cannot contain. These sets were assembled. The field was evaluated. Both are ways of deciding which colours belong together.

Take a swatch if you want to carry that decision into another view. Its label pairs a hexadecimal RGB code with the nearest colour name from a small list. The code travels with it; its neighbours stay on the wall. Compare it with a pillar, with another swatch, with the space around your hand. The label stays the same in this new company. Does the colour still look as though it belongs where you found it?

The wall does not finish the gradient. It gives you another method of collecting it, with another set of omissions.
