# Trans_Intro — Critical Reflection

## Three Ontologies of the Gap

The gap is the same. The question is the same: *How do we cross?*

But the answers reveal three different ways of being in space.

### Translation: Being Elsewhere

The transport cube solves the gap by **movement**. You were here; now you are there. The gap still exists — you simply aren't in it anymore.

In this implementation, translation changes an origin while preserving the cube's dimensions and orientation. The room supplies a coordinate frame; the collision surface and rider detection supply the possibility of transport. A position alone could not carry a visitor.

### Rotation: Being Oriented

The rotating plank solves the gap by **reorientation**. Its centre stays fixed while its ends move. A quarter-turn about the upright axis aligns its long dimension with the route. Its top stays level: this hall offers a flat bridge. Later, Rotation will turn blades about the other axes to reach higher landings.

Rotation assumes objects have **facing**. The cube is not a point but a solid with sides. Which side faces you matters. Which side faces up matters.

This creates anisotropy — the inequality of directions. "Forward" is not the same as "sideways." Rotation makes direction meaningful.

### Scale: Being Present

The scale cube solves the gap by **presence**. The bridge doesn't move or turn — it grows. It expands into the void until the void is no longer void.

Scale is about **taking up space**. The gap isn't crossed; it's *occupied*. The object claims territory through its size.

This raises questions about the politics of presence. Who gets to take up space? Whose growth is welcome? The scaled object asserts: "I belong here. This space is mine now."

## Queer Readings

**Translation** can serve an instruction: go where the route sends you. But displacement can also move the destination itself. The bank slider makes that possibility available. An operation has no single political allegiance independent of its use.

**Rotation** makes an orientation available for reconsideration. The plank remains horizontal throughout this encounter, yet the same solid alternately offers a route and withdraws it. Its facing matters because of how it meets the banks and the approaching body.

**Scale** queers presence. To grow is to become visible, undeniable, impossible to ignore. To shrink is to pass, to fit in, to accommodate. The scaled cube asks: *At what size am I allowed to exist?*

## The Gap Itself

Perhaps the gap is not a problem to solve but a space to inhabit. The transformations assume the gap is lack — something missing that must be filled or crossed.

But the gap is also potential. Space for movement, for breath, for difference. The three transformations are three ways of refusing the gap's gift: the gift of distance, of separation, of not-yet-touching.

What would it mean to rest in the gap? To neither cross it, nor rotate into it, nor fill it — but simply to be with it?

The cubes cannot answer. They are tools. But the player, standing at the edge, might pause before stepping on.

The three cubes are not equivalent solutions to a single problem. They are three different ontological commitments about what a body is in space. Translation changes position while preserving the body's shape; whether that body can be carried also depends on contact and collision. Rotation treats the body as a solid — it has faces, it has a facing, and changing orientation changes which face of the body meets the world. Scale treats the body as a volume — it has presence, it has a footprint, and changing size changes how much of the world it occupies.

Each reading carries political stakes. Translation privileges mobility and treats location as incidental. Rotation privileges orientation and treats the body's edges as meaningful. Scale privileges presence and treats occupancy as the primary fact. The map does not rank these; it asks the learner to feel the difference between them by crossing the same gap three times.

## The background receives a handle

The adjustable landing tests an assumption already made by all three crossings: the far side waits where the moving surface expects it. Shift that side by one metre. The plank continues its original rotation, but its aligned end now leaves seventy centimetres of empty space before the landing.

That is a geometric measurement. The limit of passage needs a body and a movement rule as well. A continuous straight walk, a jump and another collision shape can produce different outcomes. We should record which attempt failed before declaring a space impossible.

This gives the critical question a small, repeatable action. Keep one operation, change one relation, return with RESET. The simulator's law becomes something a learner can locate and alter. The fixed side aisles also matter: examining a limit need not make it compulsory to fall into it.
