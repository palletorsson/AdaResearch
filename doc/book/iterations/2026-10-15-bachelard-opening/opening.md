# From the moving spheres to the RAND page

<!-- @random_touch -->

We stand before three thin lines hanging in the air, one metre apart. Each is two metres long, with a dark sphere waiting at its centre. We can see the room behind and between them.

We touch the first sphere. It carries us sideways, and the room slides past. The line stays where it was. The sphere slows into one position, then departs for another. We can see how far it is allowed to go. Its next turn remains uncertain.

At the next line, we make the same journey twice as quickly. The positions repeat. The line has not grown, but there is less time inside the same distance. With the third sphere, we return to the slower pace and rise and fall as well as moving sideways. The space above the line becomes part of our journey.

We withdraw our hand. The sphere returns alone to its centre. No trace of our passage remains on the line. Yet the path has not been lost.

The positions are kept in the program. A pseudorandom generator—a repeatable calculation—produced them from a starting state set by a seed. We press REPLAY and touch the first sphere again. The turns return in the same order. The next movement can still be unfamiliar, even though the whole sequence has been kept.

Further into the room, we come to a large page of falling numbers. They arrive from above, one row at a time, in groups of five digits. We touch a group. It turns amber and stays while the surrounding numbers continue downwards. We take our hand away. The digits remain.

Our touch has acquired another consequence. At the sphere, we began a movement. Here, we have made a small still place in the fall.

RAND's *A Million Random Digits*, published in 1955, made a supply of numbers available for calculations using random selections. Producing that supply required testing and correction: its electronic source introduced biases, systematic departures from the intended even distribution. The published table preserved the results after further processing. Random numbers could be returned to at a numbered page. [RAND's production account](https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1418.html).

This falling page uses its own generated digits. We have selected a group that was already there. What we have changed is how long those digits stay in view. The rows move on around them. We read the five digits again.
