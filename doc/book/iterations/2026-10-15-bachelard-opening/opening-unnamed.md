# From the moving spheres to the RAND page

<!-- @random_touch -->

Three thin lines hang in the air, one metre apart. Two metres each, with a dark sphere waiting at the centre. The whole distance visible at once. The room continues behind and between the lines.

Contact with the first sphere, and the room begins to slide sideways. The line stays fixed in the room. The sphere slows into one position, then departs for another, carrying the viewpoint along. The limits are visible. The next turn remains uncertain.

At the next line, the same positions arrive twice as quickly. The line has not grown. There is less time inside the same distance. The third sphere restores the slower pace, adding rising and falling. Now the strip of room above the line belongs to the journey too.

Contact ends. The view stops travelling with the sphere, which returns alone to its centre. No trace left on the line. Yet the path has not been lost.

The positions are kept in the program. A pseudorandom generator—a repeatable calculation—produced them from a starting state set by a seed. REPLAY, another contact, and the turns return in the same order. The next movement can remain unfamiliar even when the whole sequence has been kept.

Further into the room, numbers descend across a large page. They arrive from above, one row at a time, in groups of five digits. One touch, and a group turns amber. The surrounding numbers continue downwards. Contact ends; the selected digits remain.

The same gesture has acquired another consequence. At the sphere, movement began. Here, a small still place opens in the fall.

RAND's *A Million Random Digits*, published in 1955, made a supply of numbers available for calculations using random selections. Producing that supply required testing and correction: its electronic source introduced biases, systematic departures from the intended even distribution. The published table preserved the results after further processing. Random numbers could be returned to at a numbered page. [RAND's production account](https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1418.html).

This falling page uses its own generated digits. The selected group was already present before the touch. What changed was how long those digits would stay. The rows move on around them. Five digits remain, available to a second reading.
