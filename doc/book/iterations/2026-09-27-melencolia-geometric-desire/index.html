Melancholy of Finitude

What keeps you looking after you know the form?

Four pale blue pyramids mark the corners of a raised square. Before going closer, try to hold the arrangement in mind. Where would you put a fifth?

Walk around the court. One pyramid passes in front of another. A gap opens where two outlines seemed to meet. The objects have stayed still; your movement changes the picture. You have met this distinction before, but now the picture has become a place with room around it.

Take the side ramp onto the platform. The four corners repeat a form you can describe: a square base, four triangular sides, one apex. Five vertices, eight edges, five faces. Knowing those counts helps you recognise the family. It leaves you free to notice something else.

The fifth pyramid is pink, taller, and stands in the centre on a matching block. Look back at a corner. Does the centre seem to organise the others, or have you made it important because it rises above them?

Its base is still square. Its apex is still a single point above it. The tall pyramid's source collects the base vertices and then adds that point:

vertices.append_array(ring)
vertices.append(_apex_position(ring))

The same connections can carry a different height. In this version the pyramid's base is 0.8 metres wide and its apex rises 2.8 metres above that base. The pink block lifts the whole pyramid another 0.8 metres above the court. The script triangulates the square base into two faces for rendering; together with the four sides, that makes six triangles. We can explain how it is built without settling what its height does to the arrangement.

Four ochre cubes sit around the spire in a cross. Follow that cross with your eyes, then look toward the corner pyramids. A few familiar solids are becoming a court, a monument, perhaps a model of somewhere you would like to go. Which reading arrived first?

The arrangement returns to the room's earlier floor plan. Its reference belongs to an anonymous sixteenth-century manuscript of geometric and perspective studies: solids drawn, combined and staged until a study begins to suggest an unfamiliar architecture.1 Here the forms stand on a platform made from the same cube vocabulary as the museum floor. The support is already part of the picture.

Imagine one cube placed outside the cross. Its construction would be unchanged; its relation to the court would be different. You do not need to build every possibility before leaving this one.

The same court, from three viewpoints. At the left, two corner pyramids hide two others. None of the objects has moved.

Beside the front of the court, five snap points offer a small construction. Connect four into a base cycle and join each to the fifth. Watch what the puzzle accepts.

Its detector recognises those connections. It does not measure whether the base is square. Keep that distinction close: the program can complete its test while your question about the form remains open. The court was here before the test succeeded. Its place in the room has another history.

The tools are still here

Follow the raised connection to the next platform. The large stone stands to the left; the winged figure sits to the right, her head resting on a fist. A sphere lies near the sleeping dog. Above them, the ladder, scales, hourglass and bell give the wall its own order. Beneath them, a saw and a carpenter's plane lie among the small tools. Here the forms have company.2

The compass is in her hand. She has not lost the instrument. A book lies on her lap, and the skirt falls in heavy folds over her knees. Beside her, a smaller winged figure bends over a tablet. One is drawing. The other looks away.

Earlier, a sphere could introduce a primitive. Here it occupies ground beside a tired animal. Nothing has happened to the sphere's definition. Much has happened to what we can see in it.

We have carried something from the court of ideal forms into this clutter of work and waiting. We might call it geometric desire: the wish that a form might let us see further, or give us somewhere else to stand. Here that wish meets a body that cannot be reduced to its instruments. Knowing how to construct another solid does not tell her what to do next.

Walk sideways. The stone hides the smaller figure, then lets it appear again. Our sculpture gives depth to an engraving; Dürer did not leave us the far side of this room. Those hidden surfaces are decisions we have made. The apparent completeness of the model rests on what the picture could not tell us.

After Dürer's engraving: an arrangement of sculpted figures, tools and solids. Their placement follows the image; their depth is an interpretation. Walking changes which objects conceal one another.

The stone has six pentagonal faces and two triangular ends. Its mesh begins with a rhombohedron and cuts off two opposite corners. The proportions are one possible reading of the printed form. We can make a closed solid without claiming to have recovered the only solid the image permits.

Now look up at the numbered square. Choose a row and add it before reading further.

Try a column. Each row and column, and the two main diagonals, sums to 34. The 15 and 14 in the bottom row recall 1514, the year of the engraving. The numbers work.

Stay a little longer. Has that correct answer finished what you came to look at?

What do we want a form to promise?

This desire will return with different demands. In the patterned surfaces of the Alhambra, geometry belongs to built space; Escher's study of its mosaics later fed his own investigations of repeating figures.4 A rule can continue beyond the piece of wall that holds it. Our museum also stops building at an edge while letting us imagine more.

Malevich's tilted square in White on White offers another possibility: a painted surface can suggest release into unbounded space. Hilma af Klint's Paintings for the Temple give abstract forms a different task, within a spiritual inquiry.5 The same circle would not mean the same thing in each of these worlds. What someone asks of it matters.

And abstraction need not carry us away from the body. Lygia Clark's hinged metal Bichos ask for hands: a flat arrangement opens into a changing object.6 To call this Brazilian work only escapist would miss that return to touch. Perhaps the escape is from having to remain a spectator. What can geometry make possible between a form and the person who meets it?

Keep that question beside the seated figure. The tools are here. Their next use is not settled.

The number that was already running

By the way out, a number is changing. It is the same counter you met on your first step into this chapter: Engine.get_process_frames(), process frames since the engine started, read fresh on every call.

It is much larger now. Some of that is yours — the picking up, the drawing, the walking, the shifting of a start. A fixed quantity of it is not, and never will be. The frames that passed before you had somewhere to stand did not become yours by being counted alongside the ones that followed. This number adds them together anyway. It has no field for the difference.

In the first room we said that its first number for you was not its first number. That is still the one thing it will not tell you, and you now know several reasons why. A record keeps what its rule admits. Someone chose the rule. The number keeps growing while you read it.

Enough to continue

Dürer's engraving is itself a made thing. An accomplished image gives us an encounter with unfinished work. That matters here. Looking, arranging, imagining and taking pleasure in a form can keep an investigation moving while its purpose is unsettled. The aesthetic attention is already doing work.

A few tools, and more possible constructions than we have time to pursue.3

Look back toward the five pyramids. They have become another picture from here. The objects stayed still while your viewpoint changed. In transformation, you can watch from one place as objects move, turn and scale. Choose one relation from this court—the repeated corners, the tall centre, the distance between the platforms—and ask what would survive such a change.

We leave with enough to continue. There is still something here we have not finished looking at.

Different promises of geometry

The chapter introduces the thread. This companion differentiates the histories and identifies possible returns elsewhere in the spine. These additional artists are references for development, not new exhibits installed in this hall.

Geometric desire

This room puts two arrangements beside one another: a court of recognisable solids and a sculptural interpretation of Dürer's Melencolia I. Geometry travels between them without remaining innocent of its company. A sphere beside a tired animal can still be described by its radius. That description no longer accounts for what makes us stop.

The movement from Plato to melancholy is the book's proposed encounter. It is not a claim that Dürer illustrated a simple defeat of Platonism. Nor does a seated figure establish that mathematics has exhausted itself. The engraving gathers capacities for measuring and making around a body whose next action is uncertain. A smaller figure is at work. Hesitation and activity coexist in the same picture. The Met presents the print's meanings as contested; the National Gallery's visual account helps distinguish depicted objects from our interpretation.

The museum makes this uncertainty material in another way. We can build a closed polyhedron, but the image does not uniquely supply every depth and angle. To make the tableau walkable is to decide what the engraving withheld. Going around the stone reveals part of the smaller figure. Going around the wall reveals the model's back. Computation offers a complete surface by introducing decisions, not by removing uncertainty.

Several relations to abstraction

The following comparisons guide returns elsewhere in the book. They are research directions, not five additional installed artworks in this room, and not a genealogy in which every tradition leads toward European abstraction.

Encounter A grounded distinction A question for a later museum encounter
Islamic geometric ornament, including the Alhambra Pattern belongs to made surfaces, materials and architecture. Its rules can suggest continuation without explaining every cultural use. What happens to a repeat when it meets a corner, door or body? Let Tiling keep the wall as well as the pattern.
Escher His documented study of Spanish-Islamic mosaics in 1936 encouraged further tessellation studies. It is one particular act of learning across traditions. When a repeated unit becomes a recognisable figure, does the ground become a second figure? Let movement change which boundary belongs to which form.
Malevich White on White proposes spatial freedom through a tilted painted square. The paint remains visibly made; liberation also had revolutionary political associations. Can a finite screen give a sense of unbounded space? Which edge, texture or bodily movement gives the surface back?
Hilma af Klint The Paintings for the Temple belong to her spiritual investigation. Geometric forms participate in a system of correspondences. When does a diagram explain a relationship, and when does it ask us to trust an invisible order? Whose knowledge receives the authority of a diagram?
Lygia Clark and Brazilian Neo-Concrete art The manipulable Bichos make the encounter with a participant part of the work. A hinge gives geometry a bodily condition. What does a hand discover that a diagram leaves open? Can an object resist the configuration the visitor intended?

Sources: Met, geometric patterns in Islamic art; Escher in The Palace, timeline; MoMA, White on White; Guggenheim, Paintings for the Temple; MoMA, Poetic Shelter; MoMA, Neo-Concrete art.

What is being escaped?

“Escapism” is worth retaining as a question about what abstraction permits us to leave. It becomes weak when it describes a whole continent. Clark's work offers a particular counterexample: the participant enters the work through touch. Abstraction can also seek transcendence, make a new social order imaginable, or reorganise ordinary perception. These ambitions can overlap, but their historical conditions matter.

For this project, the queer opening lies in a form's capacity to enter another relation without being exhausted by its prescribed use. A sphere can be a point, a ball, a coordinate, an obstruction, a thing to cradle, or a neighbour to a sleeping dog. That list does not make the sphere inherently queer, or prove a theory through its shape. It asks who is allowed to approach it, handle it, give it a purpose, and refuse the purpose already offered.

The engine makes some of those permissions literal. Our current sphere has collision and remains fixed. The model offers circulation but no act of taking up the compass. Those limits should remain visible in the writing. A later encounter might give that instrument to a hand; the present scene has not already done so.

The earlier critical draft is preserved in the revision's before folder. Its claims that geometry has reached a dead end and that the magic square is meaningless are not the working position of this chapter. Exactness can sustain desire without completing it.
