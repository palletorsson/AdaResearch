Rainbow — keep the opening

Learning elements

  • A palette as an ordered list of colours
  • An index connecting a colour to a band
  • Dividing a fixed extent among a changing number of parts

Walk through the museum's arches. Their arrangements differ, but each leaves an opening for a body. In Nails you chose a colour with a point. Here a colour joins a list, and the list changes the division of an arch.

This is a short, optional making lesson. The celebration, pickups and banner belong to the installed encounter. We will make one small arch to examine how its parts share an extent.

Bring a chosen colour

Copy the RGB value you recorded in Nails into the first entry of palette. The starting example uses Color(0.8, 0.2, 0.6). This is a deliberate transfer of a recorded value; the script does not retrieve it from the preceding hall.

Save lesson.gd and the supplied arc_strip.gd together. Attach lesson.gd to a Node3D in your working copy of the Rainbow hall, with its origin at the centre of an open display surface. Keep unit scale. The museum supplies the camera, light and player. This small arch stands in the local XY plane and has no collider.

ArcStrip builds one coloured semicircular band between two radii. Its complete implementation is printed below. Mesh construction returns from Primitives; the new decision here is how many bands share the available thickness.

The complete study — lesson.gd

extends Node3D

const ArcStrip = preload("arc_strip.gd")
const INNER_RADIUS := 0.7
const TOTAL_THICKNESS := 0.6

var palette: Array[Color] = [Color(0.8, 0.2, 0.6), Color(0.15, 0.55, 0.9), Color(1, 0.8, 0.15)]
var bands: Array[MeshInstance3D] = []

func _ready() -> void:
    assert(not palette.is_empty())
    var width := TOTAL_THICKNESS / float(palette.size())
    for i in range(palette.size()):
        var inner := INNER_RADIUS + i * width
        var band := ArcStrip.new()
        band.build(inner, inner + width, palette[i])
        add_child(band)
        bands.append(band)

Run it. Three bands surround the same opening. The innermost radius is 0.7 metres; the outermost radius is 1.3 metres. TOTAL_THICKNESS is the radial thickness of all the bands together, not the diameter of the whole arch.

palette.size() is the number of entries. Dividing 0.6 by three gives each band a width of 0.2. The loop visits each index i: it advances that band's inner radius by i * width, then uses palette[i] for its colour. Each outer radius meets the next inner radius.

The assertion requires a nonempty palette. We cannot divide the thickness among zero bands. Every nonempty palette keeps the same inner and outer boundaries; only their internal division changes.

Change the division

Replace the three-entry palette with [Color(0.8, 0.2, 0.6), Color.WHITE]. Predict the width, then run. Each band is 0.3 metres thick. Restore the original palette and append Color.BLACK: four bands now share the same 0.6 metres, each taking 0.15.

Change one colour without adding or removing an entry. The geometry should stay still. Band count and colour assignment are separate decisions. This example couples them by making one band per palette entry. Another arrangement could repeat two colours across six bands; the number of available colours would then differ from the number of parts, as it did in the opening Colour lesson.

Choose your own nonempty palette. Before running, state how many bands will appear, their individual width, and the outer radius. As a boundary case, use one colour: it occupies the full thickness without closing the opening.

The museum's arches are larger and use their own palettes and arrangement. This study retains their fixed-extent construction. Its colours are assigned surface values; it does not simulate wavelengths or the optics of a rainbow.

Supplied band builder — arc_strip.gd

# Supplied renderer: a reduced version of rainbow.gd's concentric half-strips.
extends MeshInstance3D

const STEPS := 32
var inner_radius: float
var outer_radius: float

func build(inner: float, outer: float, colour: Color) -> void:
    inner_radius = inner
    outer_radius = outer
    var vertices := PackedVector3Array()
    var normals := PackedVector3Array()
    var indices := PackedInt32Array()
    for i in range(STEPS + 1):
        var angle := PI * float(i) / STEPS
        var direction := Vector3(cos(angle), sin(angle), 0)
        vertices.append(direction * inner)
        vertices.append(direction * outer)
        normals.append(Vector3.BACK)
        normals.append(Vector3.BACK)
    for i in range(STEPS):
        var n := i * 2
        indices.append_array([n, n + 1, n + 2, n + 1, n + 3, n + 2])
    var data := []
    data.resize(Mesh.ARRAY_MAX)
    data[Mesh.ARRAY_VERTEX] = vertices
    data[Mesh.ARRAY_NORMAL] = normals
    data[Mesh.ARRAY_INDEX] = indices
    var result := ArrayMesh.new()
    result.add_surface_from_arrays(Mesh.PRIMITIVE_TRIANGLES, data)
    mesh = result
    var material := StandardMaterial3D.new()
    material.albedo_color = colour
    material.shading_mode = BaseMaterial3D.SHADING_MODE_UNSHADED
    material.cull_mode = BaseMaterial3D.CULL_DISABLED
    material_override = material

The builder samples a semicircle, storing an inner and an outer vertex at each angle. Two triangles join each neighbouring pair. The material is independent for each band and unshaded so the palette remains easy to compare. Changing the palette does not change this renderer.

Keep the chosen list. Pillar will ask how a function can produce colours between selected values, instead of choosing only among entries.

More colour, another rainbow

Two offers wait by the entrance: a crystal in a wire case and a cube to collect. Neither is required to join the celebration.

The mushrooms are dressed for it. Pearl collars beneath candy-coloured caps; stars beyond the arch; a small staircase that seems to have wandered in from a platform game. There is also a level path. You can join the celebration without taking the steps.

Enter along the coloured centre path. Confetti opens above it. You have not solved a puzzle yet. Stay a moment with the possibility that the room is pleased you arrived.

The little wire cubes have returned, spaced along the middle of the walk. Walk into one. Its outline disappears and sparks rise from its place, drawing long coloured tails through the air. Keep walking. Another cube, another burst. For a moment, the celebration marks where you have been.

Find a boundary between two colours in the rainbow. Follow it far enough to see its curve. At the control plinth, press RE-DIVIDE. Find that boundary again. Has it moved, disappeared, acquired a neighbour? Has the opening beneath the arch become any smaller?

Try the button again before deciding what kind of rainbow this is. The steps keep their height. The path remains where it was. Yet the colours have another arrangement, and the familiar outline is being asked to hold it. CELEBRATE gives you another burst whenever you want one. There is no correct partition to earn first.

In the salon, one selected colour reached the same hand at two scales. Here a list of colours reaches a series of curved strips. Each strip is made from vertices: points placed along an inner and an outer half-circle, joined into triangles. Its position in the list gives it a colour.

The button moves through four authored choices: six bands, seven, forty-eight, then two. The generator keeps their combined width fixed:

return (RAINBOW_THICKNESS * 7.0) / float(maxi(1, rainbow_colors.size()))

The code keeps a total band thickness of 7 × 0.8, shared out again whenever the list changes. The arch is scaled down to fit this hall. More strips each receive less of the fixed total; fewer strips receive more. The familiar arch can survive a change in how we divide its colour.

Four arrangements, seen from the same place. The coloured steps change with the arch; their height and the opening beneath it remain.

Look for the seams in the forty-eight-band version. The option is called continuous, but each strip still has its own colour.

For each strip, the code computes a hue. HSV names hue, saturation and value: a place around a colour circle, how far the colour is from grey at that value, and a level that reaches black at zero. Here saturation and value both stay at one. Only hue travels:

var t: float = float(i) / float(count - 1)
out.append(Color.from_hsv(t * 0.78, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0))

t runs from zero at the first strip to one at the last. A hue value of one would complete a turn around the circle; multiplying by 0.78 takes this sweep partway around instead.

A name can promise continuity while an implementation offers enough small divisions for some seams to become difficult to see. These are HSV values converted into RGB colours on a mesh. No droplets, sunlight or wavelength calculation produce this particular rainbow. Even its opaque appearance does not make the bands a climbing surface. The low steps have collision; the arch is a display.

Look for a colour you chose in the salon, first in the arch and then among the striped banners and mushrooms. There are pinks, pale blues, whites and dark colours that this sweep through saturated hues does not offer. They already share the room, even when the arch has no place for them. What would you have to change in the list, or in the rule that builds it, to give another colour a place in the arch?1

After the fourth arrangement, RE-DIVIDE returns to the first. You can stay with a version you like. Or you might want a fifth: a pale band, a colour given more room, a border that wanders away from the curve. The next change would reach beyond this button, into the list or the rule that makes it.

Before leaving, choose the version you want to stand beneath. Let the confetti return. At the far end of the walk, two slender poles hold a rainbow banner above your head. You can pass beneath it whether you collected every cube or left them all. The same programmed event can be a reward for obedience or an invitation that asks nothing of you; here you can repeat the confetti without passing a test.

Carry the distinction between a colour rule and the places where it is sampled. In the next room, pillars stand at particular addresses in a colour field. What is between them, where the field has a value but no pillar has been given a body?

  1. A question for later: queer has no compulsory colour. A flag, a garment and a pastel wall do different work. We cannot assume that conservatives share a fear of pastels; we can ask when a colour becomes a threat to an order that wants its distinctions kept. In Powers of Horror (1982), Julia Kristeva connects abjection with disturbances of identity, system and order (opening chapter, pp. 1–4). This offers a way to question the policing of a boundary, without calling queer bodies abject. A future encounter could let us soften the bands or mix their colours towards white. These are different operations. A continuous control would still have limits; smoothness alone would not make it free. ↩
