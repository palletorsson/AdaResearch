# The same floor, another palette

Do you recognise the floor in its new colours?

The checker is navy and pink now. Its squares return in the same arrangement. Step onto one and wait for its colour to change. It keeps supporting you. After the coloured rooms, this familiar floor gives us somewhere to try what we have learned.

<!-- @disco_controls -->

Nothing has moved. The square keeps its calculated zero or one and receives another colour. Leave the palette alone and press ONE STEP. Now the checker exchanges its values. Two different actions can change the picture. Follow one place long enough to tell them apart.

![Two palettes on the same held checker, seen from the same place on the museum floor.](/book-images/iterations/2026-09-28-disco-colour-in-step/checker-pair.png)

One palette choice reaches every square carrying the same value. We cannot use this control to leave just one of them pink. The reach of a choice belongs to the way the instrument has been made.

<!-- @standalone_disco -->

Start the floor and walk towards an approaching band. Let it pass underneath you. Its passage does not push your body. Walk with it, then across it. Can an invitation to move be felt without a force being applied?

Return to black and white while it runs. Notice whether you follow the same parts. Changing back cannot undo the coloured versions you have just seen beside it.

<!-- @step_sequencer -->

Bring in a repeating note. The score and the moving pattern have separate clocks. Let them run, look away, and keep moving. When you look back, where do you find the beat?

Your footsteps are not written into the score. You can move with its pulse, miss it, or keep another rhythm. The room repeats its invitation without knowing what you make of it.

# A mark in the room

In the disco, a choice of colour reached every square carrying the same value. Here you can make the little arrangement that a larger pattern will repeat. One mark, then its many appearances. How far can a decision made within reach travel?

*Mamma Mönster* records a hope behind Kristina Torsson's textile practice: soft, colourful clothes might change people. Making a pattern began with something wanted from the world. Keep that hope beside this small editor. What would you want your mark to make possible?

<!-- @pattern_architecture -->

There is already pattern underfoot. It climbs the walls. At DRESS THE ARCHITECTURE, choosing another source changes what the hall wears. Its floor still supports you, and its walls still stop you. Leave the larger image running and go to the small tile, where the beginning is easier to follow.

<!-- @pattern_tile_4x4 -->

Add a second mark beside it. Predict the repeated pair before looking across the field. Many visible changes have followed one local edit. The size of the result can make the action seem more complicated than it was.

Now try to make the mark appear only once while the rest stays unchanged. Editing the shared source makes it recur. This instrument makes it easier to change every appearance than to keep just one different.

The source stores the colours you chose. It does not store why you chose them. The wish behind the work still needs someone to carry it.

# An address needs a surface

When does repetition become an environment rather than a picture?

The red bend returns, with its blue corner and ochre interruption. It is the familiar starting arrangement, supplied again here. Your edits from the previous room have not travelled automatically. This time the question is what gives the mark a place to appear.

<!-- @pattern_tunnel_machine -->

Take the ramp into the patterned passage. Find the pale indicator on the floor. At the console, move it along a row and watch the two addresses: its position on the surface keeps advancing while its position in the small source returns to the beginning. Another place can consult the same memory.

Move the indicator onto the right wall. Rows and columns now occupy another plane. On the floor, one direction crosses the passage and the other follows it. On the wall, one runs along the passage and the other descends from the ceiling. An address needs a surface to acquire a direction in the room.

Walk inside. Follow a line to the corner. Each face begins its own grid; a shared motif does not guarantee a join. Where should counting begin, and which way should it proceed?

Enlarge the cells. The passage stays the same size, so fewer copies fit. Some divisions meet an edge exactly; others are cut short. The room does not owe the pattern an integer number of repeats.

In *Mamma Mönster*, enlarging a textile pattern for a tennis court required trials with paper and wood stencils, paint and tools suited to the harder surface. Mimmi Torsson made drawings; a local firm made metal templates. Even the proposed windbreak changed material because of its weight. Keeping a recognisable pattern required changes to the way it was made.

Here a number can enlarge the image immediately. Those other negotiations have not been reproduced by the control. Keep them in mind when the result appears effortless.

Turn off the drawn grid and keep walking. No wall opens; no floor tile falls away. The visible divisions and the structures supporting your body have different jobs. A surface can look newly continuous while its construction stays as it was.

# What counts as the same?

What would have to stay the same for this to be the same pattern?

You have followed an address around a corridor. Here the surface stays flat. Keep the source still and follow what happens when a reflection is also shifted.

<!-- @vr_tile_editor -->

At the tilted console, find the corner colour in the held P1 sample. Choose PM on the live field and follow the corner into its next appearance. Now choose PG. The reversal remains, but its neighbour has slipped along the other direction. The reflected reading shifts by half the source height.

The mark has not been repainted. An operation we know has become one part of another. Hold the result beside the earlier image and find a return you can explain.

![Held P1 and live PG in the museum side bay](/book-images/figures/tiling/symmetry-p1-pg-museum.png)

*One source, two ways of returning.*

Make the source less distinctive. Does the difference remain visible? A chosen mark can conceal an operation or introduce extra symmetry. The selected button names a construction; it does not prove that the resulting image has exactly that symmetry group.

<!-- @wallpaper_cage -->

The cage marks rotational orders one, two, three, four and six, and crosses five and seven. Its restriction concerns rotations compatible with a periodic planar lattice. A fivefold-looking flower may sit inside a repeating cell without making the whole field fivefold symmetric. Look for the boundary of what is being counted.

For ideal patterns repeating in two independent directions on the Euclidean plane, there are seventeen plane-group types. The [IUCr tables](https://it.iucr.org/Ab/) describe their operations. The catalogue closes under particular assumptions. This finite carpet still ends, meets the light, and appears differently from where you stand.

<!-- @thread_loom -->

At FOLLOW THE THREAD, cream threads rise and fall. Take the brass shuttle through their opening. Beat the pink thread into place. Follow it back at the edge, then make the returning pass.

Here an instruction says which thread passes over another. The crossings help construct the surface you are examining.

<!-- @jacquard_studio -->

Beside it, punched cards hang in a laced chain. One moves into the reader and stops. Threads rise; the shuttle crosses. The next card waits until this pass has joined the cloth.

Watch the first card return. Its instruction can begin again. The cloth has grown.

Patch one hole and wait for that card's next turn. A small difference enters the weave, then returns across its width. The earlier crossings stay as they were. Find where the change began. The cloth holds choices that the card no longer shows.

In the tartan, bands of colour cross bands of colour. Keep the twill and change the colour sequence: a houndstooth appears. The chevron and herringbone keep their yarn colours while their crossings turn differently. What looked like one pattern allows more than one place to act.

Choose the optical checks. Broad bands narrow to fine lines, then widen again. The surface may seem to swell. Lower your head towards its edge. Does the fold you saw have a rise you can follow?

![Optical checks woven in the museum loom](/book-images/figures/tiling/optical-woven-checks-flat.png)

*Changing band widths on a flat woven surface.*

Open the sample into layers. The gaps grow large enough to follow a thread beneath another. This is space made for inspection. The loom did not weave those gaps into the cloth.

Close it again. Knowing how the threads cross need not make the apparent fold disappear. The widths can be counted exactly; what they seem to form meets you at a particular distance and angle.

The machine can carry a choice through repeated passes. What part of knowing how to weave have we put into the card, and what still has to be learned with thread? These paths are geometry. Yarn tension, friction and the work of keeping a loom running remain outside this construction. Knitting would ask another question again: how does one loop hold through another?

Read this beside Sadie Plant's *Zeros + Ones* and the textile practice described in *Mamma Mönster*. Whose work becomes visible when we call a pattern a program? Which skills have we kept close enough to follow with our hands?

At the atlas plates, read the makers' names. A symmetry group can describe relations between marks without describing the labour, place or desire that brought them together.

The looms leave us samples. The later garment studio uses a separate pattern source; it does not turn this woven work into clothing. Keep that unfinished connection available as you walk on.

In Temple, a decision can spread across an entire court. Bring the distinction between a choice and the record it leaves. Which parts of a place should keep agreeing when you change your mind?

# Pattern Temple — the same, for now

The pattern meets you on the floor. Ahead, it stands upright. Around the court, narrow columns turn it away from your eye; above, the dome carries it beyond a single view. You have seen the mark before. You have to move to see what it has become here.

In Foundry, one edited source reached every occurrence that read it. Here each part of the court keeps a recipe of its own. You can make them agree without making every later choice travel between them.

At the desk, a gold point names the surface you are about to change. Move it from the far wall to the columns. The rest of the room waits.

Press ALL. A decision made for one surface reaches the others. For a moment the court agrees. The floor is still underfoot, the column still round, the dome still above you. Agreement in the settings does not remove these differences.

Change the selected palette once more. The room stops agreeing. The earlier button copied a decision; it did not bind the surfaces to every decision that followed. What looked like a single order was a set of separate records that happened to match.

The distinction is harder to read from the entrance. Repetition, height and the space between columns give the pattern an authority that a small source tile did not have on its own. We arranged the approach as well as the ornament. How much of the order belongs to the mark, and how much to the room that teaches you where to stand?

AGE makes the surface look worn. CLEAN removes that treatment. Neither action recovers the people who might have made, maintained or inhabited such a place. The signs of time can be assigned in a moment. An appearance of history is not yet a history.

Keep one wall different. It remains part of the court. Walk back across the patterned floor and look up again. Which difference can you still see from here?
