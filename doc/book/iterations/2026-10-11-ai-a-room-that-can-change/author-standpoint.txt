The standpoint emerging here is a **non-essentialist and relational approach to AI critique**, but with an important reservation: neither the technical object nor its specific capacities should disappear entirely into a generalized account of relations, infrastructures, or power.

A starting point is the observation that **AI is an unusually unstable object of critique**. Many criticisms are formulated around what a model currently cannot do: it cannot reason reliably, cannot understand, cannot make genuinely interesting art, cannot maintain coherence, cannot act autonomously, and so on. Such observations may be correct at a particular moment, but their critical force can have a very short half-life. If the technology changes, the argument can suddenly look less like a structural critique and more like a snapshot of an earlier technical limitation. This suggests that critique should be careful not to freeze AI at one stage of its development and mistake contingent incapacity for an essential property.

This does not mean abandoning critique because technology is moving quickly. Rather, it shifts the question from **“What is wrong with this model right now?”** toward **“What persists, changes, or intensifies as the system develops?”** A stronger critique might therefore concern not only particular outputs but the changing assemblage in which AI is produced: datasets, interfaces, models, human labour, institutions, electricity, hardware, education, intellectual property, cultural assumptions, economic structures, and forms of interaction. Some of these relations may remain critically significant even if model capabilities improve enormously. Indeed, questions about ownership, dependency, authority, labour, cultural homogenization, or infrastructural concentration may become *more* important as AI becomes more capable.

This is where Latour is useful. Instead of treating critique primarily as the act of exposing an object as false, ideological, or socially constructed, the critical task can become one of **following the associations that allow an object to exist and act**. AI is then not reducible to “a language model” in isolation. It exists through a vast and shifting field of relationships. Following the assemblage means asking how these relationships are formed, maintained, displaced, automated, and sometimes lost as the technology changes.

Andreotti and the Gesturing Toward Decolonial Futures work adds another dimension. Rather than immediately stabilizing AI through oppositions such as **human/machine, subject/object, intelligent/non-intelligent, tool/threat**, the question becomes how those very distinctions organize our relationship to the technology. Concepts such as separation, hierarchy, and control can themselves become objects of investigation. This is compatible with a non-essentialist position: we do not have to decide first what intelligence “really is,” or establish a uniquely human essence against which the machine must be measured. Instead, we can investigate what kinds of relations different configurations of humans and computational systems produce.

An important consequence is that **we are not external observers of this system**. We are already embedded in computational infrastructures: communication, research, writing, education, administration, media, software, networks, databases. Critique therefore cannot easily occupy a pure position outside the technology. The interesting question becomes how to act critically *from within implication*: how to experiment with these systems, understand their dependencies, redirect them, refuse certain arrangements, and construct alternatives.

At the same time, this relational position creates its own problem. If everything becomes an assemblage, there is a danger that **AI itself disappears**. We might explain a model as capitalism, infrastructure, colonial history, labour, discourse, datasets, environmental extraction, institutional power—and end up saying surprisingly little about what is technically strange or historically new about the model itself. A transformer, a multimodal model, an agentic system, or a generative world model has particular capacities, behaviours, architectures and forms of mediation. These matter. So there is a productive tension between two errors:

**freezing the object** — treating today's AI as if its current properties were permanent;

and

**dissolving the object** — explaining AI entirely through the relations surrounding it until its specific technical agency and novelty disappear.

The position you are developing sits deliberately between these two.

This is also where your notion of **AI as a room** becomes useful. A room is neither simply an autonomous object nor merely a set of relations. It is a structured situation in which bodies, tools, behaviours, expectations, histories and possibilities encounter one another. People enter it differently; things become possible or impossible according to its architecture; connections are sustained or lost. Thinking about AI as a room therefore moves beyond “AI as tool.” It becomes an environment or condition through which thinking, writing, teaching, programming, interaction and embodied spatial experience can be organized.

That links closely to your VR work. A VR world is itself a structure for thinking: code becomes space; abstract rules become encounters; teaching becomes navigation; writing becomes environmental inscription; interaction makes relations perceptible. AI can be approached similarly—not only as an answer-producing machine, but as a **space of transformations and relations that can itself be explored experimentally**.

The resulting critical question is therefore not simply:

> **What is AI?**

or even:

> **What is wrong with AI?**

It becomes something closer to:

> **How can we remain critically responsive to an object whose technical capacities and surrounding assemblage are continuously transforming, without either freezing it into a fixed ontology or explaining the object away entirely?**

And beneath that is perhaps an even broader methodological proposition:

> **A durable critique of AI should be capable of changing with its object.**

It should distinguish temporary failures from structural conditions, remain attentive to concrete technical changes, trace the relations that make those changes possible, and continually reconsider the conceptual distinctions—human/machine, intelligence/tool, autonomy/automation, body/infrastructure—that the technology itself is destabilizing.

That feels to me like the clearest articulation of the standpoint you are arriving at.
