Point Lines — what can two lines do?

Learning elements

  • Endpoints and a segment
  • Displacement and distance
  • A class and its instances
  • Parallel, cross and X
  • Position in depth

Take one end of the line in the black room. The other stays. The span turns and changes length. We will make that relation using the point from Coordinates, then arrange two lines into parallel marks, a plus and an X.

Keep the point; give it another end

Continue from your working Coordinates script. Its point, surface, collider, pickup settings and local/world readout remain. We will put its familiar construction in a class, make two instances, and draw the relation between them.

Save point_body.gd, line.gd and line_skin.gd together. All three files are printed below; only the drawing helper is supplied. On your existing PointStudy node, replace the attached point.gd with line.gd. Keep point.gd as the previous working version. Run this one station; the script creates both endpoints. The prepared museum supplies the room, light and configured XR hands.

Keep PointStudy at scale (1, 1, 1). Its position and orientation may change. With this scale, our measured units remain metres.

Give the familiar point a class — point_body.gd

We have already built a sphere with a matching boundary and made it pickable. Move those instructions into point_body.gd. A class gives us a repeatable construction: each call to PointBody.new() makes a separate point.

extends XRToolsPickable

const RADIUS := 0.14

func _init() -> void:
    freeze = true
    freeze_mode = RigidBody3D.FREEZE_MODE_KINEMATIC
    release_mode = XRToolsPickable.ReleaseMode.FROZEN
    ranged_grab_method = XRToolsPickable.RangedMethod.NONE
    collision_layer = 4
    collision_mask = 196615

    var surface := MeshInstance3D.new()
    var sphere := SphereMesh.new()
    sphere.radius = RADIUS
    sphere.height = RADIUS * 2.0
    surface.mesh = sphere
    add_child(surface)

    var boundary := CollisionShape3D.new()
    var shape := SphereShape3D.new()
    shape.radius = RADIUS
    boundary.shape = shape
    add_child(boundary)

extends XRToolsPickable keeps the pickup behaviour supplied by XR Tools. Our class adds the sphere, its collider and the settings chosen in Coordinates. Inside this file, add_child(surface) attaches the surface to this point; there is no outer point. prefix.

_init() runs when we call .new(). It builds the point before the lesson adds it to the scene. We leave XR Tools' _ready() in place. The lesson has its own _ready() for placing the points and creating the readout.

Each instance gets its own mesh and collision shape. They begin with the same dimensions, but they are separate objects. The class contains no endpoint relation or trace: those belong to the lesson using it.

The complete continuation — line.gd

The point construction now lives in point_body.gd. This smaller lesson places two instances and reads their relation each frame. The coordinate readout stays here.

extends Node3D

const CENTRE := Vector3(0, 1.12, 0)

const PointBody = preload("point_body.gd")

const LineSkin = preload("line_skin.gd")

var point: XRToolsPickable
var readout: Label3D
var point_b: XRToolsPickable
var line: MeshInstance3D
var distance := 0.0

func _ready() -> void:
    point = PointBody.new()
    point.position = CENTRE
    add_child(point)

    readout = Label3D.new()
    readout.position = Vector3(0, 1.6, 0)
    readout.font_size = 36
    readout.pixel_size = 0.005
    add_child(readout)

    point_b = PointBody.new()
    point_b.position = CENTRE + Vector3(1.0, 0, 0)
    add_child(point_b)

    line = LineSkin.new()
    add_child(line)

func _process(_delta: float) -> void:
    var world_position := point.global_position
    var local_position := to_local(world_position)
    readout.text = "LOCAL " + str(local_position) + "\nWORLD " + str(world_position)

    var a := local_position
    var b := to_local(point_b.global_position)
    var displacement := b - a
    distance = displacement.length()
    line.show_segment(a, displacement, distance)
    readout.text += "\nDISTANCE " + str(distance)

Run it. Two equal points begin one metre apart. Carry either one: the pink segment follows, and DISTANCE changes. Release it: the endpoint stays where you left it.

One class, two instances

preload("point_body.gd") loads our script class and gives it the local name PointBody. Calling .new() twice makes two instances. They share the construction and inherited pickup behaviour; each has its own position, surface and collider. Carry A, then B. Moving one must leave the other where you put it.

point is our first endpoint, A. point_b is B. The references live outside _ready(), so _process() can read them too. preload("line_skin.gd") loads the drawing helper printed below; LineSkin.new() creates its visible segment.

The class holds what makes this a pickable point. The station holds what makes these two points a line. We can later extend point behaviour in one file without moving distance calculations or a position history into it.

Coordinates already produced local_position for the first point. Here a keeps that value. to_local(point_b.global_position) expresses B in the same PointStudy frame. Now we can subtract like from like.

b - a is the displacement from A to B: initially (1, 0, 0). Its .length() returns 1.0. The displacement holds direction and length; the distance holds length alone. The final += adds a DISTANCE line to the coordinate text written earlier in that frame.

The drawing helper receives A, the displacement and its length. It draws a thin cylinder between the ends. Each frame reads the bodies again; the line remains a relation between their current positions.

Change B's starting offset from Vector3(1.0, 0, 0) to Vector3(0, 1.0, 0). Predict the distance, then run. The line stands upright and keeps its length. Restore the original offset.

Try a - b instead of b - a. The distance stays the same, but the cylinder now points the opposite way from A and misses B. Subtraction has an order. Restore b - a.

The supplied drawing code — line_skin.gd

A mathematical line has no thickness. Our example joins two endpoints into a finite segment; this helper gives that segment a surface. It supplies no collider or pickup behaviour.

Save the following beside line.gd. It places the cylinder halfway between the ends and turns its long axis along their displacement. The orientation calculation is supplied for now; basis vectors are not a prerequisite for this lesson. The complete code makes that borrowed part available to inspect and reuse.

# Supplied renderer, adapted from commons/primitives/line/line.gd.
# This finite cylinder gives the segment a visible thickness. It is not a collider.
# Inputs share the parent station's local frame; that station has unit scale.
extends MeshInstance3D

func _init() -> void:
    var cylinder := CylinderMesh.new()
    cylinder.top_radius = 0.012
    cylinder.bottom_radius = 0.012
    cylinder.radial_segments = 16
    cylinder.rings = 0
    mesh = cylinder
    var finish := StandardMaterial3D.new()
    finish.albedo_color = Color(0.98, 0.70, 0.82)
    finish.roughness = 0.3
    material_override = finish
    visible = false

func show_segment(start: Vector3, displacement: Vector3, distance: float) -> void:
    # A zero-length segment has no direction. Do not normalize it.
    visible = distance > 0.0001
    if not visible:
        return
    (mesh as CylinderMesh).height = distance
    position = start + displacement * 0.5
    # CylinderMesh runs along local Y. This borrowed basis aligns it to the ends.
    var direction := displacement / distance
    var right := direction.cross(Vector3.UP).normalized()
    if right.length() < 0.001:
        right = Vector3.RIGHT
    var depth := right.cross(direction).normalized()
    basis = Basis(right, direction, depth)

Before adding another line, change var displacement := b - a to var displacement := a - b. The distance stays the same, but the segment now extends from A in the opposite direction and misses B. The subtraction drives the drawing, not only its readout. Restore b - a.

What can two lines do?

Look at the parallel planks, the plus and the X in the hall. A second segment gives us something to arrange in relation to the first. Add this block in _ready(), immediately after add_child(line):

    point.position = Vector3(-0.6, 1.42, 0)
    point_b.position = Vector3(0.6, 1.42, 0)
    var c := Vector3(0, 0.82, 0)
    var d := Vector3(0, 2.02, 0)
    var other_line := LineSkin.new()
    add_child(other_line)
    other_line.show_segment(c, d - c, (d - c).length())

This makes a plus. A and B still belong to held bodies. C and D are position values: we have given them no spheres or pickup bodies. The second segment is drawn once in _ready() and stays while you move the first. An endpoint can define a relation without becoming a separate object we can grab.

Change those four assignments to try another arrangement. A means point.position; B means point_b.position. C and D are the two vector values. All use the same station frame.

Arrangement A B C D
Parallel (-0.6, 1.12, 0) (0.6, 1.12, 0) (-0.6, 1.62, 0) (0.6, 1.62, 0)
Plus (-0.6, 1.42, 0) (0.6, 1.42, 0) (0, 0.82, 0) (0, 2.02, 0)
X (-0.6, 0.82, 0) (0.6, 2.02, 0) (-0.6, 2.02, 0) (0.6, 0.82, 0)

Make an X. Then set the Z component of both C and D to 0.2. Viewed straight on, the lines can still look crossed. Step sideways: they occupy different depths. No shared vertex has been created where the drawings cross. We still have four endpoints and two relations.

Choose an arrangement of your own. Predict which endpoints need to move, then make it. Can you keep both lengths while changing what the pair suggests?

What the line keeps

Return to the one-line version. Replace CENTRE + Vector3(1.0, 0, 0) with CENTRE for B's initial position. Both ends now occupy the same place: displacement and distance are zero. The helper hides spans of 0.0001 units or less; it cannot orient a segment with no direction.

Restore the offset. Carry B around a detour and return it to its starting place. The line returns to the same span. It has kept no journey.

Keep point_body.gd, line.gd and line_skin.gd. Trace will keep more positions, in order, so that a detour can remain.

Lines, Networks, Measure

The point gave us somewhere to begin. Now put two positions into relation. The point you could carry becomes an end: moving it changes a span. Soon that span will ask you to walk, forbid you to cross, or gather with another line into a sign.

In the black room, a pale line hangs between two small points. Take one end and move it. The line grows longer, turns, shortens. Release it. The end stays where your hand left it, still joined to the other.

A number on the wall follows the distance between them. Try changing the direction without changing that number. The dark room leaves very little else to look at: two positions, and what happens between them.

Can you find a span of one metre? Keep one end still and move the other around it. The segment turns while its length stays steady. The number can remain the same while the line points somewhere else. What has the measurement kept, and what has it left for your eye and hand to follow?

Your hand may also keep moving while the rounded number on the wall appears still. A display has its own smallest difference.

Through the side passage: CAN YOU CONNECT THE DOTS?

Two black points wait beside a barrier: DO NOT CROSS. Bring one close to the other, then let go. Watch for the segment that joins them. What else moved?

The barrier flies apart.

Keep hold of this small beginning: two distinct points determine a straight line.1 Here we draw only the part between them: a segment. Move an endpoint. The segment changes length and direction while the connection remains.

Already something else has attached itself to the lesson. Joining two points has opened a passage.

Try crossing where the barrier stood. Before it broke, an invisible collision shape filled the space from the floor to the bar. The space looked open beneath the plank, but you could not pass through it. When it breaks, that boundary stops holding too. Disconnecting the points does not close the passage. After a short interval, the barrier returns, waiting if your body is still in its place.

Connecting the points sends another instruction to the barrier. Someone joined those events in code. Geometry supplies a relation; another instruction gives that relation a consequence for your body. We could have made the connection ring a bell, or left the barrier standing.

The words, the plank and the collision boundary all helped to say DO NOT CROSS. Opening the way required changing what enforced that instruction. For a moment, a line has given us passage through another line's prohibition.

In Coordinates, leaving the target made another arrival possible. Here, joining the points gives us a few seconds of passage. The clock has acquired another task: it puts a prohibition back.

A red line marks another division. In speech, a red line can name the limit beyond which we refuse to go. Here the phrase has a colour and a place in the room. Before touching it, you may already have supplied the warning.

The line gathers these associations as we move: a connection, a border, a promise to go no further. The words beside it, the bodies it separates and what happens when we cross give its thin shape different force. What, here, gives it the right to stop you?

Ahead, the instruction moves from a barrier to the floor.

Across the glass cover of a shallow basin, a black stripe: WALK THIS LINE. The glass supports your crossing.

Try following it. Then walk alongside it. Cross it at an angle. The stripe stays straight through all three journeys.

Did a sideways step feel like a mistake? The marking does not measure how closely you follow it. Its script draws the stripe and lettering. Yet you may already have corrected a step in answer to the words.3 You might also find yourself dancing around the same steady line.

Choose which end to start from, and the line gives you a direction to follow. It cannot settle what following will mean to a body.

Turn and look at the route your movement has left behind. Compare it with the stripe. Where do the two lines part company?4 One was waiting for you; the other has taken shape while you moved.

The recorder saves positions and joins them with straight segments. The X and Z come from your headset; the line is drawn near floor level. A lean can change the drawing even when your feet stay put.

Choose a start and an end. Walk between them with a detour. Imagine walking directly between those same positions instead. The span between the endpoints is the same for both journeys. It contains no record of the turn you took.

The trace has kept more positions. How many did it need? What happened between the ones it kept?

A body for the line

After the crossing, the hall opens into four views: pairs, crosses, uprights and a grid. A familiar mark begins to acquire other uses.2

The point needed a sphere before we could see it. Here a line takes the form of a stick, a plank, a luminous rod, a measure, a beam. The mathematical line has no width. Each of these has enough width to appear, and a shape the hand can find.

We might call this a conceptual skin: the body we give an idea so that we can encounter it. Look at what comes with that gift. The plank has a broad face and a narrow edge. The ruler divides its length into intervals. The light can seem to have hardly any substance, although this one is a mesh with a pickup body. Their common direction does not tell us what it will be like to hold them.

The barrier already gave this distinction consequences. Its visible plank and its invisible collision shape occupied different amounts of space. Here the thin rods have slightly wider pickup shapes so a hand can find them. What we can see and what we can touch are being decided together, but they need not coincide.

Two long black lines run along the floor, with equal intervals marked beside them. Walk between them. Their distance apart stays the same, although the gap seems to narrow ahead. A pair of lines can suggest depth in a flat drawing. Here you can walk into the depth they describe.

Look up at the edge of the ceiling. A shorter length hangs nearer to you. Move a little sideways until it seems to join the edge. A lower or taller eye finds that agreement from another place. Then take another step. The line comes away from the ceiling and hangs over the room again.

The two objects have stayed still. Your movement let them become one edge, then gave them back their distance. What else in the room is held together by the place from which you see it?

Four views

What can two lines become together? Two sticks make a plus. Further along, two of the same length make an X. From the marked starting position, each reads as one familiar sign. Step sideways. The sticks sit in slightly different depth planes; the crossing belongs to your view. Take one in your hand and bring it forward. How far can the sign come apart before you stop seeing it?

Beside them, two planks lie parallel. Walk between them. From one end the gap narrows into the distance; moving your head changes that appearance without moving either plank. Then move a plank. Now you have changed the relation itself. The two graduated measures beyond them give the hand another pair to compare.

In the third view, two beams stand upright. A thin luminous horizon crosses the space behind them. Your own upright posture has company. Turn one beam in your hand and keep it there. The room does not correct it when you let go.

The fourth view begins with six lines: three in each direction. Together they suggest a plane divided into places. Pull one line towards you. Its crossings separate as you move around it; a regular drawing opens into depth. Behind it, three more bars already occupy different directions. There are edges here without a surface stretched between them.

The fixed lines on the walls keep another version of these arrangements. Your hands can change the bodies in the room while the wall keeps its own arrangement.

Take the measuring laser. Its number ends at the body the beam meets. Keep it on a rod and the rod breaks; the reading reaches whatever lies behind it. The instrument that measured a distance has removed one of its limits.

The hammer can break these studies too; the pink gun leaves them coloured for a while. Wait, and the study returns. Take the rod again. Its ability to come back belongs to the room we have written, as much as its ability to resist a hand.

Follow this line

On the floor: FOLLOW THIS LINE.

The mark reaches the wall. It turns upwards and continues across the plaster. You can follow it with your eyes. Your next step meets the wall's collision boundary.

The earlier connection opened a barrier. This line opens nothing. An instruction has reached the limit of the body to which it was addressed. Do you stop, turn, look up? The mark remains available to another kind of following.

At the exit the passage narrows: × + × + × +. Cross the first X and your health falls.

The plus gives some back, opening into a brief scatter of light and a rising sound. Hurt, recovery, hurt, recovery. Two crossed lines can read as a refusal or an offer of care; here those associations have been given access to your body.

Look back at the route you have left among these arrangements. The question taking us towards the exit has become precise: what must we save if we want to keep something of the journey?

Two endpoints were enough for the segment. To remember the detour, we will need to add something.

Bring the detour.
