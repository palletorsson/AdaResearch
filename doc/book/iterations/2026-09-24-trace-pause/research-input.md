I think this is one of the central problems for artistic research now. AI changes the **scarcity structure of knowledge**.

When you began Ada Research, a great deal of understanding was embedded in the difficulty of making something work. To reconstruct an algorithm, implement Perlin noise, recreate a Vera Molnár drawing, move from a mathematical description to code and then to an image—you had to pass through the material. The friction of implementation forced you to understand at least part of the system.

AI removes much of that friction.

You can now say: *take this Molnár work, infer the generative principle, produce variants, turn it into a three-dimensional environment, write it in Godot, explain the mathematics, add interaction.* A few minutes later you have something that previously might have represented weeks of work.

That is extraordinary—but it creates a strange epistemological inversion:

**We increasingly possess things before we understand them.**

And perhaps that is the important problem.

The old sequence was roughly:

**question → study → understanding → construction → result**

AI increasingly gives us:

**question → result → ??? → understanding**

So the pedagogical and artistic task becomes filling in that `???`.

### The solution is no longer the end of the problem

This is why I wouldn't say AI "solves all the problems." It produces solutions—or things that look like solutions—at enormous speed.

But a solution is not the same thing as understanding.

Imagine AI gives you a beautiful 3D reconstruction of a Vera Molnár work. There are suddenly many interesting questions that were previously obscured by the technical problem of making it:

* What is the actual generative rule?
* Which properties are essential and which are incidental?
* What does it mean to translate a 2D algorithm into 3D?
* Is extrusion enough?
* What happens to randomness?
* What happens to the grid?
* What happens to authorship?
* What transformations would make it no longer "Molnár"?
* Why did Molnár choose one realization from thousands of possible realizations?
* Can you recognize when the AI has misunderstood the work?

Those questions are arguably **more interesting than reproducing the image**.

The technical obstacle used to protect these questions by making entry difficult. Now that protection is disappearing.

So we need another kind of friction.

### From productive friction to epistemic friction

I think Ada Research could deliberately move its friction.

Not:

> Can you program this?

but:

> Can you understand what has been programmed?

And then:

> Can you disturb it?

This could become a very strong principle for the project.

AI can generate a reaction-diffusion system immediately. Fine. Then the encounter begins.

Change one parameter.

Predict what will happen **before** running it.

Remove diffusion.

Reverse two values.

Ask what assumptions the model contains.

Find an edge case.

Make the system fail.

Translate it into another medium.

Explain it without mathematical terminology.

Explain it mathematically.

Find something the AI's explanation doesn't account for.

Now you're no longer testing someone's ability to reproduce syntax. You're testing whether they have acquired a **mental model**.

That might actually be a deeper form of algorithmic literacy.

### Curiosity becomes more important, not less

There's another shift here.

When answers were expensive, education could organize itself around obtaining answers.

When answers become nearly free, the scarce thing becomes **attention, judgment and good questions**.

The ability to ask:

> Why is it doing that?

becomes more important than knowing how to make it do that.

And then:

> What would happen if…?

That second question is particularly important.

It turns AI from an answer machine into an **experimental apparatus**.

You don't have to accept the thousand objects AI puts in your lap. You can treat them as specimens.

Generate ten interpretations.

Compare them.

Find their invariants.

Find their contradictions.

Ask why one feels wrong.

Ask what all ten failed to notice.

Now abundance itself becomes a research material.

### There is also a new kind of ignorance

Previously you might encounter something you couldn't make.

Now you encounter something you **can make but cannot explain**.

That's a qualitatively different condition.

You could have:

* a shader you couldn't write,
* a cellular automaton you couldn't derive,
* a neural network you couldn't implement,
* a mathematical object you barely understand,

and nevertheless manipulate all of them fluently.

That produces what we might call **operational knowledge without conceptual knowledge**.

And perhaps Ada Research can explicitly inhabit that condition rather than trying to pretend it doesn't exist.

The player could repeatedly encounter objects that function perfectly before they understand them.

The challenge isn't unlocking the object.

The object is already unlocked.

The challenge is gradually discovering **what you are actually looking at**.

That is very contemporary.

### Code itself changes status

Your observation that you may already have been writing your "last lines of code" when you started Ada Research is particularly interesting.

Because code used to occupy two positions simultaneously:

**code as instrument**
and
**code as knowledge**.

Writing the algorithm was one way of understanding the algorithm.

AI separates those two.

Code increasingly becomes something we can summon as infrastructure. Therefore the intellectual object moves one layer upward:

from syntax
→ algorithm
→ model
→ assumptions
→ relations
→ consequences.

Knowing `for` loops matters less.

Knowing that something is iterative matters enormously.

Knowing how to implement Perlin noise from scratch matters less.

Understanding why continuous pseudo-randomness behaves differently from independent randomness still matters.

Knowing the exact Gray–Scott implementation matters less.

Understanding why local interactions produce global morphology becomes more interesting.

AI hasn't removed the knowledge field.

**It has exposed it by removing some of the scaffolding surrounding it.**

### Ada Research could therefore make a deliberate move

Maybe the project isn't ultimately an encyclopedia of algorithms.

It becomes an encyclopedia of **ways of knowing algorithms**.

Each room might contain several layers:

**Encounter** — something happens.

**Manipulation** — I can change it.

**Prediction** — what do I think will happen?

**Explanation** — why did it happen?

**Translation** — what happens when I move it into another representation?

**Failure** — where does this model stop working?

**History** — why did somebody invent this representation in the first place?

**Politics / ontology** — what does the representation permit us to see, and what does it erase?

Then AI becomes almost perfect for Ada Research, because it can endlessly produce implementations, variations and explanations.

The artwork supplies what AI cannot automatically determine for us:

**where to look.**

And perhaps the core educational move becomes:

> **Don't make the learner produce the answer. Make the learner develop a relationship to the answer.**

That relationship can include suspicion, fascination, prediction, play, disagreement, modification, destruction, reconstruction.

That is how you keep curiosity alive in a world in which production becomes almost effortless.

And there is a beautiful connection to the ideas you've already been developing around compression, grids, traces and entropy: AI gives us an enormous compression of human knowledge into immediately executable forms. But compression always loses something. The interesting question becomes **what disappeared between the problem and the answer**.

Ada Research could live precisely there: **inside the information that the solution compressed away.**
