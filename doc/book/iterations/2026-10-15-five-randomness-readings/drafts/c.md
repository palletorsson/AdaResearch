# A suspicion about the centre

Suppose the middle of the paper grows dark. We have been following a bead along a metre of rod, and transparent circles have been collecting behind it. Our first suspicion is that the machine prefers the centre.

We take our hand away. The hand visible in the room resumes our movement, the bead returns to its waiting place, and the marks stay available. Our viewpoint has been still throughout. The drawing has acquired an advantage over the moving bead: we can look at several parts of the journey together.

The display says the targets are uniform. Equal intervals have equal chances of receiving a destination. But the marks were not made only at destinations. Twelve positions within every segment have been transferred to the paper. A journey between two targets can pass through places neither target occupies.

Our suspicion has become more precise. Which part of this darkness belongs to the selected destinations, and which part to the way we travel between them? The surface alone has not separated those contributions.

The other rows offer a comparison. Rows two, three and four double the preceding pace while following the same horizontal positions. We can compare equal numbers of completed segments. The marks agree, though they have arrived at different times. A change that mattered while following the bead can disappear from the completed drawing.

Row five introduces vertical movement at the second row's pace. Its marks spread into another dimension. The paper now makes a difference available that a statement about the shared seed would not describe. We have begun asking about the apparatus between a number and its visible consequence.

The falling page complicates the question by making selection available to our hand. Groups of five digits descend. We touch one, and it becomes amber and stationary while the rest continue. The instruction on the small stand is simply “select”.

The held group looks important because it is the one we can continue reading. It was generated before we touched it. We have changed the duration of its appearance, and with that the material on which our attention is likely to work.

The display's name recalls RAND's random-number tables. Its figures are its own generated stream. A historical table and this falling page both offer numbers for examination, but an account of either must identify how its numbers were produced and how the examined sample was chosen.

We could keep returning to our amber group and overlook what passes around it. We could release it and choose another. A claim about the whole flow would need more than the fact that these five figures caught our attention. The hand that helps us read has also made a selection the account must remember.

The Geiger machine makes a different impression: clicks with irregular gaps. Nothing in the sound immediately announces a returning sequence. Then RESTART supplies a way to compare.

We hear the opening again. The screen gives a seed, 435. A seed is the number that establishes this generator's starting state. Its successive calculations produce waiting intervals; the resulting ninety-six event times are stored before they become sound. This cabinet detects no radiation. The clicks are synthesized from that list.

The irregularity was available to hearing. The method of producing it needed other evidence. Pseudorandom generation names this repeatable calculation; it does not require us to anticipate each result by listening.

A saved receipt reports that 435 came from RANDOM.ORG's atmospheric-noise service on 13 October 2026. The receipt concerns an earlier acquisition. The current hall reuses its value across separate instrument procedures, with no fresh request on entry. That history cannot turn the cabinet into a radiation sensor.

Now keep the event times and change the resolution used to compare their intervals. At a coarser clock resolution, unequal durations can receive the same measured value. The bit procedure skips equal pairs. The sound can remain identical while its numerical account changes.

At the paper we had suspected the source because of its image. Here we can alter the account without altering the source events. That gives our first suspicion a more demanding companion: what has the instrument made available for us to distinguish?

MOVE / KEEP turns the question towards our own movement. We lift a small sphere and rotate it beneath six coloured traces. After enough valid measurements, release produces a seed between zero and 999.

Positions, orientations and elapsed times have been recorded. The apparatus rounds the measurements and hashes their encoded list, then reduces the result to one of a thousand seeds. The exact retained list can yield that seed again. The original gesture contains more distinctions than this small range of results can preserve.

Different recordings must sometimes share an output. That mathematical possibility is not a pair of demonstrated gestures. Finding and keeping such a pair would let us examine what remained different in the traces while becoming equivalent for the generator. Repeating a hand movement approximately would not establish the comparison; we would need both actual records.

At the larger sphere, holding changes the position of the body itself. We join an excursion and watch the room move past. Its direction and distance come from the generator. Its return comes from the route the program has given it.

Another seed could change where we go. It would leave that required return intact. Releasing ends our ride, and the sphere completes its journey without us.

We have followed the numbers as far as they are allowed to act. The next investigation would have to alter the rule that assigns them their consequence. Our first suspicious patch of darkness was useful: it made the apparatus visible between chance and its form.
