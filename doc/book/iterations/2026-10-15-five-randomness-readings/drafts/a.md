# After the hand withdraws

<!-- @random_touch -->

The bead returns to its place. On the pale wall behind it, a darkening remains.

We have only just taken our hand away. A moment ago the hand visible in the room was following the bead along a thin metal rod, slowing towards one position and setting off towards another. Our viewpoint stayed still. Now the visible hand follows our own movement again, while the bead completes its small journey home. The marks have become easier to look at.

They are transparent circles. One scarcely covers the paper; several laid over one another begin to make a place dark. Each segment of the bead's journey contributes twelve positions to this surface. Some lie between the destinations the program selected. We are looking at where movement passed, as well as where it was going.

There are five rods, each a metre long. We begin at the first and work down. The second takes the same sideways path twice as quickly. The third doubles that pace, and the fourth doubles it again. The rods have not grown, but there is less time inside the same distance. The marks arrive sooner. If we let each bead complete the same number of segments, their horizontal patterns agree.

At the fifth rod, the pace returns to that of the second. This time the visible hand also rises and falls. The accumulating circles spread away from the line. What had looked like a narrow place to move becomes a small field.

We leave the marks there. A crowded patch tempts an explanation: perhaps the random destinations favour it. Yet the destinations were chosen uniformly, with equal chances for equal intervals. Their connecting journeys have also entered the picture. Darkness gives us something to investigate, without telling us on its own which procedure made it.

Farther into the room, numbers descend across a large page. A stand says “select”. Each group has five digits; another row enters from above every two and a half seconds. We touch one group. It turns amber and remains at its address while the other groups continue downwards.

Our hand leaves. This time nothing returns to centre. The selected digits stay.

The page recalls RAND's *A Million Random Digits*, a supply made available for calculations using random selections. This display generates its own digits. We have not reached into the old book and rescued a line of its printing. [RAND's account](https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1418.html).

Still, the page permits a kind of reading that the fall was making difficult. We can return to the same five figures while new ones pass around them. Touching again releases them. The hand can decide how long a result remains available, even when it has had no part in producing that result.

<!-- @geiger_counter -->

Click. A gap. Another click.

The Geiger cabinet is already working. Its screen shows 435. We press RESTART and hear the opening gaps again. The indicator flashes beside a timeline whose positions have already been marked.

This machine has no radiation detector. It calculates waiting times and synthesizes the clicks from them. All ninety-six event times exist before playback begins. The seed, 435, sets the generator's starting state; its repeatable calculation supplies the values that become those intervals. This is pseudorandom generation. A fixed procedure can remain difficult for us to anticipate.

Beside this repeating present is a dated record:

> 13 October 2026, 14:29:02 UTC. RANDOM.ORG. Atmospheric noise. Value kept: 435.

That is what the saved receipt reports. The number is reused when the hall opens. These clicks bring no new sample of the atmosphere into the room.

The window, lamps and opening beads also begin from the shared seed. Each has its own procedure. We have been seeing several consequences of a number before learning how the room keeps it. A different seed can be chosen deliberately; resetting to 435 recalls this beginning.

At MOVE / KEEP, six coloured traces wait above a small dark sphere. We lift and turn it. Position and rotation enter the traces. With a sufficient recording, release produces a whole number between zero and 999.

The apparatus keeps timed measurements, rounds them, then passes their encoded values through a fixed hashing procedure. Its result is reduced to one of a thousand seeds. The recording can derive the same number again. A new gesture begins another recording, even if we intend to repeat the old one.

We have arrived with a movement that did not exist when the hall opened. It can change the seed used by the other instruments. Their calculations remain pseudorandom. Two different recordings could yield the same seed; we have no kept pair here that demonstrates it. The possible comparison is waiting for work.

The last dark sphere moves on a larger scale. Holding it brings our viewpoint into motion with the body. The room passes differently now: an excursion we can join, then leave.

The sphere chooses a direction and distance within its range. It always turns back. We release it before the return is finished and watch it continue alone. Our body is free of the carry; the sphere still has its route to complete.

The place where we let go has no mark of its own. The abacus had left a surface we could examine after withdrawing. Here the movement ends without such a wall. We find ourselves wanting to keep that position.
