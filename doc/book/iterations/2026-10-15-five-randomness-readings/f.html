How long a result remains

At first there is time to wait. Five dark beads sit at the centres of five thin rods, a pale paper behind them, one large button below: REPLAY. Nothing begins because we have arrived.

We touch the upper bead. The hand visible in the room goes sideways with it; our viewpoint stays where it was. A segment of the journey takes two and a half seconds. The bead slows toward a destination, and a new segment begins. Faint translucent circles gather on the paper, twelve to a segment, and where they overlap the paper darkens.

The centre darkens first. Our first suspicion arrives with it: perhaps the machine prefers the centre.

We withdraw. The visible hand returns to our own movement; the bead goes home alone; the marks stay. The drawing has acquired an advantage over the moving bead — we can look at several parts of the journey together — and the suspicion can be examined at leisure.

The display says the destinations are uniform: equal intervals, equal chances. But the circles were not made only at destinations. Twelve positions inside every segment have reached the paper, and a journey between two targets passes through places neither target occupies. The suspicion becomes more precise. Which part of this darkness belongs to the selected destinations, and which to the way the bead travels between them?

The next three rods keep the same horizontal destinations and double the preceding pace, row by row. A segment takes one and a quarter seconds, then less, then less again. The paper fills sooner. Yet after equal numbers of segments, the horizontal marks agree. A change that mattered while following the bead can disappear from the completed drawing.

The fifth rod returns to the second row's pace and lets the bead rise and fall as it travels. The marks spread into a small field. The pace is familiar while the space has changed.

REPLAY clears the paper and restores the paths. The waiting bead does not retain our impatience.

Elsewhere in the room, a page admits a row of digits from its top every two and a half seconds. The cadence is familiar; the mechanism is its own. It has not been listening to the bead.

The digits come in groups of five. A stand says “select”. We touch a group and it turns amber and stops at its address while the rows continue around it. We lower our hand; the group remains. Its persistence no longer requires our contact. A second touch would release it — the authority to hold is also the authority to let go.

The page is named for RAND's A Million Random Digits, though these digits are generated here. Producing that 1955 supply required testing and correction: the electronic source introduced biases that had to be found and transformed before the table could stand. RAND's production account. A published page kept numbers available at an address. This page first makes them pass, then gives the hand a way to keep some of them in view.

We read the amber group once, and then once more while another row descends. Nothing in the five figures records how long the second reading took. And the suspicion from the paper has followed us here: the hand that helps us read has also made a selection the account must remember.

Click.

A gap.

Another click.

The Geiger cabinet gives us intervals we cannot get from the page's cadence. Its screen reads 435, with a count of events played; the timeline carries marks ahead of the moving indicator. Ninety-six detection times were calculated before this playback began. No radiation is being detected. The seed, 435, set a generator's starting state; the repeatable calculation advanced from there and supplied every waiting time. This is pseudorandom generation.

We press RESTART and stay. The opening gap returns, then the next. Recognition has its own pace, and listening again has added nothing to the recording — the repetition was in the room before we asked for it.

Beside the clicks is a dated record:

Received: 13 October 2026, 14:29:02 UTC Provider: RANDOM.ORG · atmospheric noise Requested range: 0–999 · value kept: 435

The receipt reports a time that playback will never reach. The hall reuses the value it kept; no new sample of the atmosphere enters with these clicks. The document and the clicks have different jobs.

By now the suspicion has learned to ask about instruments. Keep the ninety-six times and make the comparison clock coarser: measured intervals that become equal are skipped by the bit-producing procedure, and a distinction disappears from that account while the sound still carries it. What has the instrument made available for us to distinguish? The question we first asked of a darkened paper can be asked of any readout in the room.

Another seed can be chosen deliberately; the window, the lamps and the beads would begin again from that choice. Reset recalls 435. A beginning, too, is something the room lets us keep.

At MOVE / KEEP, duration enters with our hand. We lift a small sphere beneath six coloured traces and move it, turn it. Positions, orientations and times are recorded. Too little valid movement leaves the previous seed in place; a sufficient recording yields, on release, a whole number between zero and 999.

The instrument rounds its measurements, hashes the encoded record, and reduces the result to one of a thousand values. The exact record can derive its number again; another attempt at the same gesture makes another record. The gesture holds more distinctions than this small range of results can preserve, so different recordings must sometimes share an output — a mathematical certainty for which we hold no kept pair. Finding one would be work worth keeping.

The number becomes the hall's new beginning. What we did during the visit has reached the instruments' beginnings. It has not reached their procedures.

The larger sphere waits at its centre, then offers an excursion: a few seconds outward, the same again back, direction and distance drawn within its range. Holding it carries the body; the room passes differently. We release before the return is finished, and the sphere completes the remaining time alone.

The place where we let go has no mark of its own. The paper kept a surface we could examine after withdrawing; here the movement ends without one, and we find ourselves wanting to keep that position. No counter on the sphere says how long we should take before leaving.

We can now name something the room does not let chance decide. The journey may vary inside its route; return is compulsory for the sphere, and a different draw has no power to repeal it. The requirement was placed there by the makers. If we wanted a sphere that sometimes stays away, we would have to alter that requirement and encounter what follows.

This room's small constitution is still a draft. Its last clause would concern who may revise the preceding ones. We are standing close enough to the moving object to begin asking what such a revision should allow.
