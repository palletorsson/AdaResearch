## What these notes do not yet solve

They are primarily a guide to **how each chapter unfolds**. That is necessary, but easier reading will also need work at the sentence and page level.

A passage can have an excellent route and still burden the reader with ambiguous pronouns, several new technical terms at once, or a rapid shift between a visible object, its data and its collision body. Shortening every sentence will not solve that. Sometimes the easier sentence is longer because it makes the relationship explicit.

I would therefore keep two distinct editing passes:

**First, the encounter:** What attracts attention? What happens? When does explanation become useful? Where can the reader stay without being instructed?

**Then, the prose:** Can the reader identify what each sentence refers to? Does the next sentence develop the previous one, or require another mental turn? Is a memorable formulation surrounded by sufficiently ordinary language?

The standalone reader also needs consideration. These notes often rely productively on walking, waiting, listening and comparing. On the page, a reader may need a concrete spatial description or a well-placed image to make that imagined encounter possible. Otherwise “look at the other one” remains easy in the museum and difficult in the book.

## The principle I would put above the whole collection

> **Keep the precision of the investigation, but vary the experience of reading it. A passage may teach, describe, surprise, amuse, accompany or leave something unresolved. It does not have to perform all of those jobs before it ends.**

That is what these notes are beginning to achieve. Their repeated private headings can help an editor check the work; those headings should remain backstage rather than dictate the rhythm of the finished chapters.
