# A pattern that returns

<!-- A1 -->
The right-hand picture changes. The left stays as it was. Above them, the seed still has the same number. You have pressed +1 DRAW, and the small patch you were watching has lost its colours. The cubes remain in their cells, as though nothing of consequence has happened.

<!-- A2 -->
A moment earlier the two grids agreed. Perhaps you had found an orange beside a violet that you wanted to keep. REPLAY brought the arrangement back, including that unplanned pleasure. The score from Cage’s lesson could be heard again. This bench seems to make a smaller promise: keep one number, and the picture can return.

<!-- A3 -->
The number begins a procedure. A pseudorandom generator advances through states by a deterministic rule; a seed sets its starting state. With the same generator and sequence of requests, it supplies the same values again. The picture requires another agreement: those values must be given the same jobs. Here three successive draws become the red, green and blue amounts of each cube. One discarded draw shifts every following group. Nothing in a draw says that it is red.

<!-- A4 -->
Look at the changed patch before restoring it. You might prefer this neighbour, or find the whole panel suddenly unpleasant. The right grid has stopped being a successful copy; it can still be a picture worth keeping. The instrument is comparing sameness. Your attention has already found something else to compare. Neither preference changes what the generator did.

<!-- A5 -->
Nearby, a number-book page recalls RAND’s *A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates*, published in 1955. Researchers needed samples for Monte Carlo calculations. An electronic roulette device produced digits in 1947; testing found biases, and the table was transformed before publication. Prepared for punched-card machines, this chance had passed through considerable work.[^rand]

<!-- A6 -->
A fixed table can be opened again at the same place. Our generator calculates its sequence again. Both offer a return, provided we also keep track of how we take the next number. The museum’s page complicates that comparison: its digits flow downward and disappear. Touch can hold a cell while the others continue. The page resembles a book, but try reopening a line that has already fallen away.

<!-- A7 -->
At the three textile panels, follow one cell across the display. The same draws have been assigned different pairs of marks: diagonals, upright and horizontal bars, large and small blocks. A choice that leaned one way now stands up or occupies more of its square. The marks change the figure you can see in it. Each panel has supplied a vocabulary before chance gets to choose.

<!-- A8 -->
The coloured cubes are equally well provided for. They can change colour without ever being asked to leave their places. Here, randomness varies what the program has made variable. Suppose a few draws were given another job: choosing which cubes could slip out of the panel. This is an experiment we could build. A corner might lengthen into a sleeve, dragging its last square across the floor while you try to put your hand through it.

<!-- A9 -->
We would have to decide how it follows a wrist, where it catches, whether its weight can pull. A dress code begins accumulating around the sleeve. Yet the wish to wear the diagram has changed what we are asking of it. A colour arrangement that pleased us has become a possible companion for a body. The existing bench cannot make that move for us.

<!-- A10 -->
Return to the patch you chose. In the next hall, colours will be gathered into counts, and a gauge will read their shares. Your pleasure may have depended on who stood beside whom. Carry that relation with you when the number appears.

[^rand]: [RAND, introduction and production account](https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1418.html). The historical table and the museum’s flowing representation have different behaviours.
