# A pattern that returns

<!-- B1 -->
Find a patch you would like to see again. An orange beside a violet, perhaps, or a small awkward corner that seems to belong to another picture. The two grids offer it twice. REPLAY offers it again. The score from Cage’s lesson could be heard again; here an accidental pleasure is beginning to acquire an address.

<!-- B2 -->
A nearby page recalls RAND’s *A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates*, published in 1955. Its tables were prepared for punched-card machines and for researchers using Monte Carlo calculations. A fixed page could be revisited. You could find the same chance where you had left it.[^rand]

<!-- B3 -->
The seed bench returns by a different route. A pseudorandom generator advances through states by a deterministic rule; a seed sets its starting state. The same generator, asked in the same order, supplies the same values again. It calculates them. A printed table keeps them. Standing between the grids and the book, we have two ways to recover a sequence, and still no account of how a sequence becomes this particular picture.

<!-- B4 -->
Even the table had to be made. An electronic roulette device produced digits in 1947. Tests found biases; a transformation prepared the table for publication.[^rand] The book on a desk could conceal that labour as thoroughly as a seed readout conceals the succession of requests. A small surface presents the result of a much longer activity.

<!-- B5 -->
Our museum’s page lets its digits flow downward. A touch can hold a cell while its neighbours continue; departed lines cannot be reopened here. The resemblance to a book has led us to expect a kind of return the object does not supply. Look back at the seed. Perhaps we have also asked that number to keep more than it was given.

<!-- B6 -->
After +1 DRAW, the right grid changes and the left stays. The seed has not moved. One requested value was discarded before the colours were assigned; each following group of three now begins one draw later. Those three places supply red, green and blue. Nothing in a draw says that it is red. The returning picture depended on how the numbers were used. You may prefer the new patch, even while the bench reports a lost agreement.

<!-- B7 -->
The textile panels give that use a visible form. Follow a cell among diagonals, upright and horizontal bars, then large and small blocks. The same draws choose among different pairs of marks. What leaned becomes something that stands or fills a square. Stay long enough for a figure to emerge in one panel that you cannot find in the next. Chance was given a vocabulary; your eye is doing something with the resulting sentence.

<!-- B8 -->
The cube grid has its own vocabulary too. Colour may vary; a cube may not leave its cell. Here, randomness varies what the program has made variable. Suppose we gave a few draws the job of releasing cubes from the panel. This is an experiment we could build. A loose corner might become a sleeve. Its end could trail over the floor while you search for an opening wide enough for your hand.

<!-- B9 -->
That invitation would need decisions about following a wrist, catching and weight. A dress code gathers around the imagined garment. Still, wearing the diagram asks something new of an old object. The colours that made you stop have led toward a possible relation with your body. The bench can replay a patch; it cannot decide that this is what the patch should become.

<!-- B10 -->
Keep the patch in mind as you leave. The next hall gathers colours into counts and reads their shares. It can keep that account while their neighbours change. What survives of the relation you wanted back? The gauge will have an answer to its own question; bring yours beside it.

[^rand]: [RAND, introduction and production account](https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1418.html). The historical table and the museum’s flowing representation have different behaviours.
