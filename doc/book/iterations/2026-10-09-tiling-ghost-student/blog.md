A black point hovers beside a wall of coloured cubes. Pressing its trigger changes a mark in two places. The hand touches one occurrence, but the program finds the source entry that both occurrences read. The mark has travelled through a rule before it becomes available to the hand.

This is a small construction inside Endless Museum. The surrounding room already contains larger patterns, a comparison console and a working geometric loom. The student rebuilds a selected operation among them. The rest of the room remains available to explore.

![The reconstructed glide reader turned into a wall, with the familiar black point selecting a source entry through its repeated occurrence.](/book-images/iterations/2026-10-09-tiling-ghost-student/student-wall.png)

*Fresh Godot capture from the ghost-student run. The small learner display is temporarily placed inside Symmetry Seventeen. The larger installed comparison remains behind it.*

Tiling begins with material the student already knows: a point, an addressed cube grid and a palette. The first hall separates changing the colours from changing the pattern. Foundry gives the student a small source to edit. Then the reader changes, the receiving surface turns, and a reflection acquires a shift. At the temple, two receivers can agree once and develop separately afterwards. The volume takes a side path. A colour comparison gives us time to look before the last hall places a sampled pattern on a skirt.

The external review helped us recognise what to preserve. These elementary operations already carry the artistic inquiry. A local edit has a reach. A copy has a history. A surface changes how a repeated mark appears. We kept the nine halls and the shortened reflective texts, then clarified two obligations: the loom is optional, and the garment lesson asks the student to write a small sampling shader within a supplied studio.

That distinction matters to the book's voice. The tutorial has to say what the student can make and how to check it. The reflection can stay with the desire behind making it. In the textile passages, *Mamma Mönster* brings that desire close to the work: the hope that patterned clothing might change how people live and appear together. A working shader can establish where a colour is sampled. That hope remains open to an encounter, a memory or a disagreement.

For the ghost-student rehearsal, we extracted the current printed code into fresh files. No missing lesson code was borrowed from the production artifacts. Declared infrastructure remained available: the museum project, its player and XR Tools, and the garment studio. The three helper files—point, palette and grid—were identical across the first eight tutorials. Symmetry also carried the preceding receiver script unchanged.

All nine printed examples and their prescribed variations passed: eighteen runs and 647 assertions. Those were established checks run again against the fresh reconstruction. We then asked a new question. Could the glide accept a six-row, four-column source, and could that changed source enter the next lesson without losing the distinction between a source and its copies?

The prediction came first. Six rows would give a three-row glide. Painting one selected occurrence would change exactly two visible copies. Turning and enlarging the receiver would preserve the address. An explicit copy into the temple lesson would make two receivers agree; a later wall edit could stay local. The twelve checks passed. This transfer was deliberately made in the rehearsal. The museum does not automatically save one student-edited motif through every hall.

![The student's three-repeat shader running on the wall and preview skirt in the existing fabric studio.](/book-images/iterations/2026-10-09-tiling-ghost-student/student-three-vertical.png)

*Fresh Godot capture from Ribbon Patterns 03. The wall and preview use the changed shader. Saving its source does not install that shader on the wearer.*

The final hall makes a useful limit visible. Change the shader from two vertical repeats to three, then save and wear the fabric. The preview can show three while the worn skirt still uses two. The saved record contains supported settings; it does not contain the student's shader program. The tutorial names that boundary and gives the student a way to predict it. Extending the saved record would be another lesson.

We also ran the reconstructed studies inside two actual museum halls. Seventy-one checks covered installed controls, clear approaches, simulated XR pickup and release, and the relation between preview, saved fabric and wearer. The images here come from those runs. This was a rehearsal by the same assistant that knows the project, not a blind reader or a physical headset study. Existing project diagnostics are recorded with the evidence. Passing these checks establishes their particular claims; a new reader can still find difficulty the checker never imagined.

The loom keeps another kind of history. Each completed pass retains its crossing instructions after the draft changes. Its thread geometry does not become the skirt. Keeping that difference explicit gives both artifacts more to do: one lets us inspect successive construction; the other lets us investigate how a source is interpreted on a body.

The ghost student has made the writing more exact without deciding what a pattern should mean. We can name the file that returns, the operation that changes, the record that is copied and the part supplied by the museum. Then there is room to choose a mark, keep an unexpected result, or ask what we wanted this surface to make possible.
