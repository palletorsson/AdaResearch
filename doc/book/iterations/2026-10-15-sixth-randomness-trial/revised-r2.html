What chance is allowed to do

Sixth literary trial — sequence-context revision, 16 October 2026; not an adopted edition

The bead has come back to the middle of the rod. The paper behind it has not come back to white.

There are five rods, a metre each, with a dark bead waiting at the centre of every one. We touch the first. The hand visible in the room leaves the position of our tracked hand and follows the bead sideways. The rest of us stays where it was. A series of small departures takes place along the rod. One destination, then another, with a slowing and setting off between them. Behind the bead, pale circles begin to cover one another.

We try calling each departure before it happens. Some calls land; counted along the rod, no more of them than luck would explain. That failure can be measured, and it is the first measurement this room offers.

When we withdraw, the visible hand comes back to our movement. The bead returns alone. No new circles are made on this journey home. The old ones remain.

They appear to have collected unevenly. A dark place invites an explanation: perhaps the bead has been sent there more often. But the paper receives twelve marks along every segment, including the positions passed on the way to a destination. It is not a census of destinations. The targets were drawn uniformly, with equal chances for equal intervals within their range. The journeys between them have made their own distribution on the page.

The next three beads travel the same horizontal sequence at successively doubled rates. Nothing on their rods is farther away. At the fourth there is scarcely time to follow a turn before another is underway. Yet, after an equal number of segments, the paper bears the same horizontal pattern. The paper does not record the hurry.

The fifth bead moves at the second bead's pace, now also rising and falling. The hand follows into a direction the other rods did not require. The circles spread into a small field.

After withdrawing from the bead, we press the large button marked REPLAY. It clears the paper and restores the stored paths. The first circle can be made again. The patch of darkness need not appear as a surprise to the apparatus, even if we had not known where to look for it.

Farther along, the numbers have no paths drawn behind them. They arrive at the top of an upright page in groups of five and descend in rows. A small stand says select.

We touch a group. Its five figures turn amber and stop. The other rows continue moving, so that the chosen address becomes a place around which things pass. Taking away the hand does not release the group. Another touch will.

The paper behind the beads recorded what passed through it. This page lets us keep what had already arrived. Our intervention changes neither the five digits nor their source. It changes the time available to read them.

The display takes its name from RAND's A Million Random Digits, published in 1955 to supply numbers for random selections in calculation. The electronic source used to make that table showed biases. Testing and transformation intervened before the published numbers could be put on a page. The falling digits here are generated by this exhibit, not lifted from RAND's tables.

Most of the passing groups hold a repeated digit somewhere. Nothing has gone wrong. A table from which the doubles had been combed out would be a doctored table — RAND's correctors worked against bias, not against repetition. What chance is not allowed to do is look the way we expect it to.

For a moment the selected group seems more important than the thousands we do not stop. Its amber colour has made it easier to return to. That is a fact about our selection, not evidence that the group deserved it.

The five figures are still there when we turn away. We have kept a result; the procedure that made it remains out of view.

A cabinet is already clicking.

Click. A gap. Another click.

The cabinet's screen shows a number that has also been kept: 435. The five beads began from that number too, though they have made something very different of it.

The Geiger cabinet has no radiation detector. Its ninety-six detection times were calculated before the first sound was played. 435 is the seed, the number that set the generator's starting state. The calculation then advanced that state, supplying numbers from which the intervals were made. The clicks are synthesized at those retained times.

RESTART.

Click. The opening gap. Another click.

On a small dated record, the number has an earlier life:

13 October 2026, 14:29:02 UTC
RANDOM.ORG — atmospheric noise
Requested: 0–999
Kept: 435

This is what the saved receipt reports. The room does not ask the atmosphere to speak again. The receipt concerns an acquisition; the cabinet performs a calculation and then plays its result. Those are not the same event, however often 435 appears above them.

The cabinet can also read its own intervals against a coarser clock, measuring in larger steps. Two gaps of different lengths may then receive the same measured value, and a distinction the sound still carries goes missing from that account. Nothing in the gaps has changed — only the resources brought to them.

That is the definition this room has been circling. Random is not a property a sequence owns. It is a relation between the sequence and whatever meets it: a clock, a rule, a listener. The same ninety-six times are a riddle to our ear and a recipe to the generator.

At MOVE / KEEP, the next beginning comes from our hand.

A sphere waits beneath six coloured traces. We carry and turn it. The lines register positions and orientations over time. If we have moved it enough to make a valid recording, releasing it produces a number between zero and 999.

The apparatus has kept more than the number: positions and orientations recorded over time. A fixed calculation turns the rounded measurements into a whole number, then reduces it to one of a thousand possible seeds. The same retained record gives the same result again. A second gesture, however closely we try to copy the first, makes another record.

One thousand answers must serve more possible recordings than can be distinguished by those answers. Different traces can therefore lead to the same seed, though we have no demonstrated pair to place side by side. Such a pair would be worth keeping: two movements the traces can tell apart, followed by a generator that cannot tell their seeds apart.

At the rods, a sequence of numbers gave the hand a journey. Here a journey gives the other instruments a number. Neither exchange carries everything across.

The last sphere is large enough to take the rest of us with it.

We take hold and join an excursion already under way. This time the viewpoint moves. The sphere travels horizontally across the room for three and a half seconds, following a direction and distance selected by its generator. Its way back takes three and a half more. Before each excursion it waits at the centre.

We can release it at any point. The carry ends for our body; the sphere goes on. Nothing on the floor records the place where we parted.

A new seed can give it another direction, another distance. The return is not among the things the seed is allowed to change. But the return belongs to the sphere. Once released, we are not obliged to accompany it.

In this room chance was allowed a position, a duration, a destination — each inside a rule it did not write. The rooms ahead give it more to decide.

Here, randomness is domesticated.

The sphere comes back to centre.
