01. Cube

Read both texts together

The cube comes back

The cube rises. It slows near the top, turns and passes the place where we first saw it. Then it goes lower.

Wait for another return. The same faces are still there. In Transformation we changed where the cube was, how it faced us and how much space it occupied. Here a movement has acquired a rhythm. We can begin to expect its next arrival.

Find the middle of the movement. The cube passes it on the way up and again on the way down. A photograph taken there could conceal the difference. Stay a little longer and the direction becomes apparent. Returning to a place need not mean returning to the same moment in a movement.

The cube does not remember the height it just left. Its rule reads the clock and assigns another height. Nothing needs to push it back at the top. The return was already available in the function.

We might recognise a breath or a bounce in this movement. That resemblance arrives before we inspect what made it. There is no lung here, and no floor collision has reversed its direction. A familiar rhythm can lend a body an explanation the program does not contain.

Watch a few more cycles. How soon does the next one begin to feel inevitable? Repetition gives us a foothold: enough regularity to notice a different height, a faster return, a body that starts just out of step. A small change becomes legible because something else has been kept.

The nearby cubes turn or combine several changes. From the doorway their movements may seem to belong together. Standing beside one, we can separate a change of place from a change of orientation, and the size of a movement from how often it returns. The same cube allows these differences to remain the subject.

Further in, take the pendulum bob aside and release it. Your hand enters this movement. Its angle and angular velocity help determine what happens next. The cube keeps reading its clock.

Both bodies return. One repeats a prescribed relation; the other responds to the state your gesture leaves behind. We can watch them together without asking them to be the same kind of thing.

For now, the return disappears as it happens. We have to wait again to compare it. In the next hall, a movement will leave a record we can walk beside.

02. Pendulum

Read both texts together

Where a swing leaves its past

Behind the bob, something is leaving the room very slowly.

Stand beside the frame and follow the swing. Then choose one teal mark just behind it. The bob turns. Keep looking at your mark: does it turn too?

It continues away along the hall. Walk beside it. From here the whole record resembles a wave, although the bob has only swung in one plane. The long direction belongs to something the bob did not do.

Each mark keeps a position and a time. The drawing subtracts that time from the present and gives the resulting age a distance: six-tenths of a metre for every second. An old moment is further away. We can walk beside a duration because a direction has been given another job.

Two moments one swing apart leave a repeated shape along that direction. Its spacing depends on how quickly we send the record into depth. We could double the spacing without changing the swing. This length belongs to our way of showing the return; it is not evidence that a disturbance travelled down the hall.

Look down. The same record lies across a dark surface beneath the swing. Its horizontal position remains, and so does its age. Height has been left out. You can move between the hanging account and the flattened one, checking what each makes easier to see.

Look at the requested interval and the actual gaps on the plate. FINE asks for a mark every twenty-five milliseconds. With a sixty-tick physics clock, some gaps are shorter and some longer. The request falls between the available observations. A smooth join can make that mismatch difficult to see.

Press COARSE and let a few swings leave their marks. There are fewer positions to count. Follow the oldest one: it can reach further into the past.

The list has room for three hundred entries and allows none older than ten seconds. Fine sampling fills its places sooner. Coarse sampling keeps less of each swing and more swings within reach. A person looking for a small tremor and a person looking for a long return would have different reasons to choose.

Wait with one mark. It is there, then absent. No fading gives it a farewell. The pendulum carries on from its current angle and speed; this little piece of its accessible past has gone.

Now press STROBE. The sampler uses the period measured from the pendulum's own crossings. After a few swings, compare the moving bob with the sparse record behind it. The marks nearly line up. Tick-sized differences remain, but much of the movement has vanished from what was kept.

This can become an instrument for finding repetition. It can also make repetition look like stillness. Try reading the marks before glancing back at the bob. The same setting helps with one question and makes another difficult to answer.

RESET begins this experiment again: starting angle, local time, empty list. The museum continues. We have already seen how useful it is to make a beginning without beginning everything.

In the point rooms, the drawing dot asked whether a hand had moved far enough to keep another position. Here the recorder waits for time to pass. Both leave things out. The choice becomes easier to notice when a moving body and its account remain beside one another.

At the plinth, take the point and move your hand. Your own record extends along the open side of the room. Further on, three double pendulums paint on separate canvases. The marks stay on those surfaces while the bodies continue. There is space to approach each account and return to what is making it.

We could stay and change what a record values. Ahead, rising and falling are being given yet another direction. A repeating rule will take position in space as its input. We leave this hall knowing that a wave-shaped drawing can tell us about a movement, a clock, and the decisions that let one stand for the other.

03. Sine Space

Read both texts together

What a wave can hold

The blue tray tilts away from you. Choose a hollow before pressing RELEASE. Imagine where the first sphere will go.

It touches the surface, finds a slope, meets another sphere. The landscape has become part of the movement.

After twelve seconds the bodies stop. The display separates those that crossed the outlet from those still inside and those that left elsewhere. The spheres in the neat rows below are a record: the program has moved each departed body there and removed it from further collisions.

Leave RELIEF alone and press SIZE. The surface waits for a larger body. Release again. Which hollow from your first prediction still matters? Cycle SIZE once more to try the smallest spheres. A place in the landscape has dimensions; so does what arrives there. The name of the wave has not told us their relationship.

RESET returns the middle size and the original relief. Now change RELIEF and repeat. One press makes the folds steeper; the next makes them shallower than when you arrived. Their positions across the tray stay the same. You have changed a surface, then watched what that means to a body with its own size, mass and contacts.

Open RULE. A sine repeats across the tray and another along it. Their product supplies a height at each sampled address. Triangles join the addresses; a collision shape gives those triangles the power to support the spheres. The terrain stays still throughout the trial. Time belongs to the moving bodies and to our observation window.

Look again at STILL IN. Twelve seconds gave us time to compare. It did not give us forever. A body present at this deadline has not been proven permanently trapped. And if the purpose were to hold something here, our judgement of the same count might change. What else would we need to learn before calling this hollow a shelter?

Beyond the tray, a fold comes towards you.

There is a corresponding fold on the other wall, a little out of step. Stop at the mouth of the passage. Choose a narrow place ahead. Watch what becomes of it while you stay where you are.

The walls keep changing the invitation. Press FREEZE. Now the folds wait for you. Pass one, turn, and approach it from the other direction. A shape can come to meet a body through its own motion or through the body's movement. For a moment these felt alike.

Run the walls again and follow a small ripple across a broader fold. The layers slip past each other. Walking the frozen surface carries one arrangement past the eye; advancing the phase changes how its layers agree. We have found a difference inside the resemblance.

Leave AMP where it is and slide PHASE through half a turn. Look where the left wall approaches the middle. What is the right doing now?

Both sides bend the same way. The passage swerves while the readout settles at 2.00 to 2.00 metres. Slide back towards zero and openings and pinches return. One relative setting has changed the kind of passage the pair makes.

Inspect a bend at half a turn. The number still says two metres. Notice its name: x-gap. It compares the walls at matching positions along the passage, across its local X direction. The shortest way between curved surfaces need not follow that direction. We have made a constant width by deciding how to measure it. Which width would matter to a body trying to get through?

Bring PHASE back towards zero, then raise AMP. Approach a pinch slowly. Try crossing a visible crest instead of following the opening.

You can pass through. The walls have no collision shapes; a thin slab supplies the floor. Yet the surfaces have already offered a direction, a narrowing, a reason to hesitate. Their influence did not wait for collision to be enabled.

Try following the apparent passage for a few steps, then cutting across a fold. Giving these surfaces the power to stop you would change what can happen here. It would require another part of the program.

RESET restores the declared settings. You can recognise the passage and still choose a different route through it.

Beyond the corridor, a broad ramp descends into the basin. The silver floor waits a metre below the museum floor. This surface has collision. Walk down onto it: a fold that the eye follows now changes where the foot can stand. The room opens wider. Coloured balls fall from above, bounce against a crest and gather in the hollows. Walk among them: the same relief redirects their movement and changes your footing. The terrain stays still while your height changes. Solid walls close both sides. To reach the next room you cross these folds; a second ramp returns you to level ground at the far end. The wave has become a condition of continuing.

A repeating value has acquired several bodies already. Ahead, a point travels around a circle. A graph grows beside it, then stays while the point keeps turning. Follow the point and try to predict the graph. After walking among these folds, we can look at one way their returning values are made.

04. Unit Circle

Read both texts together

Unit Circle — a circle becoming a graph

The point reaches the top and keeps going. Its height begins to fall. Beside it, a path grows across the room: the same rise, the same fall, laid out where you can follow it with your eyes.

After two turns the path stops growing. The point continues. Something has finished being drawn while the movement it describes goes on.

Take the angle control. At the rightmost point, the horizontal offset is one and the vertical offset is zero. At the top they exchange their roles. Another quarter turn makes the horizontal offset negative. There is no privileged beginning on the circle; the rightmost point becomes a beginning because we agree to measure from it.

Two lengths change together. At radius one we call them cosine and sine. Increase the radius and both offsets grow; their ratios to the radius remain the same. The construction is exact even though its visible outline is made from finite pieces.

An angle can be measured by the arc it cuts off divided by the radius. That ratio gives radians. A larger circle has a longer circumference, but one complete turn still has the same angular measure.

The graph makes another agreement. Here one turn occupies six metres along the floor. That distance belongs to this display. It is neither the circle's circumference nor the distance the point has travelled. We have given angle a horizontal address so that height can be read beside it.

Watch the point cross the same height twice: once rising, once falling. A height alone cannot tell you which movement is happening. The other coordinate restores a distinction the single reading lost. What seemed like a complete description was sufficient only for a particular question.

Farther into the hall, separate steps oscillate and another curve keeps a short trail. A drawing can suggest a route. Whether it supports a body is a further decision: these coloured graph pieces are a display, while the floor carries your weight.

The circle gives us a way to calculate a return. The next room asks what happens when a returning body is attached to others. Then its movement must answer to more than its own angle.

05. Propagation

Read both texts together

The motion that travels

Choose one sphere near the centre of the lattice. Follow that sphere alone. It rises and returns, keeping its place in the grid. Now widen your attention. Something has travelled beyond the place you were watching.

Press SINE; the button now reads PULSE. After half a second, the centre receives one push. Look for the first neighbour to respond. Its movement may be small. The readout keeps the largest displacement reached by one immediate neighbour since the reset, in millimetres. It can help you notice a response without deciding what that response means.

Press COUPLED. It becomes INDEPENDENT, the drawn links disappear, and the same push begins from the same still lattice. The centre still moves. The places around it no longer receive the disturbance.

Restore COUPLED and repeat. Each button restarts the comparison. You are changing the conditions of a beginning; you have not severed a moving wave halfway through its journey.

The sphere you chose has not visited its neighbours. Its changing relation to them has become consequential. Follow the pattern for a moment, then return to that one body. How much of what you called its movement belonged to what surrounded it?

Look at the boundary. An interior point has eight neighbours here, including the diagonals. A corner has three. Each point has the same kind of rule, yet its position in the arrangement gives that rule different relationships to work with. The edge participates in the movement. It is not simply where we stopped drawing.

The rods make those relationships visible. They have no collision and are not physical springs. A body can pass through the drawing; the stored relation still acts. Conversely, two spheres passing through similar heights would not by itself prove that one had affected the other. The switch gives us a comparison that resemblance alone cannot supply.

The floor opens into a basin. Walk down the broad ramp onto the tiles. The rim stays level with your arrival; farther in, rings spread beneath your feet. The far ramp returns you to the museum floor. Here each tile receives its height from a rule using distance and time. It does not ask a neighbouring tile what happened there. The visual resemblance survives this difference in construction.

We might want the image of a wave, or we might want a world in which removing a connection changes what follows. Those desires can produce similar surfaces and different possibilities. Which one did you assume when the first crest arrived?

Return to one sphere. The centre was chosen as the source before you entered; the switch can disconnect every link at once. Choosing a different source, or removing just one relation, would make other experiments possible. The present controls give us a particular way to ask what travels.

The next room turns to sound. One oscillation will enter another's phase. Keep the distinction we found here: looking or sounding alike is a beginning for comparison, not yet an account of how something came to happen.

06. Effect Sound

Read both texts together

One wave inside another

The floor's wave has passed under your feet. In the last hall, a disturbance travelled between points that kept their places. Here a wave enters another rule. Two balls sit in separate cages above desks. Begin with one note.

The blue ball belongs to the carrier, the orange to the modulator. Press BASELINE, let the note end, then HOLD. The instrument keeps this setting and its sound as A.

Leave the blue ball where it is. Lift the orange one a little. A sound may become brassy, brittle, bell-like, or acquire a quality for which you have no ready name. Keep listening before choosing a familiar object to stand in for it.

While you drag, the instrument can start a fresh note every three tenths of a second. A movement becomes a run of beginnings. Let the ball rest and press PLAY. Give the note time to reach its ending. Was the difference at the beginning, through the middle, or in the tail?

COMPARE returns A, leaves a quarter-second gap, then plays the current B. You do not have to preserve the first sound intact in memory. The differs: line lists the parameters your hand changed. If the lift changed only I, the modulation index, we have one change to listen through. A hand can alter several coordinates while intending only one.

At index zero, the orange oscillation contributes nothing. Raising it advances and delays the carrier's phase. The second wave changes how the first proceeds. The sound's rise and fading are controlled elsewhere. What reaches the ear as one event has several rules that can be separated.

Keep the orange ball's height steady and change its depth along the desk. That direction changes how long the modulation persists. Listen through the tail. The altered character can remain while the note grows quiet. Something we first heard as one fading has separated into two processes.

Try the ball's left-to-right direction last. It changes the ratio between the oscillators' frequencies. Keep a reference and compare. Listen for a relation you want to return to, even if it refuses the name of an instrument you know. A setting that would spoil one imitation may be the beginning of another sound.

BASELINE is a chosen reference too: a 440-hertz carrier, an attack and decay, index zero. Four hundred and forty returns a second. The slow motion we could follow with our eyes has become a pitch. The generator still shapes and scales its output. Calling this the beginning makes comparison possible; it does not uncover a sound before all decisions.

HOLD can keep a note without knowing what you liked about it. COMPARE gives that decision back to your ear. The rig reports changed parameters, but there is no field in its list for the difference that made you want to listen again.

Look back at the scope. Raising the index need not make the orange trace taller: the display rescales that lane to fit it. The output changes while the apparent height of its cause can stay much the same. Even this small window has an arrangement to uncover. It shows two carrier periods near the attack, less than five milliseconds, rather than the whole fading note. The coloured cube gives some settings another appearance. Neither can take over the listening.

Next, Bernini gives a periodic relation height and surface. We can stop the movement and still find a turn inside the form.

07. Bernini

Read both texts together

A movement kept in a column

In the last room, a periodic relation became a sound. Here it rises above you. Can a shape keep the rhythm of a movement without travelling along it?

Choose one ridge on a twisting shaft and follow it upward. Notice how it moves around the vertical axis as height increases. Change your viewpoint and find the ridge again. Then compare it with a neighbouring column.

There are two different changes to disentangle. The shaft is already twisted as a shape: its profile varies from one height to another. It also rotates slowly as a whole. Watching that rotation does not explain how the twist was made, and following the twist does not require the column to rotate.

At a low level, choose a ridge. Find it again higher up, then higher still. The turn you follow belongs to positions already stored in the form. Now keep your attention at one level and let the slow rotation bring the surface past you. Your eye can read either as movement. The first asks you to travel; the second asks you to wait.

Circular coordinates provide sine and cosine values around an axis. Combining those changing coordinates with height produces a helix. Further variations in the radius and surface give the shaft its richer profile. A periodic function that previously described movement through time now helps specify where parts of a solid belong.

Look at the columns together. Their repetition creates intervals and sight lines that a single shaft cannot. The form and its arrangement contribute different things: one supplies the twist, the other decides whether you meet a field of objects, a threshold or an aisle. The spacing gives you room to pass between them. The repetition begins to arrange your movement too.

The scene borrows the spiral architectural column. Its height, repeated bases and lit surfaces give the generated form a ceremony. Its mathematical construction does not account for the whole history of a building, the labour of making it, or what it means to those who use it. A low generated hum accompanies the columns; its level is tied to their rotation speed. Touching a shaft does not play it.

From the wall, a synthetic voice attempts “right here, right now”, pauses, then begins again. This is a separate instrument. The words return while the columns keep turning. Which “now” has the repeated phrase brought back?

Try reading the shaft once as a solid and once as a sequence of changing cross-sections. The next room leaves more of its temporal composition to chance. A rule can preserve a recognisable form while allowing individual events to arrive differently.

08. Cage

Read both texts together

The limits of the unexpected

What remains composed when the next event is left to chance?

After the turning columns, an entrance arrives without their promise of a regular return. The clock still matters. What it measures no longer has to repeat.

Let the soundscape load. A continuous layer stays with you; other sounds enter and leave. Count a few entrances without trying to force them into a regular beat. Then listen to the intervals instead. An interval can become the part you are waiting for.

The background remains recognisable while particular sounds arrive differently. There is a difference between not knowing which event comes next and hearing something the system has no means to produce. Listen for the vocabulary that persists: a texture, an attack, a relation to the drone. An unexpected entrance can bring a familiar sound.

The composition prepares a collection of generated sounds, including several variations of each effect type. It then chooses among them. Before chance makes its first selection, someone has decided what belongs in the collection. The program can surprise that person without acquiring a sound they never gave it the means to make.

Listen once for the background beneath an event, then for the event's beginning and decay. Leave the next entrance uncounted. Your attention supplies a repeatable task, although the live generator does not promise to repeat the sequence for you.

The luminous forms respond to sound activity. They do not reveal every scheduling choice, nor are they separate sources whose balance you can mix by walking between them. The room gives sound a visible company. The relation still needs listening.

Beside this live composition, the tutorial keeps six choices and their times. You can see what will come, then hear it again. The second performance may cease to surprise you. The record has not changed; your relation to it has. Bernini left a turn in a column when its movement stopped. Here the event list remains when its sounds have ended.

Chance alone does not open the collection. Admitting a visitor's recording would alter what could enter, as well as raising another question: whose sounds would we keep? That is a possible extension, not something this installation already does.

The room's title offers a reference, not a claim to perform a particular John Cage score. Its question is about authorship inside a chance procedure. In Air Music, a strike will supply a more direct beginning. You will choose when an oscillation starts and hear what continues after your contact ends.

09. Air Music

Read both texts together

After the strike

In the previous hall, a score could keep an event's place before it sounded. Here you give the note its beginning. Your hand can leave while its consequence continues.

A stick lies in a cradle at the left end of a table. Eight metal bars lie beside it, the longest nearest the stick. Take it and bring the tip down on that bar. Lift it away at once and keep listening.

The contact is over. The bar glows and rings; the plate above the row names a voice and counts its age. As the glow fades, decide whether what you hear is the strike or what the strike began.

Strike the same bar again before it finishes. There is another attack, but no additional voice. Its age returns towards zero. Now strike a shorter bar while the first is still fading. The plate names two voices, each with its own age. The first was allowed to continue.

You can withdraw from the encounter without ending it. A second action arrives in a room still occupied by the first. Try a long gap, then a short one. Reverse the order. Listen for the moment when one note makes the other difficult to hear, although both remain present in the readout.

These bars have chosen notes. Their lengths were arranged to follow those choices; the program does not discover a pitch by calculating the vibration of the metal. There is a resemblance to an instrument, and a working instrument within that resemblance. The seam is available to us: we can change the body without changing its stored note.

The row offers C, D, E, G and A, continuing into another octave. That is a decision about which pairs your hand can reach. It does not settle which pairs are good. What sounds at ease here has already been given a particular company to keep.

Try one bar slowly and then quickly. The stick's speed reaches both the sound and the glow through different mappings. Brightness is not a direct measure of loudness. The room makes a gesture legible in more than one way, and those accounts do not quite coincide.

The ribbon adds a short piece of the sounding voices. Its sum is not a third bar. Nor is it everything that reaches your ears: distance changes the encounter, while these stored samples stay the same. A representation can tell us that two voices continue even when listening makes one seem to disappear.

Set the stick down away from its cradle. Left alone, it returns. The notes have their own endings. Their mathematical decay would continue indefinitely; the stored sounds finish after seven and a half seconds. Someone has decided how long the consequence may last.

For a moment, do nothing more. Let the room finish answering. An action has ended, and something it began is still happening.

The next hall returns to solid parts and the places they offer a foot. Keep these overlapping voices in mind. Beyond the stair, their relation will become something we can watch.

10. Sky Stairs

Read both texts together

What a curve needs from a stair

The first tread meets the floor. From here the spiral seems to offer a way up.

Follow it with your eyes. The steps turn as they rise; the outline widens, then draws inward. The eye reaches places the foot has not yet tried.

The cube has returned as a tread. It has width, depth and a neighbour. A circular progression places it around the centre, a steady increment lifts it, and sine changes its distance from the axis. Three simple decisions have begun to look like an invitation.

Stand near the lower turn. The inner edges crowd together while the outer edges spread apart. The centres follow their rule faithfully. At the edges, where a foot would land, another account becomes necessary.

Surfaces connect neighbouring treads. They sit above the tread tops, so finding a connection does not settle how the crossing will feel. A line through the centres would be easier to draw. It would leave the body with the same question.

In Bernini we could follow a ridge upward without asking it to carry us. Here the familiar shape of a stair makes a stronger promise. We begin to read the next surface as somewhere we might stand. That expectation arrives before we have checked the rise, the clearance, or the support between steps.

The same interval can be a small step for one visitor and an interruption for another. Adding more samples may refine the outline while leaving that difference untouched. A handrail or a wider tread would change what this form offers. Neither is contained in the sine function.

Stay at the lower end a moment longer. The whole climb is visible; completing it remains a separate question. The room has made it possible to notice the distance between those two kinds of arrival.

Ahead, two waves meet on a display. Their parts will also obey simple rules. We will watch what becomes available between them.

11. Beats

Read both texts together

A slow pulse from fast motions

The lower row grows quiet. Above it, both waves are still moving.

Bring the frequencies of A and B close together. Follow one place on the row beneath them. Its quick movement widens and narrows slowly, as though another rhythm had entered the room.

Nothing else is driving it. The row adds the two values at each place and moment. Their contributions come into agreement, drift into opposition, then approach agreement again. The slow pulse belongs to that changing relation.

At its narrowest, the sum can conceal substantial movement in both sources. We have met a quietness that does not require either part to rest. Looking only at the result, what might we conclude about what produced it?

Move the frequencies closer. The next broadening takes longer to arrive. The readout gives their absolute difference as the beat rate. Two quick returns have made a small difference slow enough to follow. At equal frequencies, this display's initially aligned waves reinforce each other without the same slow beating.

The lower row is labelled (A+B)/2. Half the calculated height has been allowed into the drawing so it fits. A row that appears no larger than either source can represent their doubled value. The label lets us recover a distinction the arrangement might otherwise hide.

Air Music let two voices begin at different moments. Here the relation is visible and silent. We can stay with the place where the sum almost disappears, then watch it grow again. Neither source needed to stop and start again for that return to happen.

Elsewhere, red and blue steps give sine and cosine places a body might stand. The glowing figure gives oscillations separate spatial coordinates. These parts have been assembled differently: added on one axis, offered as support, or distributed across three axes.

For fixed phases, a rational relation between frequencies can close the figure. An ideal irrational relation does not exactly repeat. Our finite drawing cannot demonstrate “never”, however long we wait beside it.

At the next bench, five smooth returns make a shape that none has alone. We will be able to change how much each contributes, and keep a combination we want to find again.

12. Synthesis

Read both texts together

The sum on the bench

In Beats, two quick motions made a slow pulse. Here a wave is waiting to be taken apart.

Find the bench with the dark board above it. The board carries a pale wave with five coloured rows beneath it, and the wave already has a shape when you arrive: a long slope down and a steep rise, four times across the board, drifting to the left. On the bench, at its right end, five sliders stand in a column, one for each row.

Five amounts

Read the rows before you touch anything. The top row is one smooth rise and fall per cycle; each row below has more, two, three, four, five, and each is drawn smaller than the one above. None of them looks like the pale wave. Press HOLD on the panel at the left of the bench and the drift stops, so a place on the board stays where it is.

Now take the four lower sliders down, one at a time, and keep your eye on the pale wave while each row leaves the ladder. What remains is the top row, drawn larger: a sine. Press BASELINE and the four return together; press H1 ALONE and they leave together. The shape you arrived at is not one thing with a slope in it. It is five smooth returns added, and the slope belongs to none of them.

Bring the second row back on its own slider and choose a place on the board. The amber line marked mark is one such place. Before you raise the third slider, decide what the sum will do there: the third row is at a crest, or crossing its middle, or in a trough, and its slider decides how much of that is admitted. Raise it and read the plate on the bench. Its third line lists each row's value at the mark; the fourth adds them and prints the total drawn beside them. With these five visible rows, their sum agrees with the total. Each row makes an integer number of returns in the same span. Its slider decides how much it contributes. The pale line gathers these signed contributions at each place on the board. A crest adds; a trough subtracts. What looks like one slope is a relation maintained across many positions.

This is a visual instrument. It does not play the curve as sound. No slider changes the fundamental's frequency. Unlike the separately struck bars in Air Music, these rows cannot choose separate beginnings. A shifted row or a negative amount would need a control the bench does not offer. BASELINE restores the five amounts the room was placed with: one, a half, approximately a third, a quarter and a fifth. The plate names which coefficients differ, so you can wander and still know what has changed.

With the baseline held, look at the rise. An ideal sawtooth jumps; this one climbs through a visible distance. Its descent ripples where the ideal would be straight. Raise the fifth slider and those ripples change. Other amounts can make the sum rise further. Five smooth returns can suggest a corner and cannot make an exact one. From across the room the pale line suggests a sawtooth. At the bench its rounded rise remains available to inspect.

Take the second and fourth rows out and leave the odd three; the label offers a name. Move the fifth a little. At first the name stays. Move further and it gives way to Custom Waveform. The curve has changed continuously; the naming rule crossed a threshold. It checks all five amounts relative to the first, allowing a small difference from a recipe. Custom is a place to continue from.

Then remove the row you think matters least and find where its absence shows. It is not in one place, because each row runs the whole cycle. Near zero, a row disappears before its coefficient reaches zero: the display hides values below 0.01 while the sum still includes them. At some marks the plate can report a difference between the visible rows and the total. Something has left the picture without leaving the calculation.

Interlude — what the room keeps

An optional listening encounter. The five-harmonic tutorial is complete; this visit extends its questions. You can also continue at “Return — the recipe and the instant” below.

Leave the five amounts held for a moment. We have been changing a recipe and calculating its result again. Across the laboratory, a sound will carry the result of one change into the next. Give this encounter its own time; we will return to the bench afterward.

Press NEXT PASS. Something has acted on the phrase. Listen for a vowel, a consonant, a pause. Press again. Which is easiest to follow now? AUTO continues the returns; HEAR ORIGINAL interrupts them with the recording you began from. The comparison remains available even when the words become difficult to recognize.

The room keeps the result of each pass and makes that result the next input. The next pass begins with a sound that has already been changed. RESET returns to a copy of the original instead.

A copy can keep the values. A filter acts on them. Here, delayed sound returns into the calculation, reinforcing some frequencies more than others. Repeating the operation makes that unevenness increasingly audible. The voice and the rule participate in what remains.

Alvin Lucier's I Am Sitting in a Room begins with a voice recorded and played back into a physical room, then recorded again. The room's resonances become prominent through successive returns. A digital recorder could take part in that physical process too; tape damage is not required for its central effect.1

Calling everything we hear here analogue distortion would hide a distinction we can investigate. In Lucier's physical setup, sound passes through air between a loudspeaker and a microphone. In this virtual room, the first recording enters a numerical model. Three delayed paths use the room's dimensions; damping changes what returns. The visible walls do not, by themselves, act on the sound.2

Go to SURFACE and move ABSORB. The panels change colour and the experiment returns to the stored voice. Begin the passes again. Keep the same phrase and compare what happens under the changed conditions. Colour and acoustic response have been connected by code. One has not caused the other.

A wall can look like concrete before we have given it any of concrete's acoustic behaviour. What would it mean to give that wall the response of cloth? Or to let a curtain ring? Those rooms are possibilities for another iteration. The current control changes absorption; it does not yet let us exchange a whole material's behaviour. Even this smaller choice makes appearance and action available to question separately.

There are other choices inside the return. Each pass is adjusted to the same peak amplitude. The recording has a fixed length, with a short allowance for its tail. Sound that continues beyond that window is cut away. HEAR ORIGINAL means the first recording this instrument stored; it cannot give us the voice before recording, sampling and preparation. Walking changes where we hear the virtual loudspeaker, while the simplified resonance model stays fixed.

Listen once more to the original. What became difficult to hear has not been deleted from every place. It remains in a buffer we chose to keep. The room's rules shaped one version of the voice; comparison lets us notice their work. Which properties would we carry into another room, and which might we let it change?

Return — the recipe and the instant

Choose BACK TO HALL and find the five rows again. Press HOLD, then BASELINE. Pick a place on the pale curve and move the fifth slider. Watch that place change. Press BASELINE once more: with the phase held, restoring the five amounts brings the curve back to where you just saw it.

In the listening room, each pass began with the altered result of the last. Here the slider changes an amount used to calculate the sum afresh. The previous curve does not become an ingredient in the next one. Both instruments let us return, but they keep different things available.

Release HOLD. The five amounts stay while the shape moves across the board. BASELINE can restore this recipe without recovering the moment before we left the hall. Repeating a recipe and repeating an exact instant ask for different things to be kept. In Random Definition, that question will meet an unfamiliar result: what would we need to retain to bring it back?
