Yes. And I think Donna Haraway, Sara Ahmed, Félix Guattari and Saidiya Hartman could be particularly productive for Ada Research.
But I would add Vilém Flusser, Karen Barad, Georges Perec, Ursula K. Le Guin, Italo Calvino, Samuel Beckett and Jorge Luis Borges.
The distinction I would make is between authors who help you think about the apparatus and authors who help you invent a literary voice for encountering it. Some do both.
Your randomness chapter already contains questions about bodily orientation, deterministic systems, incomplete records, technical mediation, repetition and constrained agency. These are not merely subjects to which theory can be attached. They are things the visitor actually does.
1. The thinkers: Changing what the narrator notices
Sara Ahmed — Orientation, lines and what is within reach
Read: Queer Phenomenology
Ahmed seems extraordinarily relevant to the three opening spheres.
In Queer Phenomenology, orientation concerns how bodies inhabit space, follow lines, approach objects and find certain things within reach. Directions are not simply neutral geometric possibilities; orientations shape what becomes available to a body. 


Duke University Press



Your opening already has an Ahmed-like proposition:
The line has not grown, but there is less time inside the same distance.

The first sphere moves horizontally. The third acquires another dimension. The visitor's hand is asked to follow a prescribed course.
Ahmed would encourage the narrator to attend not only to where the sphere moves, but to how the body becomes oriented around it.
A possible literary operation:
The line is straight. Nothing requires the hand to be.
That is not an explanation of Ahmed. It is a way to allow orientation to become active in the prose.
Vilém Flusser — Playing against the apparatus
Read: Towards a Philosophy of Photography
Flusser may be the single most directly relevant thinker for What chance is allowed to do.
His account of the camera treats it as a programmed apparatus: the operator can choose among possibilities already structured by the machine. The artistic question becomes how to play with, understand or challenge that program. 


Google Books
+1



Your return sphere is almost a physical demonstration of this problem.
The participant can influence the excursion, but the apparatus has already determined that the sphere will return.
A Flusser-inspired narrator might ask a more troublesome question than whether the movement is random:
The sphere offered a thousand departures. Nobody had asked it for a thousand destinations.
This shifts attention from the random values to the program that defines their possible consequences.
Flusser is especially useful because this can become an artistic question, not simply a critique. What would it mean to play against the apparatus rather than only explore its permitted variations?
Donna Haraway — Situated knowledge and partial accounts
Read: Situated Knowledges and Staying with the Trouble
Haraway offers two complementary methods.
First, knowledge is situated. Every observation is made from somewhere, through particular bodies, histories and instruments. This is not a rejection of scientific objectivity but an argument for accountable, partial perspectives. 


Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy



That is immediately useful for the virtual lava lamps and Geiger instrument.
The narrator should not always speak as though it sees the entire world. It might speak from the sensor's position, from the recorded data, or from the participant who encounters the discrepancy.
Second, Staying with the Trouble develops speculative fabulation, string figures and making-with as practices for following connections rather than isolating self-contained entities. 


Duke University Press



This suggests a narrative structure in which objects pass something between them. The seed travels. The gesture becomes a number. The number becomes movement. Nothing is completely understood from one position.
Karen Barad — The apparatus participates in what is observed
Read: Meeting the Universe Halfway
Barad's agential realism is especially interesting for the Geiger study and sensor wall. Her work investigates how measuring apparatuses participate in producing determinate phenomena and boundaries, rather than merely observing an independently fixed set of properties. 


Duke University Press



Your coarser Geiger clock creates an exceptionally concrete situation:
Two different intervals are recorded. Change the measurement resolution and those differences no longer yield distinguishable measurements.
The literary opportunity is to describe the making of a distinction, rather than simply the disappearance of information.
But there is an important qualification. Barad's theory draws on quantum physics; a simulated Geiger counter does not demonstrate quantum indeterminacy or establish her ontology. It can, however, create a useful conceptual encounter with the role of measurement.
Félix Guattari — Machinic assemblages and the production of subjectivity
Read: Chaosmosis and The Three Ecologies
Guattari may help you escape the idea that each artifact is an isolated scientific demonstration.
The sphere is not only an algorithm. The actual encounter involves a generator, code, a VR controller, a tracked hand, a body, anticipation, movement constraints and the visitor's experience.
The assemblage produces a particular possibility for acting and feeling.
Chaosmosis is centrally concerned with machinic heterogenesis, the production and reinvention of subjectivity, and the role of artistic and non-discursive intensities. 


University of Washington Press



For the book, Guattari suggests that you need not always translate the physical experience into a proposition. Subjectivity is also being produced through movement, sound, timing and participation.
The visitor does not merely learn about the machine. The visitor becomes a temporary component of its operation.
Saidiya Hartman — The record and what cannot be recovered
Read: Venus in Two Acts and Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments
If you mean Saidiya Hartman, yes—but I would approach her differently from the others.
Hartman's critical fabulation confronts historical archives that are incomplete and structured by the violence and domination that produced them. In Venus in Two Acts, she reflects on the impossibility of recovering an enslaved person's life transparently from records that themselves participated in her silencing. 


Duke University Press



Your RAND table and movement sampler also raise questions about what a record can preserve and what remains inaccessible. But the disappearance of sensor information must not be equated with the historical erasure of enslaved lives.
What I would take from Hartman is a discipline of writing at the limits of evidence.
The narrator could acknowledge that the record does not authorize a complete story. It can offer possibilities without pretending that the missing information has been recovered.
This could become important for the wider book, especially when the narrator begins to speculate about events or intentions the simulation cannot establish.

I would pay particular attention to three of these.
Perec: The possibility that nothing extraordinary needs to happen
Your latest version is already approaching Perec.
He has a capacity to turn an inventory of ordinary occurrences into literature, not by adding metaphors but through duration, repetition, variation and the impossibility of exhausting a place.
Imagine applying that patience to RANDOM TOUCH. The spheres do their small journeys. The visitor may follow or not. The text records precisely what persists and what changes.
There is no obligation to arrive at a revelation.
This may be the best foundation for the narrator's everyday voice.
Calvino: The comedy of scientific observation
Calvino's Mr. Palomar is especially interesting because attempts at precise observation continually reveal complications in the observer's own method.
And Cosmicomics goes in another direction, transforming scientific propositions into fantastic narrative situations.
For Ada Research, I would borrow the relationship between precision and imagination, not the fantastical inventions themselves.
Consider the Geiger counter: the visitor is trying to discover the difference between two intervals. They change the clock resolution. Now the instrument reports them as equal. The effort to measure more decisively has instead eliminated the distinction.
There is something gently comic about that result.
The narrator might acknowledge the predicament without turning it into a philosophical lesson.
Beckett: A rule becomes a lifetime
Beckett's Quad may be one of the strongest references for a project built around procedural 3D movement. Performers traverse prescribed geometric paths; the arrangement of bodies, repetition and avoidance produces the work.
This is almost the inverse of conventional storytelling.
We do not start with characters who have motives and then decide what they do. We start with a rule and encounter the particular existence that the rule makes possible.
Your sphere that must return is a good example.
A Beckett-like structural principle would be to let the return occur repeatedly until its inevitability becomes the experience, rather than explaining that inevitability.
3. How I would combine these influences
I would avoid choosing a single author as the model for the entire book. Instead, I would give each influence a distinct job.
A POSSIBLE LITERARY METHOD FOR ADA RESEARCH
Perec — The observing voice
The room, objects, positions, times, differences and repetitions. The patience to attend to something insignificant.


Calvino and Borges — The narrative intelligence
Follow the logic of a system to its surprising consequences. Permit irony and absurdity without falsifying the underlying mechanisms.


Ahmed and Haraway — The situated body
What can be reached, followed, seen or known? Who is doing the observing, and what are the limits of that position?


Flusser and Guattari — The critical pressure
How does the apparatus distribute possibilities? How do the participant, technical systems and institutional rules make an experience together?


Beckett and Le Guin — The narrative structure
Repetition, carrying, retaining, changing and returning can organize a story without relying on a conventional heroic journey.


Hartman and Barad — The limits of the account
In their distinct historical and theoretical contexts, both demand care about what an observation or record authorizes us to say.


4. What would this actually do to your prose?
Consider the opening sphere again, in an original register informed by Perec's patient observation, Ahmed's orientation and Flusser's programmed possibilities.
Three lines hang in the room, each two metres long.
The spheres begin at their centres. There is no evident reason to prefer one over another.
A hand approaches the first. The sphere begins to move. The hand follows, sometimes getting ahead, sometimes falling behind. The line contains the journey from beginning to end, although the hand has not yet learned its turns.
The next sphere has the same journey and half the time in which to make it.
The third has acquired another direction. Nothing in the first two lines was missing. They simply had no use for height.
We withdraw the hand. The sphere returns to its centre.
The path is still available in the program.
On replay, the same turns occur. The visitor need not follow them as before.
Something has been repeated exactly. Something else has not been asked to repeat.

I find this more interesting than a text that finishes by announcing the difference between determinism and subjective unpredictability.
The distinction is already present.
5. One more writer I would consider essential: Ursula K. Le Guin
There is a deeper reason for recommending Le Guin beyond her prose style.
In The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction, she questions the dominance of stories organized around heroic confrontation and decisive achievement. Instead she considers the narrative possibilities of gathering and holding.
This connects directly to your recurring gesture of KEEP.
The falling digit is retained. A gesture becomes a record. A seed is saved. A journey is replayed. The book itself gathers all these little consequences.
The research is not necessarily moving toward a final answer. It is accumulating encounters that can be carried into new situations.
That could be a form for the entire book: not a march from elementary geometry toward increasingly complex algorithms, but an accumulating collection of things, relations and experiments that acquire different meanings when brought together.
It also resonates with Haraway's practices of making-with and passing patterns between participants.

My recommendation
For the literary voice, I would start by studying Perec's An Attempt at Exhausting a Place in Paris, Calvino's Mr. Palomar, and Le Guin's The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction.
For the conceptual framework, I would work with Ahmed, Haraway, Guattari and Flusser, bringing in Barad and Hartman when the particular encounter genuinely calls for them.
But I think there is one more question behind your search for a voice.
Your current narrator is primarily an observer. These authors suggest several alternatives: the narrator could be a participant, an unreliable instrument, a collector, a witness, an archivist, or even a system trying to describe the limits of its own operations.
What if the book did not have one stable narrator? What if the narrator's capacity to perceive changed as it passed through the algorithms?
In the coordinate-system hall, it might have almost nothing but position. Later, it acquires duration, memory, uncertainty, relations, forces and the ability to distinguish its own operations from their consequences.
Its language could develop along with those capacities.
That possibility interests me more than imitating any single literary author: the book's voice itself could be something generated and transformed by the research.
