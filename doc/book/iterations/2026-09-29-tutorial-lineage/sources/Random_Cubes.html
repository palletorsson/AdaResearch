# One throw and many throws

How much can one throw tell you about a die?

<!-- @dice_throw -->

A white die lies on green felt, six pips up. Pick it up and throw it. It may keep rocking after it lands. When one face holds the top, the glass records its number and orange balls rain onto the table: sixteen for each pip. A six receives a small spectacle.

The reward has a rule. It does not make the throw more earned. For a moment, though, its abundance can make this outcome feel like the one the room wanted.

No random number decides the face. The program draws random values to scatter and tint the reward, but reads the die from its orientation. How your hand let go, the cube's shape and its contact with the felt have already done their work.

Return it with **RESET** and let it fall level from just above the table. Six can come back without surprise. A tilted release can produce another face. A cube's symmetry is a reason to investigate fairness, not proof that every way of releasing it gives equal chances.

The glass keeps the throws after their little storms have passed. Each face has a bar and a percentage; the running mean stands beside the model's 3.50. A short sequence can repeat one face or miss another without settling whether the die is fair.

Follow one face's share through several throws. Another face makes that share fall. The mean answers a different question: an outcome above the current mean pulls it up; one below pulls it down. Neither movement alone tells us that the die became fairer.

If all six faces were equally likely, their mean would be 3.5. No side of the cube bears that number. It belongs to a distribution, while the observed mean belongs to the throws made so far.

The glass cannot tell you whether your last three throws were sixes. It keeps frequencies and loses order, along with every landing position. **RESET** returns the die while keeping this record; **CLEAR** empties it. The instrument has decided what a throw leaves behind.

Two more dice wait in separate trays, one blue, one pink. Throw each into its own tray. A column stands up behind them when both results have arrived. Count its blue blocks, then its pink ones. The faces have become parts of a height.

Three and four make seven. Five and two make seven too. Can you bring the column back with different ingredients? The dice need not give you the pair you want. On the shelf, small copies keep the last twelve pairs in order, each colour still carrying its share. Two columns can reach the same level without looking the same.

Look only at their tops. Now look at the join between the colours. Each view lets you call something different the same. A total keeps an answer and gives up how it was assembled; the colours let some of that difference return.

How many pairs could make two? How many could make seven? Keep the order of the dice: blue one and pink six is another pair from blue six and pink one. If every face were equally likely and the two throws independent, seven would have six routes out of thirty-six, while two would have one. The middle would occur more often without either die preferring it. Your throws may not yet make that shape. Their conditions still matter.

The shelf eventually lets its oldest pair go. The total count continues. A history needs room; this one has been given twelve places. The larger column waits while you begin again. Returning a loose die adds nothing to it.

## The six that does not finish

A single die waits beside another column base. Throw it into the felt tray. Its number becomes a section of the column, one thin block for each pip. A three leaves three blocks and closes the chain. A six leaves six, but the flag stays raised. Throw again.

This time the first section remains. Another six gives you twelve and another turn. A three after that makes fifteen and closes the chain. The small sculpture beside your tray keeps this example visible: six, six, three. Its label says WORKED EXAMPLE. It is there to show a possibility, not to tell you what you threw.

The first die in this hall made a six into a larger rain of rewards. Here the same face does something else. It keeps an instruction open. Nothing inside the cube knows that six means again:

```gdscript
total += face
closed = face != 6
```

The number enters twice: once as an amount, once as a condition. From the same six faces, the rule can build a body taller than any single throw. Look at the joins in the column. Its height has a history, and the sections still let you read it.

The amber lid marks this display's limit: five throws. If the fifth is another six, the case reads CHAIN UNFINISHED. The rule would permit more; this apparatus has run out of room. That is a different ending from a die landing three.

RETURN DIE recovers it without adding a throw. NEW CHAIN clears the live column. Until then, a closed or limited chain stays as it is even if you throw again. Before clearing it, walk around what those few landings have made. Where did the permission to keep growing reside?

Here the last thirty outcomes remain in order. A coin resting on its edge is left out. Read the ribbon as steps, heads forward and tails back: where would a walker be now, and how far has it been? The simplest random walk is a coin on a beam; two rooms on, a block on a rail takes it.

# Random Cubes

Each cube remembers its coin flips. Build the arena where form encodes chance.

Declare the random cube.

```gdscript
class_name RandomEdgeCube
extends Node3D

@export var edge_chance: float = 0.5
var edge_profile: PackedInt32Array = PackedInt32Array()
```

A cube has 12 edges. Each edge is flipped on or off by a chance. The profile records the result.

Flip the edges.

```gdscript
func flip_edges() -> void:
    edge_profile.clear()
    for i in 12:
        edge_profile.append(1 if randf() < edge_chance else 0)
```

Twelve flips per cube. Each flip decides whether that edge is beveled, hollowed, or plain. The profile is the cube's signature.

Build the cube mesh from the profile.

```gdscript
func build_mesh() -> void:
    var st := SurfaceTool.new()
    st.begin(Mesh.PRIMITIVE_TRIANGLES)
    for i in 12:
        if edge_profile[i] == 1:
            _add_beveled_edge(st, i)
        else:
            _add_plain_edge(st, i)
    mesh_instance.mesh = st.commit()
```

The mesh assembles from per-edge rules. Beveled edges get a small chamfer; plain edges stay sharp. The visual is the profile made stone.

Place a field of cubes.

```gdscript
func populate_field() -> void:
    for x in 6:
        for y in 6:
            var cube := preload("res://commons/artifacts/randomness/random_edge_cube.tscn").instantiate()
            cube.position = Vector3(x * 1.5, 0.0, y * 1.5)
            cube.flip_edges()
            cube.build_mesh()
            add_child(cube)
```

36 cubes, 36 unique profiles. No two cubes match. The grid makes differences legible side by side.

Highlight the current cube on inspection.

```gdscript
func highlight(cube: Node3D, active: bool) -> void:
    cube.material_override.emission = Color.WHITE if active else Color.BLACK
    cube.material_override.emission_energy_multiplier = 0.3 if active else 0.0
```

Inspection tints the cube. The learner can see which one they are reading.

Readout the profile.

```gdscript
func readout(cube: RandomEdgeCube, label: Label3D) -> void:
    label.text = "profile: " + str(cube.edge_profile)
```

A label shows the 12-bit profile beside the cube. The number is the cube; the cube is the number.

Offer a dice throw.

```gdscript
func _on_dice_thrown(face: int) -> void:
    edge_chance = float(face) / 6.0
    for cube in cubes:
        cube.flip_edges()
        cube.build_mesh()
```

Throwing a die changes the edge chance for the whole field. The population responds to a single roll. Randomness scales.

You have seen form encoding chance. The next map, Random Rotate, moves randomness into three axes.
<<</MAP>>>

Save a profile to disk.

```gdscript
func save_profile(cube: RandomEdgeCube, slot: int) -> void:
    UserSettings.set_value("cubes/slot_%d" % slot, cube.edge_profile)
```

Any profile can be saved and reloaded later. Randomness becomes archivable.

More rolls produce more varied profiles. The field reads richer over time.

# Random_Cubes - Technical Tutorial

## Randomizing Geometry

The `random_edge_profile` element applies randomness to mesh geometry, not just transforms.

### Edge Profile Randomization

```gdscript
extends MeshInstance3D

@export var edge_variance: float = 0.3
@export var seed_offset: int = 0

func _ready():
    randomize_edges()

func randomize_edges():
    var rng = RandomNumberGenerator.new()
    rng.seed = hash(global_position) + seed_offset  # Deterministic per-position

    # Get mesh data
    var mesh_data = ArrayMesh.new()
    var vertices = get_base_cube_vertices()

    # Randomize each vertex along edges
    for i in range(vertices.size()):
        var v = vertices[i]
        # Only perturb edge vertices (not faces)
        if is_edge_vertex(v):
            v += Vector3(
                rng.randf_range(-edge_variance, edge_variance),
                rng.randf_range(-edge_variance, edge_variance),
                rng.randf_range(-edge_variance, edge_variance)
            )
        vertices[i] = v

    # Rebuild mesh with randomized vertices
    rebuild_mesh(mesh_data, vertices)
    mesh = mesh_data
```

### Position-Based Seeding

The key technique: **seed from position** for deterministic yet varied results.

```gdscript
# Each cube gets unique but reproducible randomness
rng.seed = hash(global_position)

# Benefits:
# - Same position = same shape (reproducible)
# - Different positions = different shapes (varied)
# - No need to store random values (stateless)
# - Regenerates identically on reload
```

### Random Object Spawner

The `random_object_spawner` creates dynamic randomness:

```gdscript
extends Node3D

@export var spawn_interval: float = 2.0
@export var spawn_radius: float = 3.0
@export var object_scenes: Array[PackedScene] = []

var rng = RandomNumberGenerator.new()

func _ready():
    rng.randomize()
    spawn_timer()

func spawn_timer():
    while true:
        await get_tree().create_timer(spawn_interval).timeout
        spawn_random_object()

func spawn_random_object():
    if object_scenes.is_empty():
        return

    # Random scene selection
    var scene = object_scenes[rng.randi() % object_scenes.size()]
    var instance = scene.instantiate()

    # Random position within radius
    var angle = rng.randf() * TAU
    var distance = rng.randf() * spawn_radius
    instance.position = Vector3(
        cos(angle) * distance,
        0,
        sin(angle) * distance
    )

    # Random rotation
    instance.rotation.y = rng.randf() * TAU

    add_child(instance)
```

## Geometric Randomization Strategies

```gdscript
# Strategy 1: Vertex displacement
func displace_vertices(vertices: Array, amount: float) -> Array:
    for i in range(vertices.size()):
        vertices[i] += random_vector3() * amount
    return vertices

# Strategy 2: Edge extrusion
func extrude_random_edges(mesh: ArrayMesh, count: int):
    var edges = get_edges(mesh)
    for i in range(count):
        var edge = edges[randi() % edges.size()]
        extrude_edge(mesh, edge, randf_range(0.1, 0.5))

# Strategy 3: Face subdivision
func subdivide_random_faces(mesh: ArrayMesh, probability: float):
    for face in get_faces(mesh):
        if randf() < probability:
            subdivide_face(mesh, face)
```

# Random_Cubes - Critical Reflection

## Forms That Could Have Been Otherwise

Each cube in this arena has randomized edges. Same algorithm, different seeds, different shapes.

This raises an ontological question: **what is a cube?**

The Platonic answer: a cube is an ideal form—six equal square faces, twelve equal edges, eight vertices. The randomized cubes in this map deviate from this ideal. Are they still cubes?

The answer depends on your ontology:
- **Essentialism**: A true cube has specific properties; these are imperfect approximations
- **Family resemblance**: These are "cube-like" objects sharing a common ancestor
- **Constructivism**: "Cube" is a category we impose; the boundary is arbitrary

The map suggests the constructivist view: the forms are recognizable as cubes because we recognize them, not because they meet an ideal specification.

## The Arena as Laboratory

The walled arena creates a controlled environment—a **laboratory** for geometric variation.

This is significant. Laboratories are spaces where:
- Variables are isolated
- Conditions are controlled
- Observation is prioritized
- Replication is possible

The map treats randomized geometry as an object of study. The walls exclude the outside world; the grid positions fix locations; only the edge profiles vary. This is **experimental design** for visual phenomena.

## What Randomness Reveals About Form

When edges are randomized, the cube's "cubeness" becomes visible precisely through its degradation.

We don't notice the platonic cube's regularity until we see what happens without it. The randomized edges make visible what was invisible: the constraint of geometric ideals.

This is a general principle: **randomness reveals structure by violating it**. We learn what rules exist by seeing them broken.

## The Spawner: Randomness in Time

The `random_object_spawner` adds temporal randomness to spatial randomness.

The static cubes embody randomness frozen at instantiation. The spawner embodies randomness ongoing—objects appearing unpredictably, positions varying, the arena never twice the same.

This distinction matters:
- **Static randomness**: Decided once, then fixed (the edge profiles)
- **Dynamic randomness**: Continuously generated (the spawner)

The QFEP's φΔE(S,t) term captures this: the rate of entropy change. Static randomness has φΔE = 0 (entropy set once, then constant). Dynamic randomness has φΔE > 0 (entropy continuously injected).

## The Politics of Variation

In industrial production, variation is defect. Manufacturing aims for identical copies—any deviation from spec is failure.

This map celebrates variation. Each cube is unique, and uniqueness is the point. This is a **queer** relationship to geometry: difference valued over sameness, variation over standardization.

The randomized cubes resist interchangeability. You cannot swap one for another and get "the same." Each has its history (its seed), its identity (its edges), its irreducibility.

## The Boundary of Recognition

How much can edges vary before a cube is no longer a cube?

The `edge_variance` parameter controls this. Low variance: recognizable cubes with slight imperfections. High variance: abstract forms that might have once been cubes.

This boundary is **perceptual**, not geometric. We recognize cubes because we've learned to. The randomization tests the limits of that learning—how much deviation can pattern recognition tolerate?

## Questions That Remain

1. **Is randomized form creative?** The cubes are not designed; they're generated. Is this creation? Or just calculation? Where is the authorship—in the algorithm, the parameters, the seed, the random function itself?

2. **Can geometry be decolonized?** The cube is a Western ideal, tied to Cartesian coordinates, orthogonal axes, right angles. Randomizing edges disrupts this regime. But does it escape it, or just add noise?

3. **What is the "natural" form?** Platonic geometry is rare in nature. Most forms are irregular, asymmetric, unique. Are randomized cubes more "natural" than perfect ones? Or is this a false dichotomy?

4. **How much entropy is enough?** At what variance does the cube become unrecognizable? At what point does variation become chaos? The edge of chaos applied to geometry.

## The QFEP Connection

This map applies the QFEP framework to form itself:

- **F (free energy)**: The cube template—the ordered ideal that randomness perturbs
- **E(S) (entropy)**: The edge variance—the disorder introduced into geometry
- **λ (modulator)**: The variance parameter—how strongly entropy affects form
- **φΔE (rate of change)**: Static (cubes) vs dynamic (spawner) randomness

The arena demonstrates that even the most basic geometric primitives—the cube—can be subject to entropy modulation. The grid is order; the randomized edges are chaos; the bounded variance keeps the system at the edge where cubes remain recognizable but unique.

This is the QFEP operating at the level of ontology: not just what systems do, but what systems *are* can oscillate between order and disorder.

# Random Cubes — Summary

Random_Cubes is the fourth map in the Randomness sequence. It takes the randomness the previous maps applied to positions and colours and applies it instead to form — the edge profile of each cube is decided by a random process, so two cubes on the same grid cell are different shapes.

The space is a small walled arena, roughly square, with a raised perimeter and a flat interior. Around the perimeter, dozens of instances of the same artifact — random_edge_profile — are arranged in ordered rows. Each instance generates its silhouette at spawn time by sampling a random offset at each vertex of a base cube, so the overall layout reads as geometric repetition while the individual shapes refuse to match.

Two spawners sit at the centre of the arena and produce further random objects on demand. A dark ambient sphere hangs above the spawners to mark the observation zone. The learner enters from an elevated corner, descends into the arena, and walks the perimeter comparing one cube to its neighbour. The arrangement argues that randomness does not need to operate on obvious parameters; any property can be a random variable, including the primitive geometry a sequence has spent several maps treating as fixed.

Within the sequence, Random_Cubes is the chapter on form. After Random_Noise_Types introduced spectral identity, this map pushes randomness into shape. Random_Rotate_Random_XYZ will next push it into orientation.
