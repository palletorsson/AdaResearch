# Together, out of time

Can twenty repetitions make something that none of them does alone?

We have met oscillation: a movement returns. In Change we measured a rate and gathered contributions. Bring those ideas into a room where several processes continue at once. Before asking whether the room is working, choose what you expect it to do.

<!-- @phase_rod_array -->

Twenty light rods turn above their small square bases. Each has a white tip, so you can follow a full turn without confusing one end with the other. Begin by watching one. Then let your attention spread across a row.

Press **ONE +**. Rod ten moves a little ahead. The other nineteen continue their agreement. Press it again. Is this rod late, early, or simply somewhere else in its own cycle? You need another clock before those words can mean anything.

Each press adds forty-five degrees to that rod's phase. It keeps the same six-second period. The difference is an offset, not a growing delay.

Now press **WAVE**. Follow the five columns. A pattern travels across the array while every rod remains attached to its own base. Adjacent columns differ by one fifth of a turn; the four rows repeat that relation. None of these little machines travels across the stage. What you see travelling is a relationship between them.

Only now press **RULE**.

```gdscript
angle = TAU * frequency * time + phase
```

Frequency counts turns per second. `TAU` converts a turn into radians. Time advances the angle; phase places a rotor within its cycle. The array is indexed as four rows of five, carrying forward the addresses we learned to give a grid.

Press **DRIFT**. The back two rows now run four percent faster than the front two. A difference in frequency keeps changing the difference in phase. Watch agreement loosen. The rows need 150 seconds to gain one complete turn on each other and realign. Their clock was already running before you pressed the button, so this is an interval between alignments, not a countdown promised from your press.

These buttons select configurations immediately; a rod may jump to its new angle. After that change, watch the continuing motion. **RESET** restores common phase and frequency while the elapsed clock keeps advancing.

<!-- @ -->

The wave needed difference. Making every rod agree would destroy that particular pattern. Being out of phase becomes a fault only in relation to something we demand: meet here, leave an opening, arrive together. What other movement could your body make alongside this one?

These rods are driven by an angle formula. They do not keep time because a restoring force pulls them back. A spring and a scripted rotor may repeat, but the repetition has different causes. The resemblance is a place to begin investigating.

<!-- @conveyor_backlog -->

Walk to the conveyor. A parcel drops onto the belt, travels toward its end and reaches the collection tray. The receiver flashes when it collects a box. Follow a single parcel before reading the numbers.

The inlet attempts a delivery every 1.2 seconds. The matched receiver can collect one arrived parcel every 1.2 seconds. There will still be boxes on the belt: transport takes time. An occupied machine is not necessarily a congested one.

Press **BACK UP**. The inlet keeps its interval; the receiver now collects at most one box every three seconds. Wait. Which change becomes visible first: a longer stay in the tray, a neighbouring box touching it, or a parcel losing its straight route?

The parcels have rigid bodies. Friction carries them across a moving collision surface. Gravity and contacts determine how they fall, tilt and press against each other. The sculpture at the end is not a mesh that grows because we asked for a larger sculpture. Its shape is made by the deliveries that have not yet left.

Press **RULE** and compare the account with the pile.

```gdscript
here = admitted - collected
```

The receiver only collects a parcel that has physically reached its end of the machine. Its schedule cannot collect a promise. If the inlet is occupied, admission waits. At forty-eight live parcels the feed also waits, and the console says so. That finite capacity belongs to this implementation; the model does not hide extra boxes by deleting them.

Now press **RECOVER**. The receiver can collect one arrived box every 0.6 seconds. Existing boxes must still reach it. Watch the heap change before calling the repair complete. Increasing the collection rate and erasing the history are different operations.

**RESET** clears this local parcel experiment and restores matched intervals. It does not rewind the museum's elapsed time.

<!-- @ -->

Every box followed the same admission rule. Every collection followed the same receiver rule. A difference between their intervals nevertheless changed the space. What counted as a successful delivery upstream became somebody else's obstruction downstream.

There is comedy in anticipating a clean handover and watching a parcel arrive at the wrong moment. There is also another form appearing in the interruption. For whom is the heap only waste? What would have to change for it to become a shelter, a route, a body worth keeping?

We have made those questions available; this first machine does not yet let you carry away its boxes or climb its heap as a designed route. Carry the distinction forward: a parameter can change a pattern, but the permissions of the body still need to be built.

<!-- @ -->

<!-- @paired_time -->

Two pale faces touch at their dark rims. Each carries a hand with a pink tip. Watch one pass the top, then watch the other. For a while, either could have told you where to look.

Press **DRIFT RIGHT**. At first the difference is small enough to miss. Stay for another turn. The right hand has gained something it keeps gaining.

Choose **SAME RATE**. The hands still disagree. They will carry this distance together for as long as the rule holds. Matching their speed has not recovered the time between them.

**ALIGN** brings the right hand to the left. If you leave the rates different, the agreement immediately begins to go. Try aligning once without first making the rates equal. How long do you need to watch before the difference belongs to what you see?

Gonzalez-Torres's paired clocks make shared time available through two ordinary objects. MoMA places the work in the context of his partner's AIDS diagnosis.[^paired-time] Here, both hands receive the same pulse from the game loop. Their disagreement has to be written in. Neither battery will fail; the program has given us an unusually easy power to bring them together again.

We can keep that power visible without pretending it is something we could have offered the lovers. The two rims remain in contact throughout.

<!-- @ -->

Next, gravity. The place the spring returned to acquires a body of its own. Will the centre remain still when another body answers it?

[^paired-time]: [MoMA, Felix Gonzalez-Torres, “Untitled” (Perfect Lovers), 1991](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/81074). Ada’s accelerated dials share one engine clock. Their programmed rate difference is a comparison, not a simulation of illness, battery failure or the historical work.

# Timing machines: period, phase and throughput

This hall follows Vectors_Act4b_Oscillation in Forces. It returns to the rate and accumulation ideas from Change. Its primary order is phase_rod_array then conveyor_backlog; the later gravity hall retains its position.

## Twenty rotors

A four-by-five indexed array uses `angle = TAU * frequency * elapsed + phase`. Base frequency is 1/6 Hz. ONE + changes index 9 by PI/4 modulo TAU. WAVE sets phase `(index % 5) * TAU/5`. DRIFT sets indices 10..19 to 1.04 times the base frequency. Relative frequency is 1/150 Hz, so the two groups align every 150 seconds. Controls switch configurations immediately; elapsed time remains continuous. RESET restores equal phases and frequencies, not elapsed zero. RULE toggles the displayed equation. Rotation is prescribed, not simulated by torque. The light rods are visual moving parts above collidable bases.

## The receiver

The existing conveyor_belt scene supplies the visual deck. A new StaticBody3D provides a moving surface, with world-oriented `constant_linear_velocity` of 0.9 m/s. Spawned RigidBody3D parcels have mass 0.5, side 0.44 m, friction 0.75 and bounce 0.05. Continuous collision detection is enabled. Parcel sleeping is disabled so a queue released on a moving static surface resumes contact-driven motion. The belt surface is 1.2 m above the floor; the narrow receiving tray has its floor at 0.45 m. Belt, side rails and receiving tray have colliders. Finite contacts and the physics timestep govern the resulting arrangement.

Feed attempts occur every 1.2 seconds. Each collection pulse removes at most the oldest live parcel whose local x is at least 2.9 m and whose centre has dropped below 1.12 m into the tray. The receiver intervals are MATCHED 1.2 s, BACKLOG 3.0 s, RECOVERING 0.6 s. A pulse with no arrived parcel does nothing. Entering a receiver mode restarts its interval timer; it does not reset the feed timer or existing parcels. RESET clears parcel bodies and local counts, restores matched operation and preserves elapsed time.

`admitted - collected == boxes.size()` is the accounting invariant. Admission pauses if the inlet is occupied or the 48-body cap is reached. Capacity is shown on the console. The experiment is bounded; it is not an unlimited source of physics bodies. Parcels are rigid bodies, not XR pickables, and the pile is not a certified climbing route.

## Review

Compare one phase offset before a phase wave, then frequency drift. Compare actual transport under matched intervals, backlog and recovery. Desktop scripted-pointer and physics tests accompany the first implementation. Headset reach, hand interaction, label comfort and live performance remain to be walked.

### Compare a rate with an offset

This encounter follows the phase rods and the conveyor in **Forces → Choreography**. The map ID retains the name `Change_Choreography`. Day Zero supplied the repeating update; Change supplied a rate and its accumulation; Oscillation and this hall supplied returning movement and phase. The two dials are an application of that existing toolbox.

Begin with RESET. Each hand makes one turn in twelve seconds. Use DRIFT RIGHT, wait thirty seconds and then reveal READING: the right hand has gained 36°. SAME RATE gives the right hand the left hand's speed while preserving both current positions. The gap stops growing. OFFSET +45° changes only the right position, immediately. ALIGN copies the left position to the right without changing either rate. If their rates still differ, alignment lasts only an instant.

HOLD / RUN pauses these two stored angles, not the player or the museum. READING reveals the current ratio and clockwise phase gap. RESET restores the original angles, equal rates, running state and hidden readout. No controls move the wall or its collider.

The one-line edit above halves the rate difference. Before running it, predict the gap after the same thirty seconds. The relative rate is `(1.02 - 1.0) / 12` turns per second. It gains 0.05 turns, or 18°. At a 4% difference the dials align every 300 seconds; at 2%, every 600 seconds. This is visual alignment modulo a turn, not recovery of a lost history.

### What the program keeps

The production score stores two angles. Each update adds its chosen rate multiplied by the same `delta`:

```gdscript
left_angle = fposmod(left_angle + TAU / PERIOD_SECONDS * delta, TAU)
right_angle = fposmod(right_angle + TAU / PERIOD_SECONDS * right_ratio * delta, TAU)
```

Changing `right_ratio` does not replace either angle. That is why changing speed has no immediate jump. `align()` assigns `right_angle = left_angle`; it has a different job. `fposmod` keeps the displayed angles within one turn. The code discards whole-turn counts, so its readout cannot tell you how many times one dial has lapped the other.

The renderer maps those angles onto identical single-hand dials; all fabrication, pointer controls and the museum player are supplied. Neither dial is a wall clock. They have no independent oscillator, battery or ageing mechanism. Engine stalls advance both through the same elapsed delta; the rate difference is authored explicitly. Full hardware clock synchronization, audio, independent oscillators and lifetime simulation are outside this first edit.

### Paired-time implementation

`paired_time_score.gd` is the state model: two scalar angles, a 12-second base period, a right-rate ratio, and a local pause flag. `advance(delta)` accepts finite positive elapsed seconds and wraps each accumulated angle within TAU. Both dials share the engine delta; no random hardware drift is claimed. `paired_time.gd` constructs two matching 1.94-metre dials with 60 marks each, above a solid display wall, and rotates only their hand nodes. Readout updates reuse the labels and meshes. The station supplies seven pointer controls; it adds no player or camera.

Tests exercise no-jump rate changes, retained phase at equal rates, alignment without rate changes, deliberate phase offset, local hold/resume, reset, equal elapsed time at 30 and 144 updates per second, invalid elapsed inputs, bounded long updates, native museum captures and side-route support. Pointer dispatch is tested; physical XR comfort and Quest frame time remain unverified.

# Difference has a timing

A common substrate does not guarantee a common outcome. Twenty identical rotors admit many collective patterns through the relation between phase and frequency. Equal phase makes one pattern; a phase gradient makes another. Neither is an entropy measurement. The exhibit invites a QFEP question about which differences a system can sustain, without pretending that a measured threshold has already been found.

The conveyor locates failure in a relation. A source can meet its own schedule while exceeding a receiver's capacity. Local success becomes a downstream obstruction. The queue gives that account a shape, but its count cannot specify the shape: contact, friction, gravity and earlier arrivals participate in it.

The comic encounter depends on an expectation. A missed handover can reveal the assumptions in that expectation. Queer possibility need not end at mocking the machine or restoring its intended productivity. The accumulated body may invite another use. This first implementation makes the pile visible, while carrying and deliberate climbing remain unimplemented. Naming those permissions keeps speculation connected to what a visitor can actually do.

The forty-eight-body limit and blocked-inlet rule are also part of the scene. They protect a finite simulation by refusing further admissions. Which demands are paused, which existing bodies remain, and who can change that rule? The console exposes the choice rather than treating it as the world's natural limit.

A faster receiver can still miss a parcel outside its collection region. This machine recognises arrival through position and height: a parcel must drop into the tray. Spatial access and service frequency are separate conditions. Improving one does not guarantee the other.

### The shared pulse

The artist reference makes the difference between an algorithmic return and mortal time consequential. MoMA locates the paired clocks in the context of Gonzalez-Torres's partner's AIDS diagnosis. Ada exposes the exceptional guarantees of a digital pair: both receive the same update, neither ages, and disagreement is a parameter we chose. Equal rates can carry a difference indefinitely. Alignment is a separate intervention. These are inspectable relations, not equivalents for love, illness or loss. Keeping the artist's context beside the technical account prevents synchronization from exhausting what the work can mean.
