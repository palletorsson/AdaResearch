# The view has partitions

<!-- @color_constellation_office -->

A sheet of cyan stands higher than you. Beyond it, magenta crosses your view. The pale floor continues through both colours. For a moment, the room seems to have several distances at once.

Step sideways. A dark patch narrows, then opens into blue. Nothing in the office has moved. You have changed which panes stand between you and the far wall.

There are twelve sheets, staggered across the floor. Some turn away from the others. Their edges stop short of enclosing a room. Follow one edge until you can pass it; the colour that covered the next space falls behind you.

A desk waits to one side. Two more places to work are scattered among the partitions. Sit in imagination, or stand beside a chair and look back through the colours. How much of this office could you call your own without being able to close it?

![Looking through the overlapping colour planes in the museum office.](/book-review/doc/book/iterations/2026-10-01-office-open-colour/layers-glass.png)

*The overlap changes with your position. The panes and the gaps remain still.*

At the entrance, press **FILTER / OPAQUE**. The view closes into coloured surfaces. Take the same route again. The next opening is harder to see, although it has not become narrower.

Press once more. A chair returns through a pane. The pane still stops your body.

In filter mode, the surface lets the view behind it contribute to the colour:

```gdscript
surface.albedo_color.a = 0.52
```

An overlap adds another tint. The pale backing keeps the view bright enough to follow through the layers. This is a blend of screen colours, not a simulation of the wavelengths passing through real glass.

In Paint, your mark joined someone else's on a shared surface. Here the colour between two people changes what each can see. Opaque walls can interrupt that exchange. Gaps still let a view reach around them. Privacy depends on where you stand, where someone else stands, and how far the partition extends.

<!-- @color_space_navigator -->

Find a gap between the panes and leave the office. At the smaller models, find the red corner of the cube, then look for red around the cylinder. The colour is familiar. Its neighbours have moved.

You have been walking through an arrangement of coloured space. These models arrange colour values in space. In the cube, increasing the red channel moves a sample along one axis. Green and blue have their own directions. A colour has become an address again, but the address now comes from the colour itself.

![The museum’s RGB cube and HSV cylinder, each displaying five hundred colour samples.](/book-review/doc/book/iterations/2026-09-28-walls-a-view-from-inside/colour-coordinates.png)

*RGB channels become three straight directions. In HSV, hue goes around, saturation moves outward and value changes height.*

The RGB model assigns a sample's position and colour together. In this shortened version of the construction:

```gdscript
position = (Vector3(r, g, b) - Vector3.ONE * 0.5) * cube_size
colour = Color(r, g, b)
```

Follow the RGB cube upwards: that direction adds green. Across in the HSV cylinder, rising changes value, as height did among the pillars. Hue goes around its axis; saturation moves outward. Up is still up in the room, but the colour rule gives it another job.

Neither contains every colour as a visible point: each holds five hundred samples. The gaps belong to the display we have built. Even the black corner has been brightened slightly so that you can find it. To show a value, the exhibit has already made an accommodation. What would disappear if it refused?

<!-- @gradient_interpolator -->

At the next bench, red and blue mark the ends of a row of sixteen small cubes. Follow the colours between them, then press **RGB / HSV**. Leave the endpoints alone.

The middle changes. The agreement about where to begin and end did not decide how to travel.

![The same sixteen gradient cubes between red and blue in RGB and HSV modes.](/book-review/doc/book/iterations/2026-09-23-walls-view-and-passage/same-endpoints.png)

*The endpoints remain red and blue. Changing the interpolation changes the colours encountered between them.*

In RGB mode, the calculation is:

```gdscript
col = ca.lerp(cb, t)
```

Here `t` runs from zero to one across the sixteen samples. Red decreases as blue increases. In HSV mode, the program instead chooses the shorter arc around hue, while interpolating saturation and value. For these endpoints that keeps a bright passage through magenta, where the RGB passage was darker. Try **COLOUR A** and **COLOUR B** to choose another pair from eight presets. Does the distinction remain as conspicuous?

The path was chosen in code. The number of places along it was chosen too. Sixteen cubes let us inspect a transition without pretending we have occupied every value between its ends.

# Three ways to pass

Begin with a chair seen through two coloured panes. Step sideways until only one pane covers it. The chair, its position and its material have stayed fixed. Follow the edge to a gap and walk around the pane. A view through a surface does not promise passage through it.

Return to the entrance and press FILTER / OPAQUE. Repeat your route. Inspect `toggle_office_glass()` in `algorithms/color/color_mixing/color_constellation_office.gd`: it changes the surface material and shadow setting, without rebuilding the collider. The twelve panes are positioned by `PANES` in `office_planes.gd`. Each row contains x, z, yaw, width, height and colour. The geometry uses transforms already introduced in Transformation and a repeated construction already introduced in Arrays.

The new decision is how the surface combines with the view behind it. Both states use `BLEND_MODE_MIX`. Filter mode enables alpha transparency with alpha 0.52; opaque mode disables transparency and sets alpha to 1.0. Start with one pane. Change only its colour, then compare the same chair with and without a second pane in front of it. Keep the supplied pale backing so dark surroundings do not obscure the furniture. This operation blends rendered RGB colours; it is not a model of spectral absorption. Transparent surfaces are sorted for rendering, so their order contributes to the result.

For a first alteration, change one row's yaw, rebuild, and find a new overlap by walking. Keep widths and gaps fixed until you have checked which routes remain possible. The furniture, support geometry and comparison button are supplied apparatus; rebuilding them is not the learning target here.

At the navigator, identify the RGB axes. Each displayed sample uses its three channels as coordinates relative to the model centre. Locate the same general colour region in the HSV cylinder. Hue is now an angle, saturation a radius, value a height. Compare up in each model: RGB increases green, while HSV increases value, returning to the vertical rule of the Pillar field. Each model shows 500 samples, not every colour. Inspect the black corner's deliberate brightness adjustment.

At the interpolator, leave the initial red and blue endpoints unchanged and switch RGB / HSV. Describe the middle before reading `_recalculate()` and `_lerp_hsv()` in `algorithms/color/gradient_interpolator/gradient_interpolator.gd`. RGB linearly interpolates channels. HSV chooses the shorter hue arc, then interpolates saturation and value. Use COLOUR A and COLOUR B to choose another pair and repeat.

Carry three distinctions into Pattern_Disco: a material can change a view, coordinates can organise values, and an interpolation rule can choose a path. Keep the floor arrangement while changing its palette, then keep the palette while changing the pattern. Compare what each change makes visible.

# Colour as partition, coordinate and path

## Office — current layout, 1 October 2026

The active placement is `color_constellation_office:0:0:1#layout:planes#compare:visibility#plinth:0`. It remains at map cell (23,20), seated on the museum floor. `office_planes.gd` supplies twelve independent QuadMesh sheets, 2.75–3.55 m high, with thin matching box colliders. Fine edge strips and foot clamps make the obstacles legible. Three desks, monitors and chairs retain the office association without enclosing eight workstations.

The staggered transverse panes alternate the central route. Three smaller angled screens interrupt the rectangular pattern. A pale floor, interrupted side wings, rear opening and two overhead strips provide a neutral ground for the colour; a central ceiling slot stays open to the museum sky. The display backing is unshaded and does not cast shadows, so the global dark sky cannot erase the comparison. It does not simulate colour transmitted onto the floor by lights.

Each filter uses one double-sided quad, alpha transparency at 0.52, normal blending, unshaded colour and no cast shadow. One quad avoids applying the tint twice as would happen with two faces of a thin glass box. Fog is disabled on the filter itself; the background retains the museum's rendering. FILTER / OPAQUE disables transparency, sets alpha to 1.0 and enables shadow casting, while preserving RGB values, transforms and the exact collision bodies. Colour filtering here is layered RGB tint, not wavelength-dependent glass absorption. A multiplication trial darkened even a white pane in the current Compatibility renderer, and was rejected on visual inspection. The retained alpha mix is order-dependent; transparent sorting is a rendering limit, especially at intersecting sheets.

`layout=cubicles` retains the earlier forty-pane layout. No layout selects the older eighteen-pane installation and its `mixture=bench/light/retina` variants. These are separate configurations; mixture does not modify the active open office. Reapplying the same placement configuration does not rebuild it.

## Colour coordinates

`color_space_navigator:180:1.2:1#mount:comparison#plinth:0` adds an integral support for the two small models. The 0.7-metre RGB cube sits entirely above the support; its 500 seeded samples use colour channels as local coordinates. Another 500 samples populate the HSV cylinder: hue angle, saturation radius, value height. Internal names still say HSL in places, but the code calls `Color.from_hsv` and the displayed label says HSV. This is an inspectable model, not an interactive colour picker or a body-sized room. The black corner is slightly brightened for visibility.

## Interpolation

`gradient_interpolator:180:0.95:1#mount:comparison#plinth:0` adds its own 0.95-metre support and standing canonical control panel. The comparison configuration enlarges the sixteen cubes to 0.045 metres with 0.009-metre gaps. COLOUR A and COLOUR B advance through eight presets; RGB / HSV toggles the calculation while preserving the endpoints. Other placements retain the original rack and dimensions.

RGB mode calls `ca.lerp(cb, t)` at `t = i / (step_count - 1)`. HSV mode wraps the hue difference onto the shorter arc and linearly interpolates saturation and value. Red to blue therefore compares a darker RGB interior with a saturated magenta HSV interior. Other endpoint pairs need not show the same contrast.

# An invitation to look

The coloured sheets stop short of enclosing a room. A chair is visible through two of them, concealed by an opaque version of the same surfaces, or visible again from another angle. The comparison separates visibility from passage, but the gaps complicate its promise of privacy: opacity is local to a surface, while a view can go around its edge.

The pale backing is a further condition. The earlier dark render concealed the overlaps; the surrounding architecture and material treatment had to change for them to become legible. The exhibit's setting participates in its argument.

A queer reading can begin with the inadequacy of one permission as a description of another. Being visible, being accessible, and being able to act are separable conditions. The simulation gives us a controlled example; it does not make every social threshold reducible to a collider. Ask what other bodies, heights or modes of navigation would change the encounter. The current capsule and standing controls supply one answer, not the possible body in general.

The colour models disclose another selection. Five hundred samples make each model inspectable. The black corner is deliberately brightened to remain visible. The display changes the thing it asks us to read. Neither an accusation of failure nor a claim to an untouched original is necessary: name the accommodation and ask whom it helps.

Finally, RGB and HSV interpolation keep endpoints while producing different interiors. This gives a precise limit to the claim that shared code makes every transition available: the actual algorithm chooses a route, and sixteen samples expose only part of it. Ask what the shorter hue arc excludes, or what would happen if a different path were authored. Those are proposed experiments, not hidden controls in this room.
