# What chance may change

Four works share the gallery. Walk toward the painting on the floor, then take either side around its frame. The dartboard waits to the right, the pipes farther along to the left, and a changing particle study near the rear. Each gives chance a different job. The estimation the last room promised is the dartboard's job.

<!-- @pollock_painting_in_3d -->

Stand at the near edge of the canvas. Find a thin line, a broad mark and a cluster of small splatters. Wait for another layer, then follow the brush cursor as it traces a path. Walk around the frame. A mark that was distant is now close enough to inspect.

This is a Pollock-inspired generative homage, not a reconstruction of a particular painting. The program chooses positions, colours from a fixed palette, drip sizes and line widths. A line begins with a sampled direction, then turns by small sampled angles as it advances. At an edge, the direction reflects back toward the canvas. Chance can bend the route; it cannot enlarge the canvas or invent a colour outside the palette.

The marks are painted into a two-dimensional image shown on the floor. When a new layer appears, the small three-dimensional cursor retraces the path of one of its lines, which is already painted. It replays a procedure; it is not a brush whose movement you are controlling, and the drops are not a simulation of falling paint.

Choose a passage you like. Which part could have arrived differently? Which part was already decided by the palette, line rule, canvas and interval between additions? Calling the result random can make those authored choices disappear from view. The work becomes more interesting when we keep them visible.

<!-- @monte_carlo_dartboard -->

Move to the dartboard's console on the right. Press RESET. If new marks appear, press AUTO to pause them and RESET again. Press THROW and find the new point. Compare its location with the circle, then check which counter changed. Add a few more points individually before using AUTO to build a larger sample.

An inside point increases both the inside count and the total. An outside point increases the outside count and the total; the inside count stays where it was. Before requesting another point, predict what each possibility would do to the estimate. Check with THROW, and explain the change from the counters before looking at the distance from pi.

The circle is inscribed in a square. When points are sampled uniformly across that square, the fraction landing inside estimates the fraction of area occupied by the circle. Estimating by sampling is what the name on this bench, Monte Carlo, has meant since 1949.[^monte-carlo] Multiplying that fraction by four gives the displayed estimate of pi:

`estimate = 4 × inside / total`

A growing sample does not require every new estimate to improve. Repeat the prediction when many points are already present: one addition usually changes the ratio less. A steadier number still depends on how the points were sampled.

THROW requests a generated sample. You are not aiming a physical dart with your hand. A sampler that favoured the centre would tend to count too many points inside; adding more of those points would not repair the bias. Pause AUTO and RESET to compare another run. The counters stop at 500 darts, so RESET also begins a new sample once the count has filled. The board itself shows only the newest 300 marks: past that, the picture forgets points the numbers still include.

Here chance chooses the evidence. The circle's boundary and the counting rule remain fixed. Who gets to decide that the sampling procedure covers the space fairly enough for the answer we want?

<!-- @pipe_dream -->

Cross to the pipe study on the left. Follow a length to its next corner, then look for a place where the path doubles back near an earlier passage. If growth has finished by the time you arrive, the whole retained path is still available to inspect. The colour shifts gradually from one length to the next, swinging between cyan and magenta twice over the run, so two neighbouring passages in clearly different colours were not laid one after the other: the pipe came back to that place later in the run.

The pipe advances in one of six axis directions. A preference sometimes keeps it moving straight; otherwise the program shuffles possible turns and takes a direction that stays inside its bounds. It rejects an immediate reversal. It does not keep a list of occupied cells, so crossing an earlier route is allowed.

The default run stops after 180 segments. A dense tangle therefore does not certify that the volume has been filled, or that every corner was reached. The limit ends the run whether or not its shape looks complete to us.

Look across the empty spaces between pipes. The same local rules could have left different gaps. What would change if a previous visit made a place forbidden? That would add a memory and a new restriction, not merely turn up the amount of randomness.

<!-- @extreme_randomness -->

The particle study occupies the last bay on the right. Stay through a full cycle. Its five chapters change about every ten seconds. Watch for a change in the particles' arrangement as well as their movement; the chapter transition restages them.

For this gallery, every chapter stays inside the same spherical boundary. A particle that would escape is brought back to the edge and its outward velocity reflected. This added limit keeps the changing study above the floor and within its bay.

In the first chapter, random forces change velocities that persist from one frame to the next. Damping slows them, and a boundary turns escaping particles back. Successive positions retain a history. They are not independent points redrawn across the whole volume.

The noise chapter follows a smooth field made from sine and cosine functions in this implementation. No random draw enters that motion, and its caption says it is not Perlin noise. The last room's coherent heights changed with SEED; this field has no seed to change. The pattern chapter adds spiral motion and oscillation to its opening arrangement. The emergence chapter combines separation, alignment and cohesion with small random nudges. The evolution chapter ranks particles by their distance from a moving target; the leading tenth influence the others.

These are five sketches of different procedures, not five strengths of the same white noise. Nor is the final chapter a population that breeds: the same fixed set of particles keeps moving under a selection rule. A title gives us a starting question; the update rule tells us what is actually happening.

<!-- @ -->

Look back toward the painting. In one work, chance chose marks; in another, samples to count. It chose permitted directions for the pipe and different kinds of variation within the particle study. In every case something decided what a random choice was permitted to change.

Carry that question into the pheromone terrain. The pipe did not remember which places it had passed; the next walkers will leave a signal that can enter a later decision. What changes when a visit helps recruit another visit? Random Game still waits ahead, where such distinctions will become questions of support and timing.

[^monte-carlo]: Nicholas Metropolis and Stanisław Ulam, “The Monte Carlo method”, *Journal of the American Statistical Association* 44 (1949): use random samples to estimate a quantity, here an area. The name is the casino's; Metropolis said it was chosen for an uncle of Ulam's who borrowed money from relatives to gamble there. The mushrooms' rejected candidates are its other classic form, accept–reject.

# Four uses of a random draw

Read these works by following a sampled value into the operation that consumes it. Their shared use of random numbers does not make their outputs the same kind of process.

## A mark and a path

`algorithms/randomness/proceduralrandomness/movementbased/scenes/pollock2d/paint_dripping_2d.gd` draws into an `Image`. `_add_drip()` samples a position, palette index and radius. `_add_line()` begins at a sampled position and repeatedly turns a direction:

```gdscript
direction = direction.rotated(rng.randf_range(-line_curviness, line_curviness)).normalized()
var next_pos: Vector2 = points[-1] + direction * line_segment_length
```

Boundary checks reflect the direction when a step would leave the canvas. The resulting line segments are rasterized into the image; `stroke_path_generated` also sends their path to the 3D host. In `pollock_painting_in_3d.gd`, the host shows the SubViewport texture on a framed floor sprite and moves a cursor along that path. The gallery token chooses `#support:floor`.

This is a generative homage using marks and paths. There is no falling-paint collision calculation, and the visitor does not steer the cursor.

## A sample that contributes to an estimate

`algorithms/randomness/monte_carlo_dartboard/monte_carlo_dartboard.gd` draws two uniform coordinates in a unit square. `_throw_dart()` classifies the point against an inscribed circle, adds one to the total and, if inside, one to the inside count.

For a uniform square sampler:

`area(circle) / area(square) = pi / 4`

Therefore `4 × inside_count / total_count` estimates pi. An additional point can increase or decrease that estimate; convergence is not a promise of improvement after every throw. Sampling bias changes what the count measures.

The museum token selects `#disclosure:works` and `#controls:compact#control_front:1.0`. `museum_exhibit_stage.gd` relocates the existing button panel to a reachable console while preserving its signals. AUTO toggles continuous sampling; RESET clears the counts without toggling AUTO. Pause AUTO before making a sequence of individual THROW observations. The default maximum is 500 darts.

## A direction admitted by local rules

`algorithms/randomness/pipedream/PipeDream.gd` grows a path in six cardinal directions. `_choose_next_direction()` first gives straight-ahead motion a chance, then tests a shuffled list that excludes immediate reversal. `_is_outside_bounds()` rejects candidate endpoints beyond the configured box.

The default bounds extend ±6 m in x/z and ±4 m in y. Scaling the artifact to 0.25 gives a centreline volume 3 × 2 × 3 m; the pipe and joint radii add a small margin. Lifting its root 1.15 m keeps that volume above the gallery floor. `#residue:path` retains every segment of the run.

There is no occupancy set. The path can revisit earlier positions, and its 180-segment budget does not guarantee full coverage of the box. Changing that rule to reject visited cells would change the set of available choices at every later step.

## An update rule that changes with the chapter

`algorithms/randomness/proceduralrandomness/particlerandomness/extrem_randomness.gd` uses `#chapter:all` to cycle its five existing chapters. `start_demo()` restages the same particles at each transition; their update function then changes.

| Chapter | Procedure |
| --- | --- |
| Chaos | Add random forces to retained velocities, damp velocity and reflect particles at a radial boundary. |
| Noise | Sample a smooth field from the local `noise3()` helper, which is a product of sine and cosine terms rather than an implementation of Perlin noise. |
| Pattern | Combine spiral motion and oscillation with the opening sampled arrangement. |
| Emergence | Apply separation, alignment, cohesion and random nudges. |
| Evolution | Rank distance to a moving target; the leading tenth influence the other particles under the default selection rule. |

The particle count stays fixed. There is no reproduction, no heavy-tail sampler and no basis for interpreting all five chapters as independent uniform draws. The stage is a comparison of procedures, each with different dependencies and retained state.

The gallery token also opts into `#gallery_radius:7`. After restaging and each update, this boundary confines the entire particle sphere and reflects outward velocity. At scale 0.2 its diameter is 2.8 m, and the 1.5 m lift leaves a 0.1 m gap above the floor. The unconfigured artifact keeps its original behavior; the restored gallery is a bounded presentation of all five chapters.

Return to the room's question before entering Random Game: which variables may chance alter, and which constraints keep acting after the draw?

# Randomness_Examples_of_Randomness - Technical Documentation

## Core Concept in Code

### Pollock Drip Painting

```gdscript
extends Node3D

@export var drip_count := 500
@export var canvas_size := Vector2(10, 10)
@export var gravity := 9.8
@export var viscosity := 0.1

var line_renderer: ImmediateMesh
var rng := RandomNumberGenerator.new()

func _ready():
    rng.randomize()
    line_renderer = ImmediateMesh.new()
    generate_painting()

func generate_painting():
    line_renderer.clear_surfaces()
    line_renderer.surface_begin(Mesh.PRIMITIVE_LINES)

    for i in range(drip_count):
        simulate_drip()

    line_renderer.surface_end()

func simulate_drip():
    # Random start position (hand position over canvas)
    var pos = Vector3(
        rng.randf_range(-canvas_size.x/2, canvas_size.x/2),
        rng.randf_range(0.5, 2.0),  # Height above canvas
        rng.randf_range(-canvas_size.y/2, canvas_size.y/2)
    )

    # Random initial velocity (arm movement)
    var vel = Vector3(
        rng.randf_range(-2, 2),
        rng.randf_range(-0.5, 0.5),
        rng.randf_range(-2, 2)
    )

    var color = random_color()
    var prev_pos = pos

    # Simulate until paint hits canvas
    while pos.y > 0:
        line_renderer.surface_set_color(color)
        line_renderer.surface_add_vertex(prev_pos)
        line_renderer.surface_add_vertex(pos)

        prev_pos = pos

        # Physics
        vel.y -= gravity * 0.01
        vel *= (1.0 - viscosity * 0.01)
        vel += random_air_resistance()

        pos += vel * 0.01

func random_color() -> Color:
    # Pollock palette: blacks, whites, yellows, reds
    var colors = [
        Color.BLACK,
        Color.WHITE,
        Color(0.9, 0.8, 0.2),  # Yellow
        Color(0.8, 0.2, 0.2),  # Red
        Color(0.3, 0.3, 0.3),  # Gray
    ]
    return colors[rng.randi() % colors.size()]

func random_air_resistance() -> Vector3:
    return Vector3(
        rng.randfn(0, 0.1),
        rng.randfn(0, 0.05),
        rng.randfn(0, 0.1)
    )
```

### Random Butterflies

```gdscript
class_name RandomButterfly
extends Node3D

var velocity := Vector3.ZERO
var target := Vector3.ZERO
var rng := RandomNumberGenerator.new()
var wing_phase := 0.0

@export var speed := 2.0
@export var wander_strength := 1.0
@export var bounds := Vector3(5, 3, 5)

func _ready():
    rng.randomize()
    pick_new_target()

func _process(delta):
    # Wing flapping
    wing_phase += delta * 10
    animate_wings()

    # Movement toward target with randomness
    var direction = (target - position).normalized()
    var wander = random_wander()

    velocity = velocity.lerp(direction * speed + wander, delta * 2)
    position += velocity * delta

    # Look in movement direction
    if velocity.length() > 0.1:
        look_at(position + velocity, Vector3.UP)

    # Pick new target when close
    if position.distance_to(target) < 1.0:
        pick_new_target()

func random_wander() -> Vector3:
    return Vector3(
        rng.randfn(0, wander_strength),
        rng.randfn(0, wander_strength * 0.5),
        rng.randfn(0, wander_strength)
    )

func pick_new_target():
    target = Vector3(
        rng.randf_range(-bounds.x, bounds.x),
        rng.randf_range(0.5, bounds.y),
        rng.randf_range(-bounds.z, bounds.z)
    )

func animate_wings():
    # Oscillate wing rotation
    var angle = sin(wing_phase) * 0.5
    $LeftWing.rotation.z = angle
    $RightWing.rotation.z = -angle
```

## Implementation Notes and Complexity

Each example in the gallery is a small standalone demonstration that samples Godot's randf, randi, or randf_range and uses the samples to drive a visible behaviour. The underlying pseudo-random number generator is a Mersenne Twister with a 2 to the 19937 minus 1 period, which is effectively infinite for any realistic number of samples. Samples are O(1) to draw, and the cost of each demonstration is dominated by the rendering rather than by the random generation.

Seeding is the operation that makes a random sequence reproducible. Calling seed(value) resets the PRNG's state; subsequent calls to the sampling functions produce a deterministic sequence conditional on the seed. The map's gallery exposes a seed control so the learner can save a configuration and replay it. Without seeding, each run of the game produces a different gallery, which is often the desired behaviour; with seeding, the gallery becomes a fixed artifact for a given seed.

Distribution shape is the next concern. randf samples uniformly from zero to one; randf_range samples uniformly from an interval. Neither produces Gaussian samples; Gaussian requires either the Box-Muller transform applied to two uniform samples or a rejection-sampling approach, both of which the map demonstrates. The Box-Muller approach is O(1) per sample but involves a logarithm and a sine, which are expensive compared to a uniform draw.

Within the sequence, Examples_of_Randomness is the orientation map. It introduces the sampling primitives the rest of the Randomness sequence will use, and it demonstrates that the primitives are themselves composed of implementation decisions — seed, distribution, range. The map's gallery is a catalogue of primitives, and the catalogue is the vocabulary the sequence will exercise.

# What chance may change

Restore the four existing works that the museum route had omitted: `pollock_painting_in_3d`, `monte_carlo_dartboard`, `pipe_dream` and `extreme_randomness`. This hall sits immediately before `Random_Game` in the revised randomness museum.

The Pollock-inspired floor painting is the first and largest focal work. Its full-size frame gives the visitor a reason to change viewpoint and walk around an image. Monte Carlo provides the interactive middle stop; a compact console keeps the original buttons within reach. Pipe growth and the cycling particle study occupy separate observation bays farther along. A 15 × 25 m plan, stable floor, two routes around the painting and a clear aisle beside the rear bay permit visitors to linger or pass.

The technical comparison concerns what receives random input. The painter samples mark properties and changes of direction within an authored palette and canvas. Monte Carlo samples a square and estimates the circle's area fraction. Pipe Dream samples permitted local directions while retaining its path. Extreme Randomness cycles five different update rules, including rules that preserve velocities, consult other particles or use deterministic trigonometric fields. None of these observations supports calling the whole gallery unstructured chaos.

The critical question is concrete: what is each random choice permitted to change, and who authored that permission? A random mark cannot amend the palette. A random point does not negotiate its scoring boundary. A pipe turn cannot leave its box. A particle's rank depends on the chosen target. The gallery makes those limits inspectable without requiring one universal ranking from order to disorder.

The exit prepares Random Game by separating learned rules from uncertain events. The prose does not claim butterflies, physical paint splashes, heavy-tailed samples or a reproduction of a historical painting; those are not the four works restored here.

# Randomness_Examples_of_Randomness - Critical Documentation

## Philosophical Framing

### Art Without Artist

Pollock's drip paintings raised the question: if the artist doesn't control where paint lands, is it still art? Critics dismissed it as random splattering. Defenders argued the artist's body, gesture, intention created the work—the randomness was channeled, not abandoned.

Algorithmic art pushes further: the human doesn't even make the gesture. The algorithm generates; we observe. Where is the art? In the algorithm's design? In our perception? In the randomness itself?

### The Screensaver as Accidental Art

The Windows Pipes screensaver was designed to prevent screen burn-in—a utilitarian function. Yet it became mesmerizing. Office workers watched it instead of working. It demonstrated that random generation could be aesthetically compelling without artistic intention.

This is a democratization of sorts: art emerging from code written for practical purposes, appreciated by millions who didn't know they were appreciating art.

### Extreme Randomness and the Limits of Chaos

At maximum chaos, pattern disappears. Pure noise has no structure, no rhythm, no focal point. This is the E(S) term at maximum—entropy so high that prediction becomes impossible, recognition fails, meaning dissolves.

The "extrem_randomness" exhibit shows where aesthetics breaks down. Some randomness is interesting; too much is just noise. The λ parameter has a sweet spot.

## Politics and Assumptions

### Pollock and Masculinity

Pollock's image—the tortured genius, drunk, aggressive, splattering paint in barn-sized studios—is aggressively masculine. Action painting was marketed as physically demanding, emotionally raw, masculine.

When we algorithmically recreate Pollock, do we reproduce this gender politics? The drip algorithm has no gender, but the reference does. What does it mean to cite Pollock without his machismo?

### Screensavers and Labor Time

Screensavers emerged when computers were expensive and burn-in was a real risk. But they persisted long after LCD screens made them unnecessary—because workers liked watching them.

The Pipes screensaver represented stolen moments of non-work: watching random generation instead of generating value. In capitalism, this is subversive. The screensaver as micro-resistance.

### Simulation vs Preservation

The "random_butterflies" simulates flight patterns. But butterfly populations are collapsing—climate change, habitat loss, pesticides. We simulate what we're destroying.

Is this documentation or replacement? Does simulating butterflies help preserve them (raising awareness) or replace them (why save real butterflies when we have virtual ones)?

### The Aesthetics of Chaos

Who decides that Pollock drips are art and pure noise is not? This is an aesthetic judgment that pretends to be objective. "Good" randomness has structure; "bad" randomness is featureless.

But this preference for structure is cultural. Some traditions value emptiness, silence, the void. Maximum entropy might be aesthetic in a different framework.

## Queer Readings

### Against Mastery

Traditional art values mastery—control over materials, intentional creation, the artist as author. Pollock surrendered some control; algorithms surrender more.

This is a queer move: refusing mastery, embracing what emerges, letting the material participate in creation. The artist as collaborator with chance, not commander of outcome.

### The Butterfly as Queer Figure

Butterflies embody transformation: caterpillar → chrysalis → butterfly. This is a queer narrative—becoming other than what you were, emergence through radical change.

The random flight patterns suggest another queerness: unpredictability, refusal to follow straight lines, movement that can't be captured or predicted.

### Mess as Method

Pollock's drips are messy—paint pools, drips overlap, colors mix. This is anti-cleanliness, anti-order, anti-domesticity.

Queerness often embraces mess: the messy realities of bodies, the messy negotiations of non-normative relationships, the refusal of tidiness that heteronormative domestic life demands.

### Extreme Randomness as Dissolution

At maximum chaos, boundaries dissolve. Form becomes formless. Identity becomes indeterminate. This is queer dissolution—the ego boundary becoming porous, the self merging with environment.

The "extrem_randomness" exhibit might be read as ego death, as queer self-dissolution, as the limit where individual identity becomes noise.

## Questions That Remain Open

1. **Can algorithms be artists?** If randomness generates beauty, who is the artist—the programmer, the algorithm, no one?

2. **Is there a universal aesthetic of randomness?** Do all cultures find the same level of chaos pleasing?

3. **What is preserved in simulation?** When we simulate butterflies, what aspects of butterfly-ness transfer?

4. **Where is the line between interesting chaos and mere noise?** What determines the λ sweet spot?

5. **Can art exist without intention?** If neither artist nor algorithm intended beauty, is the result art?

## The Map's Tension

This map presents randomness as creative tool—Pollock, pipes, butterflies, chaos. It's celebratory: look what randomness can do!

But it doesn't ask:
- Who profits from generative art (algorithms don't collect royalties; programmers might)
- What is lost when we simulate rather than preserve
- Whether algorithmic aesthetics reproduces cultural biases (why Pollock and not other traditions?)
- What happens to human artists when algorithms generate infinitely

The critical reading acknowledges the genuine wonder of these demonstrations while noting their context: a tech industry that valorizes generation over preservation, efficiency over craft, automation over labor.

For QFEP: this gallery demonstrates the aesthetic range of the λ parameter. Too much order (λ → ∞) is boring; too much chaos (λ → 0) is noise. The sweet spot—interesting, engaging, beautiful—exists in a range. But this range is not fixed; it's cultural, historical, learned. The QFEP's oscillation might also oscillate in what counts as optimal λ.

## Theoretical Framework

### Pollock and Action Painting

Jackson Pollock's drip paintings (1947-1950) used random gesture: paint flung, dripped, poured onto horizontal canvases. The artist controlled the overall composition but surrendered detail to chance.

Recent analysis (Taylor et al.) found Pollock's drips exhibit fractal structure—self-similar patterns across scales. The randomness isn't pure chaos; it has statistical properties that our brains find aesthetically pleasing.

```gdscript
# Pollock-style drip simulation
func drip_paint(canvas_size: Vector2, density: int):
    for i in range(density):
        var start = random_canvas_position(canvas_size)
        var velocity = random_velocity()
        simulate_drip(start, velocity)

func simulate_drip(start: Vector3, velocity: Vector3):
    var pos = start
    while pos.y > 0:
        draw_point(pos)
        velocity += gravity * delta
        velocity += random_perturbation()  # Air resistance randomness
        pos += velocity * delta
```

### Screensaver Pipes

The Windows 3D Pipes screensaver (1994) became iconic: random 3D pipes growing through space, making turns at right angles, occasionally sprouting joints. It demonstrated that simple random rules could create surprisingly engaging visuals.

### Butterfly Flight

Butterfly flight patterns combine determinism (wing mechanics) with randomness (air currents, obstacle avoidance, search behavior). Simulating this requires:
- Basic flight dynamics
- Random perturbation
- Occasional direction changes
- Flocking tendencies (some species)

### Extreme Randomness

What happens when randomness is maximized? Pure noise—white static, meaningless variation. The "extrem_randomness" exhibit likely pushes parameters to limits, showing where coherent pattern dissolves into chaos.

## QFEP Connection

This gallery demonstrates the λ parameter across its range:

- **Pollock**: Medium λ—controlled chaos, structure in randomness
- **Pipes**: Low randomness within rules—each segment deterministic, only direction random
- **Butterflies**: Moderate λ—natural randomness in flight
- **Extreme**: Maximum E(S)—entropy overwhelming structure
- **Moss**: Biological λ—constrained growth with random variation

The gallery is a catalog of entropy configurations—different balances of order and chaos, all producing recognizable patterns (except at the extreme, where recognition breaks down).

A gallery of creative chaos — Pollock's drip paintings, pipe dreams, butterflies in flight, extreme randomness pushed to visual limits. Art where the algorithm becomes the artist.

`pollock_painting_in_3d` translates action painting into 3D particle physics: random launch vectors, gravity, paint-splatter collision. `monte_carlo_dartboard` demonstrates randomness as computational method — throw darts at a unit square, count hits inside the inscribed circle, and pi emerges from the ratio. `pipe_dream` runs combinatorial random pipe generation. `extreme_randomness` pushes past Gaussian into heavy-tailed Cauchy and Levy distributions where outliers dominate. Randomness is not one phenomenon. Pollock's drip was deliberate embrace of controlled chaos. Monte Carlo weaponized randomness for computation. Butterflies' Levy flights are optimal foraging. The gallery argues: randomness is a cultural phenomenon, not merely a mathematical one.
