# Rotation: room for a turn

The transport cube carried you without needing to turn. Here, a blade stays attached to its middle while its ends sweep across the gap. Follow one end. The other moves away.

<!-- @rotation_wall_crossings -->

## Staying upright

The green blade turns around the vertical y axis. At ninety degrees it pauses, its length reaching across your route. Walk over while the crossing holds.

At the orange blade, one end descends as the other rises. It turns around x, like a paddle wheel. The far landing is three grid cubes higher. During the pause, the blade offers a slope you can climb. Through vertical, your feet lose their agreement with it.

Beside its lower end is a small level platform. Board it and stay through the turn. You rise, move forward, and arrive beside the higher grid. Your head has stayed upright.

The platform follows an arc while keeping its surface level. It is mounted to one side so the blade can pass through vertical without sweeping through your body. Keeping you upright requires a rule alongside the turn.

The orange ride's arc, before that sideways offset, is calculated as:

```gdscript
var radial := Vector3(0, -1.5, -3.5)
var orbit := Basis(Vector3.RIGHT, PI * progress) * radial
var endpoint := Vector3(0, 1.5, 0) + orbit
```

`radial` reaches from the centre to the starting point. `Basis` turns that reach around x; `progress` from zero to one supplies half a revolution. Adding the centre gives the new position. The platform carries you until you step off or jump.

The blue blade turns around z. Board from the left: this ride carries you sideways along x and up. Turning around z cannot advance you along z. The destination is another bank.

Both rides pause for twelve seconds, then take twelve for the half-turn. Staying aboard brings you back along the upper arc. The grid wedges and still aisles offer other returns.

Beyond the blades, the cubes hold their angles. You bring the movement.

## An angle underfoot

Four bands each contain two columns and six rows of cubes. Begin at zero and walk forward along z.

Eighteen degrees. Thirty-six. Fifty-four.

The centres keep their spacing; each row adds an angle. X makes rises and falls across your route. Z makes ridges along it. Y turns the square footprints while keeping their tops horizontal. The fourth band combines x, then y, then z.

```gdscript
var degrees := float(cube.get_meta("row")) * row_angle_step
cube.basis = rotation_for_band(band, degrees)
```

The row supplies the angle; the band supplies the axis or combination. Waiting will not change the slope. The still aisles let you inspect a difficult row from beside it.

A stand in the central aisle starts another walk. Cyan capsules, 0.44 metres wide, set off along all four bands. Pink capsules follow from the same places, now 1.20 metres wide. Their height, speed, gravity and forward instruction stay the same. Rings retain their endpoints; the trial repeats, and **REPLAY** starts it again.

Watch the z band. The narrow body catches on a ridge; the wider one can slide sideways and continue. Wider need not mean less room to move. These bodies neither jump nor choose a detour: the collision solver supplies their slides. You can walk through them and try a route they did not take.

Each cube has kept its shape throughout. What changes is how it meets its neighbours and your body.

The operation on the right acts first. In Translation, upward and sideways movements could exchange order and share a destination. These two turns cannot: the second receives the orientation left by the first.

## Blade crossings

Source: `commons/artifacts/rotation_studies/wall_crossings.gd`.

All three panels have their local centre at `Vector3.ZERO` under a pivot. Each measures 8.4 by 2.4 by 0.2 metres. Mesh and physical box share the pivot.

| Station | Axis and signed crossing angle | Source-grid route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Y | +y, +90 degrees | Ground to ground along z |
| X | +x, -23.19859 degrees | Ground to +3 m along z |
| Z | +z, +23.19859 degrees | Ground to +3 m along x |

The gap between the bank faces is 7 m and the rise is 3 m. The climb angle is `atan(3/7)`. The X/Z axle height is `3/2 - 0.2/(2*cos(angle))`, approximately 1.3912 m. This accounts for panel thickness: the upper plane meets y=0 and y=3 at the two bank faces. The longer blade overlaps the banks.

```gdscript
station.pivot.basis = Basis(station.axis,deg_to_rad(degrees*float(station.sign)))
```

Each pivot sits at the panel's middle, unlike the previous bottom-hinged prototype. The source map supplies all static landing geometry; the artifact builds no bank slabs. Grid height 1 has its top at world y=0, height 4 at y=3. A four-metre basin gives the larger paddle sweep room below the deck.

The running score holds each crossing pose for twelve seconds and advances a half-turn at fifteen degrees per second, also twelve seconds. Source: `commons/artifacts/rotation_studies/array_compare.gd`.

The hall sets `row_angle_step` to eighteen degrees: rows 0 through 5 receive 0, 18, 36, 54, 72 and 90. The default outside this hall remains ten degrees. # A direction for a body

The blade rotates around its middle. Neither bank owns the hinge. As one end rises the other descends; the same operation changes the relation with both landings. A higher grid surface can become reachable without moving the centre of the rotating object. This is an argument made by a particular length, axle height and pair of banks, not a property bestowed by the word rotation.

Beyond the landing, the angle is distributed through an array. The row supplies the amount, the band supplies the axis, and the visitor supplies a direction of travel. The x ridges cut across forward movement; the z ridges extend along it. Y changes the horizontal footprint. These distinctions can be inspected in matching cube centres, visible edges and physical surfaces.

The narrow and wide capsules receive the same forward instruction, without jumping or choosing a detour. Their stopping points belong to their dimensions and movement rules. In the z band, the wider body can slide sideways and continue where the narrow body stops: its arrival does not erase the change of route. Neither trial proves the array impassable for every body. A different approach, floor-angle setting or jump may find a remainder these tests omitted. The still aisles make it possible to inspect the difficult row without completing its route.

Rigid rotation preserves the topology of each solid. Traversal depends on how solids meet each other and a body. Maintaining that distinction lets the installation test a claim about connectivity without claiming to have changed the topology of a cube.

Increasing a parameter is not a promise of increasing difficulty forever. This score continues to ninety degrees, where a cube's geometry can repeat an axis-aligned orientation. The range selected for the experiment makes the progression legible, and also leaves something outside it. Keep the range visible beside the array.

The first court gives a twelve-second pause for a climb or boarding a level end-platform. The ride exposes an additional rule: the platform is kept upright while its position follows the rotating blade. Without that rule a standing body loses support as the blade becomes vertical. X offers forward displacement, while Z demands a sideways destination. The second holds the geometry still. These are two different distributions of time and attention. Neither should be accepted as adequate for a group until people have walked the revised hall.


The last court keeps a floor under your feet while the tunnel's frames turn around the passage. Beside it, the carousels distribute different rates through equal layer heights. The same code can be read as a constraint to cross, a rhythm to follow or an enclosure to inhabit. These possibilities need their specific operations named: subtraction makes a hole; a turn orients it; repetition supplies neighbours; time lets their relations move.
