# The line that decides

<!-- @perlin_terrain_sculptor -->

A green body waits on the bench, with little daylight between its blocks. A translucent yellow box marks one cell, whether or not that cell holds a block. Deep inside the body, the blocks around it can hide the box until CUT opens the near half. Across the room, a much larger body has the same irregular outline, facing the same way.

Leave SEED alone for a moment. Press THRESHOLD and watch the green body. Then watch the field value on the plate while you press again. Something has disappeared. Which number changed?

The field value stays. The line it must exceed has moved.

```gdscript
static func contract_occupied(value: float, v: float, threshold: float) -> bool:
	return value - contract_bias(v) > threshold
```

The whole decision fits inside that return. A field value arrives; a height-dependent bias is subtracted; what remains must be greater than the threshold. Equality goes with empty. The result has two states, although the values arriving at it can differ by tiny amounts.

There are twenty-four addresses along each axis: 13,824 occasions to ask. A voxel is a volume element, a little region whose occupancy receives this answer. We have carried the array into three dimensions. The field supplies values at its sample positions; the grid supplies the places where those values can count.

Look at the bias on the plate. Lower cells receive help. Higher cells have more subtracted. That preference is written here:

```gdscript
static func contract_bias(v: float) -> float:
	return (v - 0.5) * CONTRACT_BIAS_SCALE
```

`v` runs from zero at the bottom to one at the top, and the scale here is 0.5. The bias runs from −0.25 to +0.25. We have given this field a downstairs before placing anything on it. No gravity has acted on these blocks. Even a fragment hanging in the air stays where its address puts it.

The threshold button visits five values and then returns to the first. Within the rising run, −0.20 through +0.20, occupied cells can only be removed while the field and bias stay fixed. Watch a thin connection; watch a tiny island. They do not necessarily disappear at the same step.

The counter for separate pieces has another rhythm. As the threshold rises, a thin connection can break and leave two pieces. A small island can vanish entirely. Less occupied space can mean more pieces, or fewer.[^voxel-pieces] Counting bodies asks a different question from counting how much is occupied.

Press CUT. Part of the model opens to your gaze. Now check the occupied count. It has stayed put. The cells concealed by CUT are still in the array; this button changes which instances are drawn. Bring them back. A disappearance has acquired two possible explanations within reach of the same hand.

<!-- @science_screen -->

The screen keeps a horizontal slice of that array. On the left, colours vary with the field after the height bias. On the right, each square receives the decision: occupied or empty. A small yellow frame marks the selected coordinate in both.

Press CELL at the bench. The box visits another address and the screen takes its horizontal layer. Follow a dark square on the right into the coloured square at the same position on the left. There is still a value there. The decision has withheld a block.

A thin yellow square runs around the large volume at the height of that slice. Press CELL again. The square changes level high above you. Later, inside the glass flight box, you can rise until this reading meets your eyes. The blocks stay put. We have moved the place from which we read them. A smaller yellow outline keeps the selected address inside the volume, though solid neighbours can hide it. An empty cell still has a location. The decision has withheld a block, not erased its address.

![Flying above the one-metre voxel cells. The yellow frame locates the selected horizontal slice; the glass enclosure lends flight while occupied blocks remain solid.](/book-review/doc/book/figures/Noise_Voxel/flight-from-above.png)

Return to THRESHOLD. The right-hand border shifts through a left-hand field that stays still. You can now see several different values receive the same answer. The binary mask cannot show how far a rejected value missed the line; the other display keeps that difference as a shade, darker the further below, though it prints no scale.

The screen reads the bench's stored decision and sets it beside the value from which that decision was made. One view can show a difference that the other has put on the same side of the line.[^voxel-screen]

There are two readings beside each other because each lets us ask something the other has lost. The coloured field has already passed through another rule, a colour mapping. We have no unencoded original glowing behind the screen. We have enough distinct readings to follow one transformation and put pressure on its account.

Now try SEED. The field changes, and the decision map changes with it. The coordinate remains an address, but what arrives there is different. The number on the bench lets you notice that the comparison has begun again.

<!-- @voxelnoise -->

Walk towards the large blue volume with its pink edges. Each cube is now one metre across. Twenty-four cells can rise twenty-four metres above the floor. At the bench a recess was a detail; here you can approach it with your whole body.

Both works ask at the same 24 × 24 × 24 positions in the same Perlin field. Their cell spacing differs: four centimetres at the bench, one metre here: twenty-five times the spacing. The model leaves a small gap around each visible block so its addresses remain legible. The large volume joins occupied neighbours and draws their exposed faces. Its surface also supplies the collision triangles.

The coordinate rule is small enough to carry across the room:

```gdscript
return n.get_noise_3d(u * CONTRACT_SPAN, v * CONTRACT_SPAN, w * CONTRACT_SPAN)
```

The three coordinates run from zero to one within either display. `CONTRACT_SPAN` is 9.6. Increasing the physical spacing therefore enlarges the arrangement without reaching into a different part of the field. The same decisions have gained another relation to your height, reach and route.

Step through a ground-level opening in the glass. Your feet no longer have to ask for a floor. Rise along the edge, then approach a gap. Release the movement and hang there. The blue blocks still stop you; an opening has to admit your body before it can become a route. The glass lends this movement a boundary. Descend to either doorway and leave: the museum asks for your weight again.

The plate at the bench counts groups joined through cell faces. Stand beside the volume and ask what that count cannot tell you. It does not measure headroom. It does not know your step height, or whether a passage has an entrance. One connected mass can contain a route you cannot use. Empty space can offer passage, or leave you without support.

There is pleasure in a cavity before its usefulness is settled. Try looking from another side. The blocks need not become an enemy, a resource or a successful level to hold your attention. They can make you curious about what sort of body would find a way here. Our own body gives one answer, with particular dimensions and particular permissions.

<!-- @dark_sphere -->

The dark orb is still here, turning above its faint violet shadow. Leave the threshold at one value and watch it for a while. The voxel body holds its arrangement. The orb continues to brighten and dim.

```gdscript
var pulse_t := (sin(_time_elapsed * pulse_speed) + 1.0) * 0.5
_sphere_material.emission_energy_multiplier = lerpf(pulse_min * _emit_mul, pulse_max * _emit_mul, pulse_t)
```

We have met this sine before. It passes through a range of emission strengths while the sphere keeps its form. Nothing here asks whether the sine has earned the right to keep a block.

The same museum can host a continuously dressed body and a body assembled through yes-or-no decisions. The threshold is useful; it gives the volume a boundary you can encounter. The field's other differences remain available to another operation, another material, another question.

# Read a decision before changing its source

The room builds on Noise_One: one scalar can become displacement or colour. Here it becomes a stored occupancy decision. Four primary works carry different parts of that argument.

1. At **perlin_terrain_sculptor**, keep the initial seed. Press THRESHOLD and compare the field value at the yellow cell with the changing line. Read `contract_occupied` in the book only after noticing which quantity stayed fixed. The button wraps from +0.20 to −0.20.
2. Compare occupied cells with separate pieces. The first count decreases through ascending thresholds; the second need not. A component may split or disappear. This is six-face adjacency, not a route test.
3. Try CUT and inspect the unchanged occupied count. It changes the visible MultiMesh instances. Restore the view, then use CELL to choose another sample.
4. At **science_screen**, follow the yellow coordinate between the score slice and the binary mask. Change threshold at the bench. Inspect a value whose answer changes. Change CELL to view another horizontal layer. SEED then changes the field itself.
5. Approach **voxelnoise** through a ground door in its glass flight enclosure. Find a form shared with the model. Both have 24³ decisions; the physical spacing grows from 0.04 to 1.00 m. Rise in the clear margin, release movement to hover, and approach an opening. Distinguish the occupied set from what a body of your size can traverse. Desktop: look + WASD, Space up, Ctrl down. VR: tilt the left controller and use its stick. Descend and leave through either ground doorway to resume walking.
6. Find **dark_sphere**. Its emission and rotation continue independently. Compare a value used as a varying material strength with a value used in an occupancy test.

For a group, one person controls the bench while others compare the screen and volume. Swap positions. Alone, keep the seed fixed while returning between the three views. The question to carry into Noise_6_Wall is what another receiver can make of a value the threshold rejected.

# Noise Voxel: from a field to an occupied set

This account describes the four authored works in the current museum placement. The previous account and scene/map sources are preserved under `doc/space/voxel-review-2026-09-13/before/`. The original lattice, threshold, height-bias, MultiMesh, SurfaceTool and connectivity arguments remain; the sampler, dimensions, controls and collision claims below follow the implemented hall.

## One sampled field

`perlin_terrain_sculptor#stand:lattice#seed:31415` is the source. The map supplies a reproducible initial seed. It constructs FastNoiseLite with TYPE_PERLIN, FRACTAL_FBM, three octaves and frequency `noise_scale * 0.1`, initially `4.0 * 0.1 = 0.4`. Both model and receiver use the same helper:

```gdscript
static func contract_noise(seed_value: int, scale: float, octaves: int) -> FastNoiseLite:
	var n := FastNoiseLite.new()
	n.noise_type = FastNoiseLite.TYPE_PERLIN
	n.fractal_type = FastNoiseLite.FRACTAL_FBM
	n.frequency = scale * 0.1
	n.fractal_octaves = octaves
	n.seed = seed_value
	return n
```

Each axis has 24 samples. An integer address becomes `index / 23.0`, then the helper multiplies by CONTRACT_SPAN = 9.6. These are field coordinates, distinct from the physical centres of the displayed cubes. The endpoints sample 0 and 9.6; the frequency configured on FastNoiseLite then affects its evaluation. The field is continuous in its mathematical construction; the implementation computes finite-precision values at selected positions.

In volume mode the receiver is also 24³, so every index denotes the same sample in both works. The earlier 24³/32³ pairing covered the same coordinate domain but sampled different intermediate positions. Matching domains alone cannot establish matching cells.

The receiver formerly accepted the Perlin contract and then reset its sampler to Simplex in `_configure_noise()` during rebuilding. That function now preserves the contract. Legacy receivers without the contract keep their original Simplex path. Shared seed alone was insufficient: algorithm, coordinates, frequency, octave settings and decision rule must also agree.

## Classification and vertical preference

Let `v = y / 23`. Bias is `(v - 0.5) * 0.5`. The decision is strictly `field - bias > threshold`. At equality the answer is empty. The bottom bias is −0.25, helping occupancy; the top bias is +0.25, hindering it. This subtraction introduces a vertical preference. It does not simulate gravity, falling fragments or a physical soil process.

At a fixed seed, sampling and bias, increasing the threshold produces a subset of the previous occupied set. The five button positions are −0.20, −0.10, 0.00, +0.10, +0.20, followed by a wrap to −0.20. The wrap increases occupancy and must not be mistaken for a counterexample to monotonicity. The initial position is 0.00.

The recorded seed-31415 comparison is:

| Threshold | Occupied cells | Face-connected pieces | Largest piece |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| −0.20 | 11,040 | 4 | 11,028 |
| −0.10 | 9,176 | 15 | 9,085 |
| 0.00 | 6,767 | 25 | 6,661 |
| +0.10 | 4,582 | 21 | 4,498 |
| +0.20 | 2,685 | 32 | 2,577 |

These are measured outcomes of this configuration, not counts promised for a new seed. Occupancy falls monotonically. The component count can rise when a connection is removed and fall when an entire component disappears. The flood fill treats six face neighbours as adjacent; edge and corner contact alone do not join two components. It counts connected components of the occupied cells, not cavities, traversable routes or a general entropy measure.

## Four controls with different scopes

THRESHOLD changes classification and broadcasts the source settings to the hall's volume. CELL cycles five selected coordinates and changes the screen's horizontal layer, the large volume's yellow section perimeter and its selected-cell outline. This selection update is scoped to the same hall and does not rebuild either body. The receiver's link opt-out also disables selection updates. SEED generates a new named five-digit seed and rebuilds the field. CUT hides cells with `z > grid_size / 2` in the model's MultiMesh while retaining `_voxels`, all occupancy counts, the receiver and the screen section. With 24 indices this keeps 0–12 visible; the cut is not exactly half the index range.

The lattice mode now omits the legacy slider case and floating labels in favour of its four-button panel and cased readout. The legacy standalone sculptor still offers its own controls. The lattice panel is enlarged and tilted; the model stands on a low solid plinth with a clear northern approach. The source now occupies map cell (13,7), avoiding the retained raised platform near its former controls.

The source discovers its museum ancestor and broadcasts only to receiver descendants of that hall. A receiver in another streamed hall is outside the update. The screen similarly finds its own hall's lattice source; if none exists it returns an empty data record and displays source-unavailable information, not an invented substitute field.

## Model, screen and full-size volume

The model allocates a MultiMesh for up to 13,824 instances and draws only occupied, currently visible cells. Its centres are `(index - 11.5) * 0.04`. Each BoxMesh is 95% of that spacing: 0.038 m. The little visual gaps expose the lattice; they do not change the component-count adjacency rule. The selected marker now uses the same cell centre. The bench model is a visual instrument, not an individually collidable cube world.

The science screen explicitly uses `mode:voxel_slice`. It shows a 24 × 24 horizontal layer at the selected y. It recalculates the field values from the source sampler and reads the actual stored `_voxels[x][y][z]` booleans. The left heatmap maps the biased scalar through a fixed colour range; the right map displays occupancy. Two yellow outlines identify the same selected x,z address. The screen is a 2D rendering carried by a 3D stand. Its cache includes source identity, seed, frequency, octaves, threshold, cell and grid size. CUT does not change its data.

`voxelnoise:180#stand:volume#cell_size:1#flight_box:true` faces the same way as the bench and uses 1.00 m spacing and cubes which meet at their faces. The stage occupies 24.4 × 24.4 m around a 24 m cube domain. A 28 m glass enclosure leaves a two-metre margin on each horizontal side and four metres above the domain. The occupied mask is computed first; a second pass emits only faces with an empty or out-of-bounds neighbour. Each exposed square contributes two triangles. Shared interior faces need no rendering or collision. This preserves the occupied set while reducing mesh construction cost. The boundary includes cavity surfaces, not just the outside silhouette.

The same triangle array constructs the ConcavePolygonShape3D. Rebuilding removes the preceding mesh and collider before adding their replacement. A fully empty result creates neither. A matching collider establishes a physical boundary, but does not certify that a player can climb through the volume. The chamber's surrounding museum route remains available independently of the volume's changing occupancy. The 33 × 44 m source hall has a clear flight court and open roof. The old interior structure and utilities shift nine cells in x; side/end boundaries expand. Both raised cells and old openings are retained. The four artifacts remain; the model and screen move with that x offset, the volume moves to (16,24), and the orb to (5,8).

## What survives a representation change

The model and volume have the same scalar samples and occupancy at different physical spacings. Their finishes, block margins, render methods and collision behaviour differ. The screen exposes a selected section; it cannot show the whole 3D connectivity at once. The dark sphere retains its independent sine-driven emission, rotation and halo. It does not receive the voxel controls.

Sampling resolution, display scale and threshold are distinct. More samples cost more evaluations: doubling all three dimensions multiplies the cell count by eight. More geometry does not by itself provide better access, clearer reading or a more desirable body. Here the deliberately modest lattice allows the reader to inspect a decision and its consequences without making every address a separate Godot node.

## Verification and limits

The accepted rendered museum run passed 61 checks: all 13,824 scalar values and occupancy decisions agree at each of five thresholds; the screen's 576 decisions agree with the stored layer; boundary face counts and collision triangles agree; physical rays hit sampled roofs; empty-volume cleanup and CUT separation pass; all four buttons respond through the project's desktop interaction pointer. Broadcasts exclude an unrelated receiver. The museum reports no severed route for this hall.

This uses synthetic mouse events through the real desktop pointer rig. It is not a human walkthrough or headset test. Headset reach, comfort, step traversal and Quest frame cost remain pending. Mesh rebuilding is still synchronous and a threshold change can produce a hitch; the desktop timing record is not a headset performance guarantee.

## Shared section in the room — 25 September

The volume's section perimeter is 24.1 m wide around the 24 m field domain. Its centre height is `(selected_y + 0.5) * 1.00`; the authored five-cell cycle visits 12.5, 8.5, 6.5, 17.5 and 12.5 m. The small wire box follows the same x,y,z address, including empty cells. Both markers are depth-tested, unshaded observations with no collider; solid neighbours can occlude the address marker. Sixteen BoxMesh bars are reused as the selection moves. No field, threshold, occupancy, boundary generation or collision rule changes. The frame's fixed label is readable from either approach.

The preceding 0.20 m staging check injected press/release PointerEvent objects into the actual buttons; it does not test ray aiming or hand tracking. It compares all 13,824 field samples and decisions at five thresholds, checks unchanged mesh identity while selecting cells, matching mesh/collision triangles, CUT isolation, receiver opt-out and separation from another hall. Controlled walking paths go around the bench corner; a failed direct diagonal is retained in the review. That earlier check and photographs remain at `doc/book/iterations/2026-09-25-voxel-correspondence/`; the one-metre flight revision is at `doc/book/iterations/2026-09-25-voxel-flight/`. ## One-metre cells and local flight

The `cell_size` placement setting defaults to 0.20 m for existing volume placements; this hall explicitly selects 1.0. The number of addresses, normalized sampler, seed, threshold and occupied set are unchanged. Boundary vertices and collision now fall on the metre grid. Stage, captions and yellow section markers derive their size from the spacing. The miniature remains at 0.04 m.

The opt-in `flight_box` uses the existing museum_flight_box.gd and XR movement adapter. Its new `edge_length` setting is assigned before it enters the tree. Existing Menger and Array boxes retain their 12 m default, controls and speed. This hall uses 28 m; door openings retain their human-scale 3 m width and 2.6 m height. Flight is selected from the body's position, ends outside the envelope and does not remove voxel collision. Neutral input hovers; combined velocity is bounded at 2.4 m/s. The threshold and CELL buttons do not replace the flight enclosure.

The glass floor, sides and roof are physical, with two ground-level doors. No room-wide free-flight toggle persists after departure. Desktop and synthetic XR checks exercise the real museum movement/provider; headset comfort, tracking, reach and Quest performance remain unverified.

# What does the threshold allow to have a body?

At a dark square on the screen, the field still has a value. The square is empty because a particular comparison assigned it that state. This is a precise place to begin the critical inquiry: a relation between data and a rule, visible beside the outcome it produces.

The rule is useful. It gives us a discrete volume, a surface we can draw and collision triangles a player can encounter. Its loss of scalar differences makes a workable world. Asking what it excludes need not end in demanding infinite resolution or abolishing every distinction. It can lead to another use for what the current representation cannot carry.

The three instruments allow this question to stay specific. THRESHOLD changes the occupied set. CUT leaves that set intact and changes its visibility in the model. CELL changes which slice the screen reads. SEED changes the field supplied to the comparison. A missing block, a hidden instance and a sample outside the selected layer can look alike from one position. Their causes, and the available interventions, differ.

The initial field already contains decisions. Perlin basis, fractal octaves, seed, sample spacing and a vertical bias all precede the threshold. The bias gives lower addresses a numerical advantage without invoking gravity. Exposing this preference lets a visitor ask who gave the volume its downstairs. The answer here is a line of source, not nature speaking through the scene.

The full-size body introduces a further relation. Twenty-five times the model's cell spacing makes a cavity a different proposition for the visitor. The occupied component counter remains indifferent to headroom, shoulder width, a safe landing or a reachable ledge. A topology of face-connected cells is useful but insufficient to describe these bodily possibilities. Nor is the number of pieces an entropy score: the observed fall from 25 pieces to 21 at a higher threshold is already enough to trouble a single story of increasing fragmentation.

The queer opening I want to keep is the possibility of remaining interested in the body before assigning it an authorised use. A recess can attract attention without becoming cover from an enemy. A hanging fragment can be desirable without becoming loot. A topology can solicit a body we do not yet possess. The room gives this desire a material question: which dimensions, controls and collision rules would that encounter require?

This does not make a threshold an adequate model of a person's gender, social belonging or political exclusion. The analogy becomes useful where it helps us locate a rule, an effect and an available change. It becomes reductive if people are treated as scalar values waiting for a more generous cutoff. Here we can compare the classification with other operations and ask what each account cannot hold.

The orb retains one such difference. Its sine varies emission strength while its mesh remains. It belongs in this hall precisely as an independently running work with an already learned operation. It lets the sequence accumulate: variation can colour, displace, illuminate or help decide occupancy. Adding the threshold has enlarged the toolbox, not replaced everything that came before.

## Experiments to return to

These are proposals, not installed controls. Use the rejected scalar as emission on the occupied boundary; reverse the height bias while retaining the same seed; compare face-only adjacency with edge/corner adjacency; put differently sized player capsules beside the same cavity. Each comparison should change one named relation and preserve enough of the scene for the consequences to be legible. None needs to declare one outcome the most free.

The earlier essay and its theoretical associations remain in the dated source snapshot. This version grounds the inquiry in the four works actually present; it does not attribute the hall's threshold mechanism to an author whose argument the installation has not established.

The flight enclosure alters another condition: a route no longer needs continuous support below the feet. It still needs clearance around the body. The visitor can rise to a previously distant opening without changing the field that left it empty. The glass bounds that permission; outside its doors the museum returns the body to walking.
