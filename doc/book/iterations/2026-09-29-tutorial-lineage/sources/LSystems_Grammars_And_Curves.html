# When forward goes sideways

<!-- @the_believing_turtle -->

Does an instruction say how it must be obeyed?

Three short lines rise from three origins. Each has read the first F of the same sentence, taking a stride of 0.32 metres. We have kept the travelling state from Growth, but the question has moved: what agreement connects an instruction to a movement?

Press READ F. The blue line goes sideways. Its gold arrow still points up; its white arrow points across. The gold arrow shows the stored heading, the white one the direction the reader will draw. Those two directions no longer agree.

The sentence has not changed. Neither has the stride. This reader takes the direction it was given and exchanges its x and y components. What used to contribute to height now contributes to horizontal distance. Before seeing a whole tree, we can catch the disagreement in a single step.

Now press ANGLE. The middle reader changes from twenty-five to forty-five degrees. Its first line stays upright. Why did this change produce no visible turn? Keep pressing STEP until the sixth symbol. The first two Fs extend the stem; a plus turns the heading; a bracket saves it; another plus turns again; the next F draws. The middle reading has departed from the green reference. An angle matters when an instruction uses it.

All three readers follow:

```text
FF+[+F-F-F]-[-F+F+F]
```

There are twenty symbols and eight drawing instructions. Finish the sentence, finding corresponding forks as you go. When the brackets close, the saved positions and headings return, as they did in Growth. Each drawing has read the same bracket at the same moment. Yet one spreads differently, and another seems to have forgotten which way a tree should stand.

Here is the direction calculation in the believing turtle:

```gdscript
var dir := Vector2(cos(heading), sin(heading))
if sideways:
    dir = Vector2(dir.y, dir.x)
```

The angle called heading supplies the two components of a unit direction. The conditional exchanges them. The next line uses that direction to take a step in the museum's drawing plane:

```gdscript
var nxt := pos + Vector3(dir.x, dir.y, 0.0) * step
```

The third component is zero: these drawings remain planar inside a three-dimensional room. The step multiplies the direction; adding it to the current position gives the next position. This is the vector operation we brought through the earlier halls, now given another reading.

Try to match every blue fork to a green one. The correspondence is unusually dependable. With the same angle, exchanging x and y at every step reflects the entire construction across the diagonal x = y, relative to its origin. It preserves lengths, angles between segments and which branches connect. A reflection can overturn our expectation of uprightness while retaining this much structure. It is not simply a ninety-degree rotation; follow an off-axis branch to see the difference.

![The green reading and the blue READ F reading of the same sentence at TREE, drawn from the rule, with the diagonal x = y through their shared origin](/book-review/doc/book/figures/lsystems/believing-turtle-reflection.png)

*Both readings drawn from the rule at generation two: 172 symbols, 64 segments each, 25 degrees, a 0.32-metre stride. The blue one is the green one reflected across the dashed diagonal; every fork has its counterpart.*

Press READ F again. The blue reading returns to the green one's arrangement. The exchange is reversible. Its strangeness has a precise operation we can repeat, inspect and undo.

TREE gives all three readers two complete rewriting generations: 172 symbols, 64 drawn segments. The same local differences accumulate across a larger body. No drawing has been resized to fit its case. Find the first fork again before trying to recognise the whole outline. More detail can obscure a relation we understood one step earlier.

STEP reveals recorded interpreter states, not a calculation suspended between button presses. After the last symbol it returns to the beginning of the reading; RESET also restores the initial angles and forward convention. The original lectern and its smaller studies remain behind this desk, including their unequal step lengths and finite drawing prefixes. They offer another comparison, with different conditions.

Calling this reader a believing turtle gives us a way to attend to its commitments. It does not give the turtle an inner conviction. The commitment is executable: F is assigned a direction through this code. We can change that assignment. We can also ask what the change has left untouched. The branches still have no collision surfaces; a sideways drawing has not yet become somewhere we can stand. That further desire needs another operation.

1. Begin with all three readers at 25 degrees, forward, after one 0.32 m F. READ F exchanges x/y only in the blue reader. Compare its stored heading (gold) with its drawing direction (white).
2. ANGLE changes only the middle reader to 45 degrees. The first F does not move. STEP five times to symbol six, where the first branch segment makes the changed turns visible.

## Continue a drawing that has already begun

This is the later **sweep** lesson, in **L-systems → Grammars and Curves**, before Architecture. Point Trace introduced keeping positions. Mesh introduced vertices and triangle connections; refinement introduced circular sampling; rotation supplied changing orientation. Here those tools meet: a path carries a ring, and neighbouring rings carry faces. Point Trace keeps its earlier, simpler encounter.

### First use what is here

Three supplied strokes are suspended in front of the wall. Their six pink end grips are part of the interface. Grab a lower end and move your hand slowly. The old length stays where it was; sampled positions are added beyond it. Release to leave the addition in the air. You can also continue from the other end; the stored path reverses its order before growing there.

Bring an end close to the receiving wall. Its centre snaps to the wall surface and sampling stops until you release. This makes a visible attachment. It is not a structural joint or a gravity simulation. The runs can now join through explicit sockets. Their skins can overlap and do not become a welded solid or block the visitor. Together their paths can compose another, incomplete wall in front of the room's wall.

The pen on the plinth begins a separate stroke. Pick it up, hold its trigger while moving, and release the trigger to keep the line. On desktop, the existing right-click pickup/drop and left-button held action drive the same pen. The artifact adds no player, controller mapping or installation step.

**UNDO** restores the whole drawing before the last completed gesture or geometry edit. Extending a supplied line and undoing leaves the supplied line intact. **CLEAR** removes the strokes; **EXAMPLE** replaces them with the three opening strokes using the current radius and side count. Both can be undone. **RESET** also restores default settings and returns the pen; put it down first. Undo restores geometry, not the pen pose or the next-stroke settings. History keeps the last sixteen states during this visit. Closing or unloading the hall does not save a drawing to disk.

### Inspect the same work three times

**VIEW** cycles TUBE → PATH → RINGS → TUBE. It changes the representation, not the stored positions. PATH is the retained centreline. RINGS shows the sampled cross-sections. TUBE connects them with faces and closes the two ends. The controls are available after you finish a gesture.

**THICKNESS**, **SIDES** and **COLOUR** edit the last stroke in the list and choose the setting for the next new pen stroke. Continuing an earlier stroke preserves that stroke's own material and resolution. Undo an extension before trying a different route. The readout counts the last stroke in the list, not the sum of the whole work.

### What one ring needs

The production builder is `commons/primitives/pipes/swept_tube.gd`. Its `build(path, radius, sides)` function receives positions we already know how to retain. At each position it supplies two perpendicular directions, `frame_normal` and `binormal`, across the path. This is the ring expression, taken from that builder:

```gdscript
var angle := TAU * j / float(sides)
var radial := frame_normal*cos(angle)+binormal*sin(angle)
vertices.append(points[i]+radius*radial)
```

`i` addresses a point along the path; `j` addresses a place around its ring. Changing `sides` changes the second count. It does not take more samples of your hand movement. Changing `radius` moves each ring vertex away from its centre without moving the centre.

The supplied frame transport carries the ring around bends without making it suddenly look towards world-up. Understanding or rewriting quaternion transport is not required for this first edit. First locate the familiar circular rule inside the supplied construction.

### Join two rings

Let `a` and `b` be neighbouring vertices on one ring, and `c` and `d` their corresponding neighbours on the next. This production line adds two triangles:

```gdscript
indices.append_array(PackedInt32Array([a,c,b,b,c,d]))
```

The last address wraps to the first with `% sides`. That closes the seam around a ring. Separate triangles cap the two ends. For N path points and S sides, the body has `2 × (N - 1) × S` triangles and the two caps add `2 × S`. The total is `2 × N × S`.

Make the one source edit above before changing the path: twelve sides become eight. Each opening stroke retains its 41 centres and 0.065 m radius. The capped mesh changes from 984 to 656 triangles. Look near a curved silhouette. Then make your own continuation and compare again. The number is a check on a visible decision, not the reason to prefer one drawing.

### After the first sweep: a connected system

Choose **NETWORK** to load the three-form example. **FORM** cycles the last run and next pen stroke through tube, pipe and cable. A tube retains the path you drew. A pipe uses outer and inner rings with annular rims, leaving an open bore; its sleeves make the mouths visible. A cable redraws between its endpoints and any joined supports with a downward parabolic offset. **SAG** changes that offset while keeping the supports fixed. This is authored drape, not a simulation of gravity or cable length.

Hold the pen trigger beside another run and draw away to start a branch. To join a free end, continue it close to another run, wait for the amber marker, then release. The shared port is recorded only on release. Silver fittings mark these sockets. Connected endpoint grips disappear so extending cannot silently pull a joint apart. Use the pen there to branch, or UNDO to restore both runs and their port records. Merely crossing two skins makes no connection.

`run_network.gd` owns that relationship; `swept_tube.gd` owns the mesh. The reusable `PipeLayout` accepts named anchors and per-run `kind`, radius, sides, sag and optional sampled points. Moving an anchor rebuilds its dependent runs. Its existing glass and drain command builders remain available. See `commons/primitives/pipes/README.md` and `layouts/three_forms.json` for an executable layout example. The current workbench has no fluid, audio or electrical routing and does not save a drawing between hall visits.

### Explore the form library

**PARTS** cycles four groups from the existing pipe and glass library: straight, 45° and 90° bends, U-bend and S-bend; tee, Y-junction, cross and reducer; coil, wave, flask and beaker; cap, drip and condenser. **CABLES** shows five existing cable arrangements: chord, spline, authored sag, festoon and truss. These are supplied forms to work from, rather than five new algorithms to learn now.

Each group replaces the current drawing; UNDO brings that drawing back. A pink grip marks every declared free port, including the third and fourth arms of a junction. Choose FORM, then take a grip and draw onward. The supplied piece stays in place while a new tube, hollow pipe or cable grows from its port. Release beside another free port to connect two pieces. On supplied parts only the declared ports accept a connection; on drawn runs you can also branch along the path.

The catalogue reuses the existing geometry and port metadata through `part_library.gd`. Some stock pieces are solid display meshes; they are not all hollow plumbing. The new PIPE run has an open bore. Neither kind simulates flow. The supplied pieces keep their shape in PATH and RINGS views while your new runs reveal their construction. THICKNESS and SIDES apply to drawn runs, not the supplied part meshes. COLOUR can recolour the last supplied piece. Eight pieces and runs share the workbench budget, so a five-piece group leaves room for three additions.

### What is supplied, and what is limited

`tube_painting.gd` owns the retained strokes, undo states, view modes and bounded input. `tube_pen.gd` reuses the existing drawing pen. `tube_end.gd` reuses the existing pickup handle. `station.gd` supplies the museum console. Their complete source accompanies this tutorial; keep them intact for the first ring edit.

New samples are actual nib or end-grip positions at least 0.045 m apart. A sudden jump above 0.75 m stops further additions until release. Each stroke can retain 256 positions; the work can keep eight pieces and runs. The drawing volume and feedback keep an endpoint from writing into another exhibit. A tube at the point limit needs a separate new stroke. Tight turns and self-intersections are not repaired into a manifold solid. The pink grips and the wall are interactive/collidable; the tube skin is visual geometry. Native automated pickup and input tests are recorded separately from physical headset testing.

# A direction can have another reading

This hall follows Growth's saved turtle state. It keeps position, heading and a branch stack, then separates two changes: the angle of a turn and the mapping from heading to a drawing direction. Architecture will assign width, walls and room placements to interpreted records.

## The actual shared program

The unchanged original rule is F -> FF+[+F-F-F]-[-F+F+F]. One generation has 20 symbols and 8 Fs; two have 172 symbols and 64 Fs. The ordinary artifact defaults to generation four, with 4096 Fs. Its original three drawings still show at most 261 segments each and use strides 0.055 / 0.050 / 0.050 m. The stochastic pair retains its own two rules and seeds, at most 91 drawn segments each. The original generation bars are capped at 1.1 m; their numeric labels report actual counts. The brass stack is a static maximum-depth summary, not an animated push/pop instrument.

The new opt-in InterpreterLesson calls the same interpret() method as the original three drawings. It returns raw segment endpoints plus state records after every symbol. The new desk uses one or two complete generations without the ordinary prefix cap. All three readers use 0.32 m strides, no fitting, and origins raised equally to 2.2 m so downward reflected branches remain above their cases.

The shared interpreter uses this operation:

```gdscript
var dir := Vector2(cos(heading), sin(heading))
if sideways:
    dir = Vector2(dir.y, dir.x)
var nxt := pos + Vector3(dir.x, dir.y, 0.0) * step
```

Heading begins at PI/2. Plus adds the turn angle and minus subtracts it. The brackets push/pop [pos,heading]. The component exchange changes displacement without changing stored heading. Applying it uniformly to all displacements and saved positions reflects the whole path across x=y. It preserves distances and the abstract branch connections; it does not create a new graph. Merely seeing intersecting strokes does not establish new connected junctions either.

# What changed, and what remained possible?

The sideways tree invites a desire to find another form. The experiment lets that desire encounter its operation: the direction components exchange places. Each new position has an exact correspondence with the earlier one. Recognition may falter before the relation does.

That is valuable without making the reflection a proof of new topology. Distances, angles and the abstract branch connections survive. A fixed floor or an inhabiting body could make the reflected arrangement have different consequences, but this drawing has no branch colliders. The next step would be to implement and test that relation. The book's question about possible bodies becomes more specific when we can distinguish a changed appearance from a changed condition of support or access.

The operation also presses a linguistic expectation. F is familiar as forward, while the blue reader takes it across. Yet the program is still following its assignment. A change of interpretation need not be a failure to follow rules. The reader's commitment is in executable code, and the visitor has a limited, visible way to revise it. This does not attribute belief or consciousness to the turtle.

The older essay asked how technical practices mediate between signs and forms, and whether one formalism exhausts the possible. Those remain useful questions. Three mathematical curve families are not evidence of three cosmotechnics by themselves, and the geometry of a finite drawing cannot establish a claim about cultural difference. A fuller discussion of Simondon or Hui needs its own reading and argument. The manuscript is preserved in the archive; this pass develops the implemented comparison.

The finite curve gallery supplies another limit. A fourth-generation Hilbert panel visits 256 sampled grid vertices. More detailed stages may pack the display densely, while its texture remains a bounded raster. The parameter interval, the resulting image, the stored points and the occupied pixels are different objects to describe. Calling the construction space-filling must not turn a finite set of addresses into a claim that it has visited every point a body could encounter.

The new desk permits a few transformations of meaning and preserves a reference. It does not yet offer visitor-authored rules or arbitrary interpreters. Those are opportunities for a later pass. A useful extension would let F place a wall, leave a sound or register a visit, while retaining the same sentence and reporting the added consequences. Increased variety alone would not tell us which extension provides a useful difference for this body.

The first pass therefore keeps this question open through a dependable comparison. We can make an unfamiliar reading, locate what it preserves and undo it. Architecture will add a further demand: a drawn branch must meet the needs of a person trying to move, pause or return.
