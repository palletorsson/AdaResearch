# A path made by its next step

<!-- @flow_field_painter -->

A coloured line bends across the little canvas. At its leading end, a particle keeps moving. Choose one and stay with it while its neighbours spread into the picture.

A label above the far edge of the canvas says FIELD HELD. Something is held still here, but it is not the particles.

Press **ARROWS**. Directions appear across the canvas. An arrow stays at its address while a particle passes nearby. The particle asks for a direction where it is now, moves a little, then asks again from the place it has reached.

The bridge and the benches before it read addresses chosen along an interval. Here the calculation can be condensed into two lines:

```gdscript
var direction = field_direction(position)
position += direction * speed * delta
```

The second line changes the input to the first. Read them again with that new position. A route begins without a list of destinations.

`direction * speed` gives a velocity: how fast, and which way. `delta` is the time since the previous frame, in seconds. Multiplying by that small interval gives the displacement added to the position. The clock has entered the sum.

During each step the program uses the direction sampled at its beginning. Then it looks again. Raise SPEED while leaving SCALE alone. Longer steps cross more of the field before the next reading. The arrows have stayed where they were, but the particle is visiting a different sequence of addresses.

The trail lets us follow what those questions produced. It keeps a limited number of positions, joining them into a line. Older positions leave as new ones arrive. The particle needs its present address to continue; the recent past is kept for us to look at.

## Where a direction comes from

The field supplies one noise value at each address. With its time held, the same address returns the same value, and nearby values vary smoothly. The program turns each into an angle, then obtains a direction from that angle:

```gdscript
var angle = noise_value * TAU * 2.0
var direction = Vector2(cos(angle), sin(angle))
```

`TAU` names a full turn in radians. Cosine and sine give the two components of a unit direction. We will return to the circle that makes this possible in Wavefunctions, and to the noise supplying the values later still.

For now, look at what the conversion chose. This particle does not compare neighbouring values to find a downhill slope. It receives an angle made from one value. A similar-looking swirl could have come from another rule.

Change SCALE. The directions now vary across space differently. The existing trails still carry the earlier arrangement; give them time to leave the picture. Or use RESET to scatter new starting positions and clear their trails. RESET keeps the field's seed; SEED chooses another field as well. Changing a beginning and changing what waits there are different interventions.

Press **FIELD TIME**. Now the field changes while the particles move through it. A particle returning to an address can receive a different direction. Press again and the field holds at this later moment. Movement continues through what it has become.

## The edge returns you

Watch a particle reach the frame. It leaves one side and returns on the opposite side. The boundary has been made into a connection.

A long stroke can suddenly cross the canvas. The recorder has joined the last position before the wrap to the first one after it. Its line suggests a journey through the middle that the particle did not make. The same drawing operation that helped us follow a bend has drawn across this jump too.

![Arrows and particle trails on the canvas inside the Flow Field hall](/book-review/doc/book/figures/change/flow-field-near-museum.png)

*The arrows keep their places. Long strokes across the canvas join positions on opposite sides of its frame.*

Opposite edges are joined for movement, but their field values need not match. Follow a return and look for a change of direction. A world can provide a return route and still contain a seam.

<!-- @ -->

Elsewhere in this hall, two arrow displays, a particle swarm and steering agents offer other ways to follow a field. The Grand Zoetrope puts twelve poses around a turning drum. Compare its repeated return with a particle’s path across the canvas.

Back at the canvas, the trails can suggest a current from a little distance. Come closer and follow two particles passing near one another. Neither has to make room. They read the field without reading each other; their proximity changes the picture without changing their instructions.

What would it take for one to disturb another's route? We would have to give the neighbour a part in the next calculation. The flowing image makes that possibility tempting before the bodies possess it.

There is another relation missing from their movement. Each new direction replaces the last; no accumulated velocity resists the turn. Later in Forces, a push will end and a cube will keep going. Something it received earlier will still be crossing the room.

# Shared field experiment

The primary `flow_field_painter` is mounted at y=1.05 with scale 2.5. Its opt-in `shared_field` lesson aligns the backing canvas with the XZ trails, uses 80 particles and seed 43, and starts with field evolution held.

## Two clocks and one sampler

ARROWS reveals 24 glyphs. Both glyphs and particle updates call `field_direction`. Arrow geometry refreshes at 10 Hz; particles update every rendered frame. A nearby arrow samples its own address and has its own refresh schedule: it is a representation of the field, not the source steering the particle.

FIELD TIME toggles only the field clock, holding its current time on pause. Particles continue. In lesson mode SCALE affects the noise frequency once; legacy instances retain their existing sampling behaviour. Existing SCALE and SPEED controls remain.

The field takes a noise value, turns it into an angle and constructs a unit direction with cosine and sine. It does not calculate the gradient of the noise. The two short code blocks in final.md are teaching sketches of the angle conversion and the position update, with the coordinate mapping and renderer omitted.

## What the controls retain

RESET scatters new particle positions and clears their trails without selecting a new seed. SEED changes the seed and resets those particles. Neither rewinds every clock. Changes to speed or scale leave existing trail positions present until new samples replace them; they therefore temporarily show the history of more than one setting.

The fixed sample capacity limits each trail's memory; it is not a fixed duration. Increasing speed changes the distance travelled between samples at a given update interval. Different display timing can change the sampling and numerical trajectory. Finite updates hold a sampled direction for a whole step before looking up another.

## The return seam

Opposite edges wrap the particle coordinates. The renderer joins consecutive stored positions, including across a wrap, so a long visible segment can cross an interior the particle did not traverse. The noise itself is not periodic: wrapping position does not ensure that directions agree at opposite edges.

## Which body this makes

This is prescribed motion through a direction field, not a force, gradient or fluid model. The update stores no inertial velocity, mass or particle-to-particle collision negotiation. Neighbours can change the aggregate picture without changing one another's movement. The standalone `vector_field_visualizer`, `vector_field_grid` and other supporting artifacts are independent examples; their arrows do not steer this painter.

The revised final.md follows a single particle, the held field, a changing field, and the edge's return before introducing the missing interaction. An immersive field affecting the visitor would require a separate receiver and a verified movement connection. That remains development material, not a capability added by this prose pass.

# Flow — an address made by the preceding step

A moving particle and a held field establish two different kinds of change. Its new position becomes the next lookup address. The visible arrows help us inspect that relation without supplying the particle's actual samples. Speed changes the step length; scale changes spatial variation; advancing field time changes what a returning address can receive.

The edge joins two places for movement without making the field periodic. The trail can invent a crossing across that join. End with neighbouring particles that have no interaction rule: the current-like picture suggests a relation their bodies do not yet possess. Forces then gives a body an accumulated velocity that a new instruction cannot simply erase.

Current reading: final.md, revised 24 September 2026. Supporting implementation and operating details are in tutorial.md. This is the existing small painter, not a new room-scale current or a force acting on the visitor.

# The address comes from the update

A particle inherits an address from its last movement. Its next direction is prescribed there. Holding field time while letting particles move makes those two kinds of change distinguishable. The arrow display shares the rule yet refreshes less often; the trail joins samples across a coordinate wrap. These visible seams let the reader test what the picture can claim.

The body here stores no inertial velocity and negotiates no collision. Ask what capabilities and relations must be added for another kind of body to exist. The next sequence can answer through force, mass and acceleration rather than through a fluid-like appearance alone.
