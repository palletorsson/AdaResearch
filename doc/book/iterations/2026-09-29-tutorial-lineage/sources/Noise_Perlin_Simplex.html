# What counts as the same

A hollow on one side of the aisle faces a ridge on the other. It is easy to call that the difference between Perlin and simplex. Walk a little further before deciding.

Two changes of seed could have made two different pictures too. So could a different scale of sampling. We need a way to ask the fields a narrower question.

<!-- @simplex_noise -->

Start at the Simplex panel. Press REPLAY and choose a feature to recognise. Move FREQ a little, watch that feature change, then replay. The field returns to the declared setting. Its variation has a way back, as the colour grids did in Randomness.

In the previous room a height change caught the walking body. Here the small cubes leave space for us to walk around their arrangement. Each sampled coordinate receives a height and a colour. The underlying field relates neighbouring values; the cubes present selected values as separate steps. The grid we see is one way of reading that relation.

The Simplex visualizer asks for a value like this:

```gdscript
func generate_noise_at(x: float, z: float) -> float:
	return noise_generator.get_noise_2d(x * frequency, z * frequency)
```

The slider changes the coordinate multiplier. The display can keep the same cube positions while asking the field about a different scale. A ridge can change across an unmoving grid.

Find the pink marker. Press SAMPLE on the shared instrument and watch it move to another cube. Across the aisle, another marker takes the same address. Their heights need not agree. The readout now gives the coordinate, each field's answer and their signed difference.

```gdscript
func sample_pair(p: Vector2) -> Vector2:
	return Vector2(pair[0].sample_at(p.x,p.y), pair[1].sample_at(p.x,p.y))
```

One question goes to two functions. The coordinate is shared. The answer belongs to the function that returns it. A positive difference on the display means the Perlin value exceeds the simplex value at this address; it does not award a point to either landscape.

<!-- @perlin_noise -->

Cross to the Perlin panel and compare the readouts. Both begin with the same seed, octave count, frequency and gain. Their displays share amplitude, sample spacing and finish. The Perlin visualizer chooses its basis in this branch of `_noise_type_for`:

```gdscript
"perlin":
	return FastNoiseLite.TYPE_PERLIN
```

The Simplex visualizer chooses `TYPE_SIMPLEX`, which this engine implements as OpenSimplex2. The plate reads the choices back from the instantiated generators. Names can help us find a work; here we also have a way to check what they claim.

Both belong to gradient noise: a local direction contributes through its relation to a displacement, and contributions are combined into the value at an address. Perlin's lattice and OpenSimplex2's construction organise those contributions differently. The dot product from Vectors is still in the toolbox. The library has folded a longer calculation into the small call above; the call has not made that calculation disappear.[^engine-noise]

Choose one comparison to keep in view: the turn of a ridge, the spacing of hollows, or the difference between answers at one coordinate. The shared seed makes each field repeatable. It does not make a ridge on one side the partner of a ridge on the other.

Now alter FREQ on just one side. Look back at the central plate. Alongside the different bases, it reports another difference: the scale at which the field is sampled. That one adjustment changes what you might have attributed to the basis. MATCH returns both generators to the declared settings. REPLAY on either side returns that side alone.

Press VIEW. Keep following the pink markers as the relief is levelled at once into two flat arrangements. The colours keep their values. VIEW leaves the seed alone. If you used REGEN, you keep that new field; only the way its values occupy space has changed. Nothing has been flattened inside the noise function; the display has stopped spending its answer on height.

The cubes remain at half-metre intervals. Their square arrangement is still present whichever basis produced the values. It would be easy to mistake the grid of the display for the lattice inside the calculation. VIEW gives us another way to notice that two decisions have been folded together.

Return to relief. The same answers regain height. In Noise Space 10 we joined height to collision so a change could stop a foot. These cubes are a visual comparison with a walking aisle between them. A visible surface and a surface that carries us ask for different work from the implementation.

Turn back towards the entrance. Two arches stand before the displays. Step beneath one and look up. The same field that gave the little cubes their heights now presses into the wall around your shoulders. Your feet stay on the flat museum floor. What you were looking across has become something you can stand inside.

The arches begin with the same curve. They ask their respective fields for values across the same patch, more closely spaced than the cubes, and spend those answers on relief. Come out and press VIEW again. The little landscapes flatten; the arches remain curved. Their colour remains too. We supplied that curve before asking either noise function for anything. A surface can look grown while borrowing its room for us from a shape we chose in advance.

![Two noise fields wrapped over matching walk-through arches](images/noise-archways.png)

*Native museum view. The arches and cube displays read the same two fields. VIEW removes the added relief; it leaves the supplied arches standing.*

Follow SAMPLE back from a cube to the pink point beneath its arch. The address returns on another body. Change the frequency from outside and walk underneath again. Here the displaced surface also has collision; the arch holds its current shape while someone is inside. We have made that accommodation too.

Matching conditions costs us possibilities. One shared finish helps compare shape while withholding what another colour treatment might reveal. Half-metre samples give the field a body we can inspect; variation between those addresses has no cube of its own. The comparison makes some distinctions easier to see by agreeing to leave others out.

<!-- @perlin_noise_terrain -->

Behind the pair, a small pink sheet stands above a low plinth. Stay with it for a moment. Its surface changes in steps even while the two larger fields wait for another action.

Its timer advances a third sampling coordinate:

```gdscript
func _on_timer_timeout() -> void:
	if not _is_truthy(animate):
		return
	time_offset += 0.5
	generate_terrain()
```

The mesh helper samples a three-dimensional field. The timer visits another slice every half-second. We have met a coordinate changing before; here the change keeps arriving without another gesture. It is neither an erosion calculation nor a memory of the sheet's previous shape.

The artifact is called `perlin_noise_terrain`, but its helper leaves the generator's basis at the engine default, OpenSimplex2S. This retained work makes the room's question recur inside its own collection. Its one-metre sheet samples in index coordinates, uses seed one and has its own clock. It cannot serve as a third matched surface simply because it stands near the pair.

<!-- @dark_sphere -->

The dark orb changes too. Its pulse gives the eye another rhythm to follow, independent of the small sheet's half-second steps. MATCH does not restart either work.

A return always has a scope. We recovered the two fields' parameters, while the other bodies continued. Even a careful comparison takes place in a world that has more going on than the question currently admits.

<!-- @ -->

Look again at the hollow that first caught your attention. If you were choosing a landscape in which to hide, it might matter for a different reason than if you wanted a clear view. There need not be a winner on either side of the aisle. Keep the ability to repeat a field, change a condition and recognise what the comparison supports.

Try two tellings of the encounter: what you saw, and the operation you learned to recognise. Where they do not quite meet, you have a reason to return. Hold a seed. Change a threshold. Look at an edge after the veining is gone.

The small sheet keeps changing. A little noise, and something begins to feel organic. Perhaps that is the body we wanted. Its swelling can carry pleasure, uncertainty or a queer promise of becoming. The ease of the operation is useful: we can put a similar gesture on many different objects.

After several rooms, that usefulness can become a mental algorithmic wall. The familiar answer arrives before we have worked out the next question. Different bodies acquire a family resemblance. We can call this tendency blob culture and begin investigating it here, among our own objects. Its cheapness is the ease with which we can add the filter; what someone makes of that gesture can still matter deeply.

Stay with the sheet and ask what else this body could do. Its changing slice has no record of what happened to the preceding surface. We could give it stored states, neighbours that affect its next move, something to exchange with another body. Each would require another decision and something further to learn. Noise can remain part of those constructions.

In the next chapter, Cellular Automata, a row waits for its first update. We will let a cell's next state depend on the states beside it. Bring the pleasure of the moving surface, and leave room for another form.

[^engine-noise]: The [Godot 4.6 FastNoiseLite reference](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.6/classes/class_fastnoiselite.html#enumerations) distinguishes TYPE_PERLIN from TYPE_SIMPLEX, which uses OpenSimplex2, and TYPE_SIMPLEX_SMOOTH, which uses OpenSimplex2S.

# Try a comparison, then test its conditions

1. Choose a feature in each field before looking at their names. REPLAY the Simplex field, move FREQ, then replay it again.
2. Press SAMPLE on the central instrument. Follow the pink markers to the same local coordinate in both displays. Read their two values and the signed difference.
3. Compare the live generator settings. The matched pair differs in noise_type; a shared seed gives repeatability within each basis, not corresponding ridges.
4. Move one FREQ or OCTAVES slider. Watch the plate name the changed condition. Use MATCH to restore both declared configurations; use either REPLAY to restore only its own side.
5. Press VIEW. Check whether the answers change when relief becomes a flat colour display. Return to relief. The cube grid is a display choice shared by two different generators.
6. Step beneath each arch. Come out and press VIEW: why do the cube fields flatten while the arches remain curved? Follow the shared sample into the arch; then change a frequency from outside and walk beneath the changed surface. An occupied arch holds its shape until the visitor leaves.
7. Visit the small pink terrain. Its third noise coordinate advances by .5 every .5 seconds. Then watch the independent orb. MATCH belongs to the pair and does not restart either clock.
8. Follow the central aisle onward. The labelled Lab Path side portal is not connected. Cellular Automata changes the operation from querying a field to updating stored neighbourhood states.

No walkability, isotropy or performance ranking follows from these small display fields. A shelter comparison would need bodies and lines of sight; a speed comparison would need measurements. Keep those questions available for a later instrument.

# A live comparison contract

Current sources are SimplexNoise.gd, SimplexVisualizer.gd, PerlinNoise.gd, NoiseVisualizer.gd and the optional pair_study.gd. The original visualizer and scene resources remain unchanged in this pass. Map structure and utilities remain unchanged. The current authored row replaces the museum's stale six-work row.

## Two bases, matched conditions

The instantiated generators report TYPE_SIMPLEX (0, OpenSimplex2) and TYPE_PERLIN (3). Both use seed 20260910, generator frequency .05, fBM, four octaves, gain .5, lacunarity 2, coordinate multiplier 10 and zero sampling offset. Godot distinguishes OpenSimplex2 from TYPE_SIMPLEX_SMOOTH (OpenSimplex2S). [Godot 4.6 FastNoiseLite](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.6/classes/class_fastnoiselite.html#enumerations).

Both displays have 64 cubes, half-metre spacing, amplitude .8 and the existing shared colour ramp/finish. Local sample centres run from -2 to 1.5. Cube centres use y=value*.8 in relief and y=0 in plate. Colour uses the same signed-to-unit mapping in both views; it does not depend on whether height is displayed. The cube field has no added walking collision. Unlike Noise_Space_10, it is a visual comparison with an aisle.

## Local controls and truthful readback

The simplex placement selects `study:compare` and retains `witness:show`. The optional study looks up the Perlin twin within the nearest museum hall; it never chooses a streamed neighbour. Unconfigured placements retain their existing controls and display behaviour.

The existing front panels face the -z approach in this installation. Each retains its FREQ and OCTAVES sliders, REGEN and REPLAY buttons. The shared supported instrument adds SAMPLE, VIEW and MATCH. Five sample coordinates are (-1,.5), (1,-1), (-1,-1), (0,0) and (.5,1), all actual cube centres. Pink markers follow their current cube heights; the plate prints both generator values and perlin-minus-simplex.

VIEW applies the existing plate/relief configuration to both local roots. MATCH invokes their existing replay methods, restoring declared seeds, frequency multipliers and octave counts while retaining the selected display view and sample. It does not affect the small terrain or orb.

The witness previously updated only when constructed and always claimed that only the basis differed. The optional study refreshes the live plate every .15 seconds. The shared line formatter now reports changed contract terms. Integer seeds compare exactly, so a difference of one is not hidden by floating-point relative tolerance. The change applies to witness text in both root scripts; other artifact behaviour is retained.

## The small sheet is another experiment

perlin_noise_terrain now stands at y=1 above a low plinth. Its existing script, sampler and timer are unchanged. The live generator uses TYPE_SIMPLEX_SMOOTH, seed 1 and frequency .07. NoiseHelper sets seed/frequency and leaves the basis at the engine default. The inherited Perlin name is not evidence of a Perlin basis.

There are 484 mesh vertices (22 × 22) in a one-metre sheet. MeshHelper queries a 3D field in index coordinates: x_index*.1, y_index*.1, time_offset. Its .5-second timer adds .5 to the third coordinate. This is resampling successive slices, not integration of an evolving terrain state. The original scene also rotates its plane; its mapping is distinct from the cube pair.

# The conditions that let a difference appear

A comparison begins by deciding what may differ. The pair keeps sample positions, seed, octave count, coordinate scale, height mapping and material parameters aligned. That preparation gives the generating rule a clearer place to become observable. It also closes other avenues of inquiry. A visitor who wants the colour to do something else has a legitimate question the matched comparison has temporarily set aside.

The shared sample makes this decision explicit. One coordinate goes to two functions. Their answers differ without either becoming a failed version of the other. A seed gives each generator a repeatable configuration; it does not supply corresponding ridges across algorithms. Difference at a selected point is a measurement. Its usefulness depends on what the learner is trying to make.

VIEW changes that use. The values persist while their height contribution disappears from the display. What looked like a terrain becomes a flat arrangement of coloured cubes. Neither form reveals a pristine original behind the other. Each admits a relation the learner can work with. The visible square grid is another construction, common to both displays, and must not be mistaken for a direct view of either generator's internal lattice.

The central plate is implicated too. Previously it claimed that the basis was the only difference even after a control had changed another condition. Its sentence described the intended experiment rather than the current one. The revised instrument reads the instantiated generators and lets its claim change. A museum label can be accountable to the work it names.

The small terrain complicates the paired lesson without spoiling it. It has another sample spacing, seed, basis, mesh and clock. Its inherited Perlin name does not describe the basis selected by its helper. Keeping the work lets the visitor inspect that distinction. It can be a useful slice through a 3D field without being a valid third member of this controlled pair.

The queer possibility here is methodological: different uses remain available after we have learned a rule precisely. A hollow might obstruct a crossing, shelter a body, hold a pool or offer an image someone wants to wear. This hall does not test all those uses. It gives us a way to avoid confusing the current test with the field's only possible purpose.

The orb continues while MATCH restores the pair. We make a small region repeatable inside a world whose other processes continue. Holding a condition still is work performed for an inquiry; it is never the recovery of a world without conditions.


Palle's further question is the mental algorithmic wall of "just add noise". The easily applied filter can give a surface an organic gesture and signify queer ambiguity, desire or dissolution. Its usefulness and pleasure are part of the argument. Repetition can also make many different objects inherit a familiar body. Blob culture names a tendency to investigate, beginning with the museum itself, rather than an established claim about all visual culture.

The question is what that first satisfaction leaves unexplored. On the small sheet, a changing sampling coordinate makes the surface move without storing its preceding shape. Giving a body memory, exchanges or neighbours that affect its successor adds further possible relationships. None automatically confers life or freedom. The next Cellular Automata chapter introduces one such capacity and lets us inspect its constraints. Noise can travel into that chapter as a seed or influence; the research continues beyond the first convincing organic gesture.
