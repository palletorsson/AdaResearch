# A neck between sources

<!-- @metaball_world -->

Can two sources make one body before their centres meet? Walk the primary field and look for the narrow neck between rounded regions. The surface does not mark ownership: it is the place where a sum crosses a threshold.

At the desk, press EMPLACE. The same ten sources move from a column to a ring, a pair, a shell and a scatter. Then press KERNEL. The contribution of each source changes from inverse square to Wyvill, Gaussian or cone. Keep the source count fixed while you change the arrangement; keep the arrangement fixed while you change the falloff. That separation is the lesson.

```gdscript
# metaballgenerator.gd
total_field += mb_sr2[m] / dist_sq
if cv0 > iso_level: cube_index |= 1
```

Each source adds its strength times its radius squared over the square of the distance, and each corner above the level sets one bit of the case.

Press HOLD before making a change. The earlier boundary stays behind the live field, so a neck can be read as a relation rather than a special object. When a bridge pinches off, nothing has been cut from a mesh. A different set of samples simply crosses the same threshold in a different place.

<!-- @raymarched_metaballs -->

The raymarched specimen nearby makes a related surface by another route: signed distance functions are blended while a ray advances. Its smoothness is not evidence that it shares the same field. Look for what the extracted field can show that a ray step keeps implicit, and what the ray can make continuous without storing triangles.

<!-- @nakama_metaballs -->

Nakama keeps the blob vocabulary but never extracts a surface: it pushes one sphere in and out by the field’s value, so the body can breathe but no neck can ever pinch off. That is the comparison, and it is not a third control on the desk.

<!-- @metaballs -->

At the back the same raymarched body stands again at full size, twelve metres of it. It is not a sum: the threshold that ends it is where the ray stops. What the small copy nearby let you walk round, this one lets you stand under.

<!-- @ -->

A metaball has no outline to defend. It is a negotiation among sources, kernel, sampling grid and threshold. The next hall asks whether we need to store a boundary as triangles before we can see it.

# Influence, summed

A metaball is a source, not a small solid. At every sample position the generator adds the source contributions, then extracts the surface at one threshold.

```gdscript
var total := 0.0
for source in sources:
    total += kernel(source, p)
return total - iso_level
```

Start with EMPLACE. The count remains ten while the same sources are arranged as column, ring, pair, shell or scatter. This is a change in provenance: where the contributors stand. KERNEL changes the falloff while the positions remain fixed. Compare the neck, the isolated lobes and the points where a bridge disappears.

HOLD keeps a copy of the earlier mesh. RULE opens the short statement that the visible surface is a threshold event, not a census of objects. The raymarched and Nakama specimens keep alternative implementations in view without collapsing them into one answer.

# ISO_Metaballs technical

The map preserves the original 18 × 17 structure at offset (6, 7) inside a longer 30 × 32 museum room. `metaball_world` is the single book-primary field; `raymarched_metaballs`, `nakama_metaballs` and `metaballs` remain secondary comparisons. `iso_metaball_console` is a decoration and forwards no field state of its own.

The console binds the `MetaballGenerator` child of `metaball_world`. EMPLACE cycles the exported `emplacement` enum; KERNEL cycles `kernel`; both call the generator's guarded `apply_grid_config`. HOLD duplicates the current ArrayMesh as a visible earlier boundary. RESET returns the shipped column/inverse-square pair. Controls use the project's pointer-aware push buttons and forgiving touch volumes.

# The body after the boundary

A merged metaball surface looks organic, but the appearance does not tell us which source owns a patch of skin. The field erases that division by summing before extraction. This is useful and restrictive: it permits a neck without a seam, while hiding provenance inside the result.

The desk separates arrangement from kernel so the smooth body cannot be mistaken for a natural body. A shell and a scatter can share a silhouette under one falloff and separate under another. The threshold is a decision that appears as geometry. It makes a body possible, and it withholds the reasons from the surface that body sees.

blurb.md

Raw captured source

Each metaball is a point radiating influence. A distance field — strongest at center, fading outward. One sphere alone is trivial. But place two near each other and their fields sum. Where combined density crosses the threshold, a surface appears. Not two objects touching. One object, born from proximity.

The marching algorithm traces this threshold — finds the skin where inside becomes outside. Move the sources closer: the surface bulges, stretches, merges. Pull them apart: it pinches, thins, splits. No mesh editing. No vertex welding. The geometry is a consequence of relationship.

Metaballs are the implicit surface at its most liquid. Form without skeleton. Identity without boundary. Two fields overlapping don't collide — they become one body, then separate again, unchanged. Merging is not loss. It's the native state.

---

Two methods. Same phenomenon. Sixty CPU-driven blobs approximate organic form through sphere deformation — Kouhei Nakama's approach, where geometry chases the field. Nine shader-driven balls bypass geometry entirely — SDF raymarching renders the implicit surface directly on the GPU, never extracting a mesh at all.

Metaballs are isosurfaces of summed radial functions. Where influence overlaps, surfaces merge. Separation and fusion governed by a single threshold. The math doesn't distinguish between one blob and two — only whether the field exceeds the boundary.

One method builds triangles to describe the surface. The other evaluates the field per pixel and stops when it hits the edge. Both find the same thing: the contour where inside becomes outside. The surface was always there. The question is whether you extract it or just look until you see it.
