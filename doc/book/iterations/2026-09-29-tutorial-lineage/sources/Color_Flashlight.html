# What reaches the surface

<!-- @flashlight_demo -->

Several pools of colour meet on a pale board. Their edges are easier to follow than their crowded middle. Pick one edge. Which lamp is responsible for it?

Take the red lamp and turn it aside. Follow what leaves the surface. Bring it back slowly, across the other pools. There is a place where its red becomes difficult to name on its own. Move through that place again. Nothing has been painted over, yet something has appeared that neither lamp makes alone.

Leave the red lamp aimed at the surface. It stays where you release it. Take the green one and move its pool across the red. Separate them, then let their edges meet. Try the blue. The white lamp begins aimed aside; bring it into the comparison when you want to see what another contribution does. If the middle grows too pale to distinguish, separate the pools again. Their edges give you somewhere to begin.

In the previous room, colour was assigned to forms and positions. Here you can move one of the conditions of its appearance with your hand. What, exactly, have you changed?

The receiving surface keeps the same material throughout. Its stored base colour, its *albedo*, is a near-white value. Each lamp carries a `SpotLight3D`: a light with a position, a direction and a cone. Turning the handle turns the cone. The surface receives a different contribution because you have changed where that cone falls.

Aim a beam off the board and onto the floor or the cube standing to its side. The cone can cross the boundary of its intended exhibit. Does the colour still read the same way? Follow a shadow or an edge before giving the result a name. The lamp carries a value, but it cannot carry a complete account of what you will see wherever you point it.

Beside the board stands a coarse, pale sphere. Carry the red beam across it. One face brightens while its neighbour stays dull. Move the lamp to the other side and watch the difference change places. No face has been repainted.

We met these flat faces among the primitives, where they interrupted the promise of a smooth sphere. Here they give the light different directions to meet. Each face has a direction, called its *normal*, which the renderer uses along with the light and material to calculate its appearance. What looked like one pale body begins to answer face by face. Bring a second colour across the first. The construction you could barely see now divides their meeting.

![The same pale sphere under a red lamp held on either side. The camera, mesh and material stay fixed.](/book-review/doc/book/iterations/2026-09-23-flashlight-faceted-sphere/same-sphere-moving-light.png)

The red lamp has crossed from board to sphere without changing its assigned colour. Inside each lamp, `flashlight_color.gd` finds the light and gives it its colour:

```gdscript
var spot = _find_spot_light(target_root)
if spot:
    spot.light_color = light_color
```

Four copies of the same scene receive four values. The red instance is given `Color(1, 0, 0, 1)`; the green instance `Color(0, 1, 0, 1)`. The first three numbers address red, green and blue channels, each from zero to one. Zero contributes none of that channel; one gives its full value. The fourth number, alpha, stays at one here.

The assignment happens when the scene starts. While you move the lamp, its colour value stays fixed. You have changed where its light meets the room.

There is a useful limit here. These lamps offer RGB values to a renderer. They do not calculate a spectrum of wavelengths. The room also has other illumination: turning the lamps away does not promise darkness. Even the pale surface we have kept fixed is a chosen condition of this experiment. We have made a comparison manageable by leaving much of the world in place.

Perhaps the surprising body here is the patch of colour. You cannot pick it up separately from the meeting that produces it. Move one participant and its boundary moves; withdraw another and it changes again. The code gives us a way to repeat that meeting. There is still more to ask of it.

<!-- @colour_light_rooms -->

Through the side opening, a white room waits with a small light hanging near its centre. Stay with one corner as rose passes into violet. The walls keep their edges. Their colour has left one arrangement and entered another.

Earlier, your hand moved a lamp whose colour stayed fixed. Here the lamp stays in place while its colour changes. You can keep walking, or let the room change around you while you stand still.

On the far wall, a wide pink rectangle draws you towards it. Its narrow luminous lip gives it an outline. From the middle of the room, how much depth can you find inside that outline?

Walk closer, then move sideways. A return wall comes into view. There is room to step inside, and a back wall that stops you. The rectangle has become a recess without anything being rebuilt. Its pink sources keep their colour while the light in the room moves on. The surrounding colour changes the meeting you are looking at.

![The pink recess, photographed from inside the installed white room.](/book-review/doc/book/iterations/2026-10-02-light-an-opening/figures/pink-threshold.png)

The next doorway opens below a tall light well. Three rectangular lips draw the eye upwards. Between them, the walls close around the opening; beyond the last lip is the museum's sky.

Wait beneath it. Rose leaves the ceiling for violet. The code changes the lights around the opening; it never assigns a new colour to the sky. Does the piece of sky you see still seem to belong to the same distance?

![The same light well and viewpoint at two moments. The roof and its opening stay fixed while the surrounding lights change.](/book-review/doc/book/iterations/2026-10-02-light-an-opening/light-well-pair.png)

A shallow ramp brings you onto a raised landing. Look back through the well. The nearer edge covers another part of the farther one. The view from below has company now. Neither position can give you every edge at once.

James Turrell's light rooms and Skyspaces offer a reference for staying with this uncertainty.[^apertures] Here a rendered sky meets an enclosure made from engine primitives. We can inspect every wall and still need to move to see how their edges fit together.

# Move a condition of appearance

Primary encounters: `flashlight_demo`, then `colour_light_rooms`. Read `final.md` for the book voice and `technical.md` for the implementation.

1. Find the boundary of one coloured pool on the board. Identify the lamp responsible by aiming it aside, then back.
2. Leave that lamp aimed at the board. Move another so the pools separate and overlap. Describe the change before naming additive colour.
3. Bring the white lamp into the comparison. If the overlap becomes hard to distinguish, separate the beams again.
4. Aim onto the floor or side cube. Carry the same light across the faceted sphere; compare neighbouring faces before naming their normals. No face has been repainted.
5. After following the light between these receivers, inspect `spot.light_color = light_color` in `flashlight_color.gd`. The assignment stays fixed while position and direction change.
6. Enter the side white room. Keep one corner in view while the central light changes. Compare this fixed lamp/changing value with the handheld lamps' fixed values/changing positions. Approach the pink recess, then move sideways to find its return walls. Its two sources keep their pink value while the surrounding room changes.
7. Enter the taller room and look through the stepped light well. Wait for its light to change, then walk up the ramp and compare the lips from the raised landing. Separate these observations: first change time while holding the viewpoint, then change viewpoint. The room's script does not recolour the museum sky.
8. Carry light-versus-material into Color_Nails, where a colour choice addresses a surface resembling part of a body.

The lamp's grab and orientation are the interaction. Do not instruct the visitor to switch it off, choose a hue, spawn a prism or select a receiving-ground variant: this placement provides none of those controls. Ask what the RGB model can show and what it leaves unrepresented, without treating this renderer as a spectral optics laboratory.

## Making score: a room receives a value

After reading the encounter, return to the supplied `colour_light_rooms` scene. Its `set_phase()` method includes the same kind of assignment used by the handheld lamp:

```gdscript
central_light.light_color = c
```

First give `c` one fixed RGB value, run the scene, then substitute another. Keep the white receiving material and camera position. This reuses the light assignment already introduced in this hall; building all the room geometry is a separate exercise. The luminous reveal has its own material and is not the source that lights the walls.

Then inspect `_process(delta)`: Point Zero's repeating callback advances the phase of a 110-second cycle. Pause the cycle with `running = false` and call `set_phase(0.16)` or `set_phase(0.38)` to compare two moments. Those are authoring changes in the script, not controls installed in the hall. The transferable operations are assigning a light colour, changing it over time, and holding conditions fixed to read a difference.

## Repeat the comparison

## Trace the implementation

The map places this token at grid (9,9):

```text
flashlight_demo:180:0:1#plinth:0#mount:standing#receiver_sides:7
```

The registry resolves `flashlight_demo` to `res://algorithms/postprocessing/flashlight_demo/flashlight_demo.tscn`. That scene instances `flashlight.tscn` four times. Each copy inherits XRTools' pickable scene and contains a spotlight and a `ColorSetter` child running `flashlight_color.gd`.

The colour assignment is actual source:

```gdscript
var spot = _find_spot_light(target_root)
if spot:
    spot.light_color = light_color
```

Red, green, blue and white instances supply `Color(1,0,0,1)`, `Color(0,1,0,1)`, `Color(0,0,1,1)` and `Color(1,1,1,1)`. Lens tint and emission are set separately so the handle's lens identifies the beam. They do not recolour the board.

The receiving `Canvas` is a 6 × 4 × 0.1 metre CSG box. Its material has albedo `(0.95, 0.95, 0.95, 1)` and roughness `0.9`. The lamps have a 25-degree spotlight angle, range 20 metres and shadows enabled. Direction, distance, material and other scene lighting contribute to the rendered result; pixel values should not be equated directly with the exported RGB value.

## Standing installation

The optional `mount:standing` metadata is read by `flashlight_demo.gd` before it stages the receiving surface. It places the board centre at local y=2, enables board collision, and puts each handle at y=1.1. Lamp energy is 2.5 in this installation; white begins turned 60 degrees aside. The map places the root at museum floor level. Legacy placements retain their previous mounting.

The separate `ground` authoring option supports `white`, `swatch`, `relief`, `mirror` and `none`. This room uses `white`. These are authoring configurations, not controls offered to the visitor. In particular, the option called `mirror` changes material properties; it does not install the project's player-reflection system.

# A colour you cannot carry alone

Turn the red lamp away. The patch changes, although the board's material has not. This small discrepancy gives the room its critical opening. Calling the patch a property of the board was convenient; now that account leaves something out.

The lamp does carry an assigned colour. Its `ColorSetter` gives the spotlight an RGB value when the scene starts. But that value does not specify the complete appearance of every surface it might illuminate. The visitor changes a direction, the cone meets another part of the room, and the renderer evaluates another situation. A colour value can remain stable while what we see changes.

This makes relation available to experiment. Separate two pools; overlap them; remove one contribution by aiming it aside. The provisional body of the overlap has no independent handle. Its boundary depends on participants that can leave. As an artistic argument, this offers a way to investigate an identity sustained by a meeting. It does not demonstrate that every identity, outside this encounter, obeys the same rules.

The apparatus also selects what can count as a meeting. Four lamps share one scene and receive different RGB assignments. Their apparent differences are produced within a common implementation. That commonality makes some recombinations inexpensive. It does not make every desired transformation available: the visitor cannot turn a lamp into a reflective material simply by changing its RGB value. Different consequences require different connections in the code.

Keeping a near-white board fixed gives us a comparison we can follow. It also postpones questions about other receiving materials. The map's authoring system offers swatches and relief, but those variants are not installed as visitor controls here. Aim onto the existing floor or side cube first. What happens when the beam leaves the surface chosen to make its lesson easy to read?

The room uses RGB lights, not a calculation of wavelength spectra. Calling its beams red, green and blue is useful for following its code. It should not become a claim that three numbers exhaust colour, or that all viewers will make the same distinctions. Nor is this an isolated dark chamber: the museum's other illumination remains in the result. The conditions we did not move are still participating.

For Ada's question, “what bodies are possible?”, this room supplies a precise working case: a visible patch sustained by light, a receiving material and a rendering arrangement. The queer invitation is to try another relation and attend to the difference it produces, while keeping track of what this particular system permits. QFEP can frame that inquiry into constraints and unfamiliar possibilities; this encounter does not measure a numerical entropy threshold.

In the annex, the pink outline gathers attention before its recess gives the body a place to approach. The overhead opening also offers an inviting central view. Moving sideways or climbing brings other edges into relation. Keep the level-floor and raised viewpoints equally legitimate: authoring a compelling view should not make its eye height a compulsory body. The geometry can be inspected, but no single position shows all of its boundaries together.

The next room changes the receiver's significance. When the surface resembles part of the player's body, the difference between illumination and a chosen material becomes a question of appearance one might wear.
