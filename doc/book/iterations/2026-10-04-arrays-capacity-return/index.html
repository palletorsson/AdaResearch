From an indexed arrangement to a rule, a surface and a score. This review compares the saved teaching plans with their revised proposals, beside images of the existing museum encounters.

Summary metadata updated. Implementation remains unchanged.Full tutorials, final manuscripts, artifacts, room geometry and runtime logic are outside this change. Images are dated prior captures, shared by both versions. The Disco tutorial contradiction remains open pending a full rewrite.

The sequence arc

Before · saved plan

The list first used to retain a trace now supplies addresses for objects, values and events. The route expands to two and three indices, admits bounded variation without surrendering addresses, then reads the same structures as a pattern and a score. Repetition is a construction the learner owns; its spatial display and physical meaning stay separate.

After · revised proposal

PROPOSED. Keep an address while changing what its value asks a receiver to do. Return to Trace’s list and Transformation’s positioned body; extend a small occupancy record into a plane and a volume with explicit storage conventions. Then let an address choose a tile’s turn, give the same image another support, and let a clock read a score. Occupancy, orientation and musical events remain different kinds of data. Carry the read/write operation and declared readers into Colour and Tiling; automatic transport between museum exhibits is not assumed.

Before · saved plan

Keep the address, vary its occupant

The labelled row loses a cube without closing its gap; the square grid gives that retained address two indices. Bringing Trace's list and loop, the student builds a small grid and keeps occupancy values separate from the references to its visible cubes. Nested loops place the copies; a supplied collection event changes one value. At the Molnár study, they add one bounded random offset per site and store it, so changing its strength does not draw again. The result is a repeatable arrangement that distinguishes address, occupant and chosen departure.

After · revised proposal

Keep a place when its occupant leaves

The row loses a cube without closing its gap; the nearby grid lets the student locate another absence with two indices. Bringing Trace’s list and loop and Transformation’s positioned body, they begin with four slots, then extend the same read/write operation to a small grid. Follow the installed convention, grid_data[x][z], and keep occupancy values separate from visible-node references and spacing. A named display adapter and collection event are supplied. The student empties a chosen slot, changes the spacing and explains why the other addresses remain. They carry the occupancy record and its reader. Molnár’s retained departures offer an optional next experiment; the changing floor remains an encounter with the physical consequence of an empty place.

Try it independently

Empty a chosen slot, then change the spacing. Predict which stored value changes and explain why neither operation renumbers the neighbours.

A question to keep open

The address survives an absence. What does the stored zero leave unexplained?

Before · saved plan

Make a third index usable

The flight enclosure lets the student reach a selected cube above the ground layer and return to its empty address. Building on their two-index grid, they extend a small study to layers, explicitly connecting display order x,y,z with storage order layer,row,column. The flight region, selection marker and collection signal are supplied. The new capability is to navigate a three-index record; visible gaps are not extra array entries.

After · revised proposal

Name the layer and keep the convention visible

In the flight enclosure, the student selects a cube above the ground and later reads zero where it stood. They bring the two-index occupancy record and extend the study to layers. The displayed address is [x, y, z], while the installed volume reads values[y][z][x]. Before changing a value, they show how the earlier [x][z] plane becomes one [z][x] layer: the conversion is part of the lesson. Flight, selection and collection are supplied. The student predicts an unequal-index address, changes its value and reads it back without shifting its neighbours. They carry an explicit storage-to-space mapping. Open structures and suspended fragments remain further uses of addressed places, not additional required data systems.

The complete summary, before and after

Before · saved plan

A rule reaches every tile

At the black-and-white fields, the student changes one motif and sees a larger figure cross the tile joints. Their indexed grid and rotation function now become a pattern generator: a small asymmetric tile is supplied, while they assign its quarter-turn from the row and column. Remainder arithmetic makes the count return after four, and a condition lets one chosen address depart from the rule. The large floor and comparison controls remain supplied. The student leaves with a local address rule that can generate a field of any chosen finite size.

After · revised proposal

Let an address choose a turn

At the paired four-by-four samples, one extra turn at each address makes a larger figure cross the joints. The student brings addressing and rotation, but now gives the value another declared meaning: zero quarter-turns is an orientation, not an empty slot. With the asymmetric tile and image renderer supplied, they write one address-to-turn function using remainder by four. They predict a selected tile’s result, then add one exceptional address. Enlarging the field repeats the same calculation without adding instructions. They carry the motif choice, turn rule and exception separately. The other fields provide comparisons; the floor’s apparent ridges do not become steps underfoot.

Try it independently

Predict a turn at an address you have not inspected. Keep one exception, then enlarge the field and explain why the local rule needs no extra clauses.

A question to keep open

When a larger figure appears across the joints, where does the work of the small rule end?

The complete summary, before and after

Before · saved plan

A picture and the wall carrying it

The six familiar fields stand upright, and a patterned wall can turn open onto a finite landing. Reusing their tile rule, the student mounts the same generated image on a small upright surface and compares it with the floor version from several distances. Image generation, the existing hinge and the exterior landing are supplied. They distinguish turning a mark within an image from turning its carrier and collision together; no new syntax is required. A short comparison record travels onward: the same data can face another direction without gaining another physical permission.

After · revised proposal

Keep the pattern, change what carries it

The same pattern now stands in front of the student. They bring the motif and turn rule from the floor and reuse its generated image on a supplied upright receiver. The eye chart and a walk toward and away from it let them compare what remains legible as its apparent size changes. No new syntax is needed. At the exception wall, they predict the difference between turning a mark inside the image and turning the sheet with its collision. One alters the picture; the other can open a passage. The hinge and finite landing stay supplied. They carry a comparison separating source, appearance and access, including the brief chance to look back at the outside of the museum’s enclosure.

Try it independently

Before using either control, predict which turn could open a route and explain why changing the image alone cannot do so. Keep the same image while comparing floor and wall.

A question to keep open

What was the thin enclosure doing for our forward walk, before we saw its other side?

The complete summary, before and after

Before · saved plan

Read a score with a clock

The monochrome floor changes while its squares remain still, and the separate sequencer begins with empty cells. Bringing indexed values and the elapsed clock, the student writes a short repeating score, then advances a playhead through it at a chosen interval. A supplied sound receiver accepts track and trigger; the existing grid display and floor remain available for comparison. One loop reads the stored pattern while another count can drive visual phase. The learner can move an event without changing tempo and change tempo without rewriting the score, carrying a reusable timed reader onward.

After · revised proposal

Let a clock read the choices you kept

The monochrome floor changes while a separate sequencer offers empty cells to fill. The student brings indexed lookup, remainder and elapsed time. They make a short score with explicitly named track and step indices, following the instrument’s [track][step] convention; false means no new sound trigger, not a missing floor tile. A supplied sound receiver handles the note. The student advances a playhead through the kept score, moves an event without changing tempo, then changes tempo without rewriting the entries. They stop the score and watch the floor continue. They carry a reusable timed reader. The sixteen-step score’s connection to twelve floor columns is a comparison of addresses, not a lesson in recording footsteps.

Try it independently

Move an event while tempo stays fixed, then alter tempo without changing any entry. Stop the score and predict which floor behavior continues. As a further comparison, explain why step 0 and step 12 can flash the same floor column.

A question to keep open

What changes when you move with a rhythm but the system does not record your steps?

Open contradiction · full tutorial still awaits rewrite
