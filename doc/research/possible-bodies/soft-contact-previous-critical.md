# Twelve tests, twelve questions the material must answer with its body

Ahmed asks not just what a body can reach, but what it encounters on the way. Orientation is not a single direction — it is a field of affordances and obstructions that the body discovers through contact. The obstacle gallery is this field made systematic. Twelve scenarios, twelve different geometries that the soft body must negotiate. Each obstacle poses a different question: can you fit through this gap? Can you wrap around this curve? Can you survive this impact? The body cannot answer in advance. It must try, deform, and discover.

The gallery format reveals something important about orientation that a single obstacle cannot: the same body responds differently to different geometries. The soft body that wraps beautifully around a sphere may wedge in a narrow gap. The body that squeezes through a channel may splatter against a sharp edge. Compliance is not a universal virtue. It is specific — the body yields in ways that depend on the particular geometry of what it encounters. Ahmed describes this as the unevenness of orientation: the same body is more or less capable depending on the direction it faces, the space it enters, the demand the environment makes.

Merleau-Ponty's flesh is tested most sharply in the soft-on-soft collision scenario. When two compliant bodies meet, neither is the immovable reference. Both deform. Both yield. Both record the encounter in their spring networks. The mass-weighted correction — heavier bodies move less, lighter bodies move more — introduces a hierarchy, but it is a hierarchy of inertia rather than rigidity. Both bodies are changed by the contact. This is Merleau-Ponty's reversibility without the asymmetry that rigid obstacles enforce: two soft bodies touching each other is the closest the simulation comes to genuine mutual perception, where neither party is purely subject or purely object.

The narrow gap scenario carries the most political weight. The gap width determines passage. A soft body that is stiff enough to maintain its shape cannot fit through a narrow opening. A body that is compliant enough to squeeze through loses its shape in the process. The trade-off between structural integrity and navigability is not abstract — it is a material constraint that determines what spaces the body can access. Ahmed writes about institutions that admit certain bodies only if those bodies are willing to reshape themselves: the gap is the institution, the deformation is the cost of entry, and the stiffness parameter determines how much the body insists on arriving in the shape it left with.

The empirical stance of the gallery — observe, don't predict — aligns with Merleau-Ponty's phenomenological method. He distrusts theoretical accounts of the body that proceed from principle to prediction. The body is not deducible. It must be encountered. Each gallery scenario is an encounter that cannot be fully anticipated from the equations, because the interaction between hundreds of springs, multiple contact points, friction coefficients, and geometric constraints produces emergent behavior that exceeds what any single formula describes. The gallery teaches through accumulation: twelve observations build an intuition that no equation could provide alone. The body learns what it can do by doing it, and each doing leaves a trace that informs the next.
