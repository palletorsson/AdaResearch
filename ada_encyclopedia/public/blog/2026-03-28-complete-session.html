I. Day 1–2: Naples (March 23–24)

The Museum Floor

The session began with photographs from the Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli — MANN. The collection includes Roman mosaics excavated from Pompeii and Herculaneum: geometric floors that have survived two thousand years because their mathematics is sound. The question was whether we could rebuild them procedurally in Godot, from first principles.

The Truchet Problem

The truchet pinwheel was the hardest. The naive approach — diagonal flip — produces a woven pattern, not the Roman original. The fix was 90° rotation per tile, not reflection. Each tile has a quarter-arc in one corner; rotating it 0°, 90°, 180°, or 270° gives four states that, placed randomly on a grid, produce continuous curving paths. The key insight: rotation preserves arc continuity at tile boundaries, while reflection breaks it.

The difference between a Truchet tile and a Roman mosaic is 2000 years of accumulated craft knowledge compressed into a rotation rule.

MosaicPalette and the Aging Shader

All six patterns share a MosaicPalette — a unified color system derived from actual Roman pigments: terracotta reds, travertine whites, volcanic blacks, Pompeian blues. An aging shader adds surface wear: micro-cracks, grout erosion, color desaturation, and differential weathering where foot traffic would concentrate. The palette and shader together give procedural geometry the look of excavated stone.

Ring Stack Carpet Composer

The mosaic editor on the web side lets you compose floor designs using a ring-stack metaphor: concentric regions from center outward, each with its own pattern, palette, and border treatment. This matches how Roman craftsmen actually worked — building outward from an emblema (central panel) through successively simpler border zones. The editor outputs JSON that drives the Godot artifact.

II. Day 2–3: Facades (March 24–25)

The Galleria System

From floors we moved to walls. The Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II in Milan provided the reference — a 6-zone facade system dividing each bay into base, arcade, mezzanine, piano nobile, attic, and cornice. Each zone has its own vocabulary of ornamental elements: rusticated stone at the base, half-columns and pilasters in the middle registers, dentil courses and modillions at the cornice.

The FacadePartLibrary ended up with 42 parts organized into 9 category files under commons/facade_parts/. The web-side FacadeComposer at /facade-builder renders 52 SVG glyphs and outputs a v2 plan JSON format that Godot reads directly.

22 Facade Presets

Each preset encodes a historical style as a part configuration. The progression tells a story of architecture itself:

Classical → Roman

Doric simplicity, Corinthian elaboration. Column orders, entablature, pediment. Every proportion derived from the module (column diameter).

Renaissance → Baroque

Alberti's palazzo type. Rustication, piano nobile, stringcourse. Then Borromini breaks the rules: curved walls, hidden geometry, optical tricks.

Pompeii

The painted facade — not carved stone but fresco on plaster. Four Pompeian styles, each a different relationship between wall and illusion.

Postmodern

Superstudio's continuous monument. Memphis Group's arbitrary color. Deconstructivist fragmentation. The facade as commentary on the facade.

Villa San Michele

The exact recreation of Villa San Michele (Axel Munthe's house on Capri) tested the system's limits. The villa mixes Roman spoliae, medieval arches, and Art Nouveau whimsy in ways no preset can capture. It required manual part-by-part composition — which proved the facade builder could handle it. Every column, every arch, every loggia bay placed individually.

III. Day 3: The Grid (March 25)

Floor Plan Editor

Floors and facades need rooms. The floor plan editor introduced edge-based walls — walls defined not by grid cells but by the edges between them. This means a single cell can have a wall on its north side and nothing on its south side. Doorways are edge interruptions. The editor supports undo, multi-select, and exports directly to the map JSON format that Godot reads.

The crucial design decision: walls live on edges, not faces. A cell at (3,4) has four potential wall edges: north, south, east, west. This is how architects actually think about plans — rooms defined by their boundaries, not their contents.

FloorPlanSpace Artifact

The FloorPlanSpace artifact takes a floor plan JSON and constructs a standalone museum room in 3D: walls at the correct height, doorways cut into them, floor patterns applied per-room, lighting calculated from room volume. Each room becomes a self-contained teaching space.

The Failures

Iteration 1 was a thin bitmap — draw the meander pixel by pixel on a grid. It looked right at 1x but fell apart at any other scale. Iteration 2 thickened the lines, which broke at corners. Iteration 3 switched to geometry quads, which worked for straight runs but couldn't negotiate turns. Iteration 4 got an exact visual match by hardcoding coordinates, which is not procedural at all.

Iteration 5 introduced braid topology — treating the meander as two interlocking strands that weave over and under. This produced beautiful patterns that were not meanders. Iteration 6 tried boundary functions (implicit surfaces), which produced smooth curves that were too smooth. Iteration 7 went tile-free: just a self-avoiding walk on a grid. Closer, but the walk didn't know when to spiral inward and when to continue forward.

The Theoretical Detour

Iterations 8 through 11 went deep into mathematics. Self-avoiding walks (iteration 8) connect to polymer physics. Braid groups (iteration 9) formalize the over-under pattern. Category theory (iteration 10) abstracts the composition rules. And 2.5D knot theory (iteration 11) treats the meander as a projection of a knot in three-dimensional space.

Each framework explained SOMETHING about the meander. None of them explained everything. The meander sits at the intersection of combinatorics, topology, group theory, and craft knowledge. No single formalism captures it.

The Insight: Tiles Repeat, Mosaics Don't

The fundamental constraint revealed itself through failure. Tile-based approaches (iterations 1–7) assume that the pattern can be decomposed into repeating units. But a meander border, especially one that negotiates corners, cannot be decomposed this way. Each stone in a real mosaic meander is placed individually, responding to its local neighborhood. The border doesn't tile — it flows.

Tiles repeat. Mosaics don't. The meander requires per-stone freedom — each element responding to its neighbors, not copying a template. This is the fundamental constraint we haven't yet solved.

Space-Filling Ontology

The meander investigation opened onto a larger question: how do patterns fill space? The meander is a space-filling curve (it visits every cell of a strip). So is the Hilbert curve. So is the Cantor set (in the limit). So are Penrose tilings (aperiodically). Each of these is a different answer to the same question: how does a one-dimensional path cover a two-dimensional region?

V. Day 5: Theory (March 26–27)

22 Blog Posts

The practical work generated theoretical questions. The theoretical questions generated 22 blog posts forming an interconnected architecture of ideas. Each post addresses one facet; together they describe a methodology for learning through building.

The Theoretical Architecture

QFEP Methodology

F + E → λ → P. Formal model meets Experiential encounter. The gap (λ) is where learning happens. Products emerge from navigating the gap.

Building as Knowing

Procedural reconstruction as epistemology: you don't understand a Roman floor until you've written the code to generate one. The failures teach more than the successes.

Grid as Governance

The grid is not neutral infrastructure. It constrains, enables, regulates. The fresco is a miniature city — its borders enforce order, its motifs assert belonging.

Queer Readings

Deviation, entanglement, orientation. What happens when a pattern refuses its grid? When a meander breaks its own rule? These are queer moments in ornament.

The Lambda Parameter

The QFEP framework crystallized around the λ parameter. In every reconstruction attempt, there is a gap between the formal model (the algorithm) and the experiential reality (the physical mosaic). This gap is not a failure — it is the site of learning. The 14 meander iterations each produced a different λ, and each λ taught something that neither the algorithm alone nor the mosaic alone could teach.

F(algorithm) + E(mosaic) → λ(gap) → P(insight)
λtruchet = rotation vs. reflection
λmeander = tile vs. per-stone freedom
λfacade = preset vs. manual composition

The 2D–3D Knowledge Loop

Theory demanded tooling. The 2D–3D knowledge loop connects web-based 2D interactive editors to VR 3D experiences through a bridge artifact called the Science Screen. Each algorithmic primitive gets a web editor (at /primitives/point, /primitives/line, /primitives/grid, etc.) that teaches the concept interactively. The VR infoboard shows the concept embodied in three dimensions. The Science Screen places the 2D editor inside the 3D world — a window from VR into the web.

By end of day 5: 16 primitive editors built, 20 VR infoboards connected, the Science Screen artifact working.

VI. Day 5–6: Infrastructure (March 27–28)

The LOD System

With 1670 artifacts, 657 maps, and 55 sequences, the project outgrew the ability of any single document to describe it. The LOD (Level of Detail) system applies the same principle used in 3D graphics — show less detail when far away, more when close — to knowledge itself.

python tools/lod_query.py # L0: overview
python tools/lod_query.py mosaicanalysis # L1: sequence detail
python tools/lod_query.py pompeii_mosaic_floor # L2: artifact detail
python tools/lod_query.py meander # fuzzy search

The tree is generated from the actual codebase by lod_tree_generator.py, not maintained by hand. Any change to the code changes the tree. Sessions can record discoveries with lod_session_writer.py, and those discoveries become part of the tree for future sessions.

Heat Map and Focus Vector

The heat map generator scans the project and produces a weighted grid of what needs work. Red cells are broken or empty. Yellow cells are incomplete. Green cells are solid. The focus vector takes the heat map output and calculates a productive direction: what to work on next, based on what has worked before.

The focus vector doesn't just say "work on spine sequences." It says "spine sequences, because the last three sessions that focused on spine sequences produced 4.2x more commits per hour than sessions that jumped between tasks."

The /continue Skill

One command to resume work. /continue reads the LOD tree, checks the heat map, consults the focus vector, reads the last session's handoff notes, and starts working. No orientation phase. No context loading. Just: pick up where the last session left off.

Command Center

The command center is a 6-panel mission control dashboard with real data. Coverage metrics, heat maps, task queues, capture status, blog index, session history. Everything an AI agent or human operator needs to understand the project state at a glance.

Oversight Light and Ada-Researcher

Oversight Light: an execution engine with a task queue. Tasks go in, results come out, everything is logged. The autonomous researcher skill (ada-researcher) uses the execution engine to perform deep investigation — reading code, cross-referencing documentation, testing hypotheses — without human intervention. It reports findings, not questions.

VII. Day 6: The Foundation (March 28)

Spine Sequence Audit

The spine is the curriculum. 42 sequences, each teaching a different algorithmic concept, each containing 5–15 maps. The audit found 71 maps with missing or broken spawn points and teleporters — the basic infrastructure a player needs to enter and leave a map. All 71 were fixed in a single pass.

Six sequences had zero maps. They existed as concepts in the curriculum spine but had no physical space in VR. Each was filled with at least one map — a minimum viable teaching environment that future sessions can expand.

Map Studio

The Map Studio replaced the old map builder with a 3-layer editor built from scratch. Three layers: structure (floors, walls, heights), utilities (spawn, teleporter, ramps, labels), and interactables (artifacts). Each layer has its own editing mode. The editor validates as you build — no spawn point means no save.

Maps as Narrative Spaces

The day's central discovery: maps are not containers for artifacts. Maps are narrative spaces. Every map follows the same arc:

The Map Narrative Arc

Entry — The player spawns. First impression sets expectations.

Teaching — The core concept is presented. Artifacts demonstrate, infoboards explain.

Exploration — The player experiments. Sliders, buttons, parameter spaces.

Reflection — A quiet space to consolidate. Often with a view back toward the teaching area.

Exit — The teleporter to the next map. Positioned to pull the player forward into the sequence.

Maps are not rooms with stuff in them. Maps are stories with a beginning, middle, and end. The spawn point is the first sentence. The teleporter is the last.

The Priority

Day 6 ended with a clear focus: spine sequences are the ultimate priority. Every tool built this week — the facade builder, the floor plan editor, the pattern catalog, the LOD system, the command center — exists to serve one purpose: building maps that teach algorithms through spatial experience.

IX. What We Learned

  1. Building is knowing — You understand a Roman mosaic by writing the code to generate one. The 14 failed meander attempts taught more about Greek key patterns than any art history textbook. Procedural reconstruction is epistemology.
  2. Tiles repeat, mosaics don't — The fundamental constraint. Tile-based generation assumes repeating units. Real mosaics have per-stone freedom. Each tessera responds to its neighbors, not to a template. This is why the meander resists tiling.
  3. The grid is governance — A mosaic grid is a miniature city. Its borders enforce order. Its motifs assert belonging. The ornamental program of a Roman floor is a political statement about who lives here and what they value.
  4. The λ parameter — The gap between algorithm and reality is not a failure. It is where insight lives. F + E → λ → P. Every reconstruction attempt produces a λ that neither the formal model nor the physical artifact could produce alone.
  5. Maps are narratives — Entry, teaching, exploration, reflection, exit. A VR map is not a room with artifacts in it. It is a story with a beginning, middle, and end. The spawn point is the opening line. The teleporter is the closing sentence.
  6. The LOD tree is a mosaic — Fractal knowledge at every scale. Zoom out and you see 55 sequences. Zoom in and you see the individual function that generates a single truchet tile rotation. Same structure, different resolution. The tree is the project and the project is the tree.
  7. Focus vectors drive productive work — Heat map identifies what needs work. Focus vector calculates the productive direction based on what has worked before. Agent picks up the vector and commits. The cycle is: heat map → focus → agent → commit → repeat.
