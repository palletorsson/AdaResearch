# Array — the same address at two scales

**Bring:** the familiar point, grip interaction, independently placed cubes and the distinction between an object and its position.

**Support:** the 32-address row now sits above a one-metre-high, two-metre-wide platform of metre boxes. Addresses are spaced one metre apart. Paired wedges at each end provide two-metre-long approaches; every support module remains editable in the lab editor. The array cubes keep their original collection behaviour.

**Do:** walk along 32 cubes. Collect one: its value becomes zero while the other addresses keep their places. Cross the 7 × 7 grid and distinguish `[1,2]` from `[2,1]`. Grip the small pink point beside shaft B. Move it across the miniature grid; the matching address lights up in the shaft. Write zero to open that tile, or one to restore support.

**Learn:** stable indices; independent array rows; occupancy values; local position mapped to a discrete index; the same data represented at two physical scales; collision following a value.

**Surprise:** twelve centimetres of hand movement selects another support tile sixty-eight centimetres away in the larger grid. Scale changes, but `[x,z]` stays the same address.

**Controls:** VR grip holds the point; move your hand over the board; trigger toggles the selected cell. Desktop right-click picks up or drops the point; aim and use the wheel to adjust reach; left-click toggles the cell. With the point released, use ADD, REMOVE and RESTORE ALL on the panel. R / right B retries the trial.

**Installed instruments:** the row's live readout hangs from two ceiling plates. The 7 × 7 readout keeps its existing inward-facing ceiling mount. The hand-index board and its three rectangular keys sit in cream cabinets, connected by rear trunking to a junction on the slab above. These are housings for the existing data and controls; the service connection is visible architecture, not a simulated power network.

Both the miniature grid's address display and the right-hand control unit's readout sit at 2.35 metres, angled down towards the visitor. The grid, grab point and keys stay at hand height, leaving the view towards the shaft open beneath the displays.

**Enter the volume:** shaft A contains 6 × 6 × 6 collectible cubes. Fall into its glass frame to borrow the noise exhibit's flight: neutral input hovers; looking and moving carries you through the addresses. Desktop Space rises and Ctrl descends; in VR, aim the left controller and use its stick. Leaving the box restores gravity. Collect all 216 cubes by touching them with the player body; each collected value becomes zero while its `[x,layer,z]` label stays in place. The frame remains after the last cube. The old upper landing pad is removed to keep every address accessible.

**Pass:** 32 row cells and 49 grid cells retain independent addresses. Actual desktop and XR grip select the same indices. A write changes the mesh and collider of exactly the selected tile. Restore waits if a body occupies a missing support. The 3D array retains 216 addresses while the player collects every cube; flight starts and stops at the frame and does not depend on remaining cubes. Retry restores all arrays without changing museum progress.

**Carry forward:** a grabbable point as an index controller; one occupancy model driving a miniature and walkable support. Move, copy or turn the complete assembly in the lab editor. Its shaft grid must be kept over a shaft if removing a tile is to open a drop. Its 4.76 m footprint fits the fixed five-metre shaft; its cells are 0.68 m, not one metre.

**What was 03?** The previous COPY / CONSUME / RESTORE deck added or removed one-metre support cubes; an agent could do this automatically. That adapter remains in the library. This trial now uses explicit hand-selected indices so it is clearer which address each action changes.

**Scope:** separate Array trial. Original `final.md` and `tutorial.md` stay unchanged; the Molnár comparison and subsequent dedicated 3D, tiling and clocked-array halls remain future work. Native encounter tests do not substitute for learner-code tests or physical headset testing.
