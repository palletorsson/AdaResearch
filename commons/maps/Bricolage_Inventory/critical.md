# Bricolage_Inventory - Critical

## Philosophical Framing

Lévi-Strauss distinguished the bricoleur from the engineer in "The Savage Mind" (1962). The engineer designs from first principles, creating tools specific to the task. The bricoleur works with "whatever is at hand"—a finite, heterogeneous set of tools and materials not originally designed for the current task.

This distinction maps to computation: the engineer writes algorithms from mathematical specification; the bricoleur recombines existing libraries, patterns, and code fragments. Most software development is bricolage.

## What This Concept Cannot Hold

The map presents an idealized, clean inventory. But real bricolage involves:

- **Damaged parts**: Components that don't work as expected
- **Unknown affordances**: Parts whose potential hasn't been discovered
- **Incompatible materials**: Things that can't be combined
- **Missing pieces**: The part you need but don't have
- **Excess inventory**: Too many options paralyzing choice

The pristine workshop metaphor conceals the messiness of actual making.

## Politics of the Inventory

Who decides what's "at hand"? The inventory is never neutral:

- **Colonial extraction**: Whose materials become whose inventory?
- **Economic access**: Premium parts vs. salvage
- **Knowledge asymmetry**: Knowing what's possible vs. what's available
- **Gatekeeping**: Who gets access to the workshop?

The bricoleur's "making do" can be a creative response to scarcity, but it can also be romanticized poverty. The engineer's "designing from scratch" can be innovation, but it can also be resource-intensive privilege.

## Queer Reading: Inventory as Identity

Queer theory offers a reading of inventory as non-normative identity construction:

- **Received categories**: The primitives come pre-labeled (cube, sphere, cylinder)
- **Refusing categories**: The bricoleur asks "what can this BECOME?" not "what IS this?"
- **Recombination**: Identity built from available fragments, not designed from essence
- **Making do**: "Wrong" parts used in "right" ways (a sphere as a joint, a cylinder as a column)

The bricoleur's workshop is inherently queer: it refuses the blueprint's insistence on proper parts in proper places.

## The Specimen Problem

Displaying primitives as "specimens" invokes museum epistemology:

- **Decontextualization**: Parts removed from use, placed under examination
- **Classification**: Each specimen labeled, categorized, positioned
- **Colonial gaze**: The viewer examines; the object is examined

This is pedagogically useful (see one thing at a time) but philosophically troubling (things only become meaningful in use).

## Questions That Remain Open

1. Is the bricoleur's "making do" a virtue or a constraint? When does creative limitation become enforced scarcity?

2. Can digital primitives truly be "inventory"? Unlike physical materials, they can be duplicated infinitely. Does this change the nature of bricolage?

3. The map assumes primitives are the atoms of construction. But why these primitives? Why not voxels, splines, point clouds?

4. Where does the inventory come from? The bricoleur inherits tools—from whom? With what debts?

5. Can the engineer become a bricoleur by choice? Or is bricolage always necessity masked as creativity?

## QFEP Connection

In the Queer Free Energy Principle framework, the inventory represents the stable foundation (F term)—the ordered, known, predictable elements. But the bricoleur's question ("what can this become?") introduces entropy potential (E(S))—the recognition that each part contains unrealized possibilities.

The workshop is a free energy minimum, but it's a staging ground for assembly, where new configurations will test new constraints.
