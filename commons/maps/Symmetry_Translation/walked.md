# Symmetry_Translation — walked

> R-021/R-028: considered critical tutorial, ghost-drafted from the working map;
> Palle rules the voice. The walk (tutorial) woven with the turn (critical).

## The cast

pattern_tile_puzzle · pattern_loom · ten_print_textile

## The walk

The tile repeats. The `pattern_tile_puzzle` lays your motif down, shifts it by one cell-width, lays it again, and again — and that single move, iterated, is the *lattice*, the invisible grid of repetition under every wallpaper, carpet, brick wall and tiled floor. Watch the `pattern_loom` perform it as labour: warp threads pulled taut, one motif dragged across the floor into a carpet that has no natural edge, only the edge of the room it happens to be in. Translation is the first and simplest of the four symmetries — reflect and rotate and glide are still two maps off — and it is worth feeling how much it already does: infinite surface from one finite square, purely by *not changing anything except position*. Then find the `ten_print_textile`: a loom running the one-line 1980s program `10 PRINT CHR$(205.5+RND(1))`, weaving an endless bolt of diagonal fabric — a symmetry machine with a single coin-flip inside it.

## The turn (critical)

Pure translation is sterile — perfect repetition, the same square forever, and a wall of it is closer to a prison than a pattern. The map's turn is what the `ten_print_textile` smuggles in: **one random bit inside the machine, and repetition becomes cloth.** The lattice provides the order (every tile in its place); the coin-flip provides the difference (which diagonal, this cell); and between them, with almost nothing, you get a textile that reads as alive rather than stamped. This is the sequence quietly refusing the binary the whole curriculum keeps dismantling — order versus chaos, structure versus noise — by showing them as *collaborators*: the grid without the flip is dead, the flip without the grid is static, and the bolt of 10 PRINT fabric is exactly what they make together. It forward-rhymes hard to randomness (the seed, the die) and back to the arrays' loom (the grid as the first computer) — and the loom is worth crediting by its makers, not just its mechanism, because translation is the symmetry they lived by: the weavers, most of them women, in Persian carpet workshops and at village looms across the world, who carried a repeating unit and shifted it across the warp all day, running the lattice by hand long before it was a word. It sets up the chapter's real argument: symmetry is not the enemy of variation, it is the *frame* variation needs to become form. The tile repeats — and the moment one thing inside it is allowed to differ, the repetition stops being a cage and becomes a weave, which the weavers also knew, since no two of their rows were ever quite the same.
