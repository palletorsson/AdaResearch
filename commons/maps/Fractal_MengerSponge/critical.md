# Which openings can be inhabited?

The sponge offers a concrete distinction between topology, geometry and a body's capabilities. Repeated removal creates connections. The dimensions of a particular opening decide whether a particular capsule can enter it. Flight brings a passage within reach without shrinking the visitor or disabling the walls. These are separate operations with different consequences.

The object can have more connected holes while offering few additional routes to this body. The second cut makes one-metre openings. A ray crosses them; the tested standing capsule cannot. More detail has not automatically made the room more inhabitable. Nor would a smaller body necessarily encounter the same refuge or exposure as the current one. That comparison remains open.

GHOST lets the space borrowed by the body appear as the procedure's discarded volume. The deleted cells acquire another use. This is a precise place to explore queer misuse: the algorithm's remainder becomes a visitor's passage. It need not make every deletion liberating. In this construction we retain enough information to redraw the complement, and the map provides a separate supporting floor. Those conditions matter to what the removal can afford.

The connection to [Entropic morality](../../../doc/research/qfep/entropic-morality.md) concerns who carries the consequences of a revision. A maker at the desk can request a new shape. A visitor inside depends on the previous one. The desk therefore refuses solid rebuilds until the enclosure is empty, while allowing non-solid view changes. This limited interlock does not establish a general politics of consent; it makes one dependency explicit and preserves the occupant's current route out.

The protective boundary has another role. The glass makes the domain of borrowed flight legible. Leaving its ground doorway returns the body to ordinary locomotion. A boundary can sustain a usable capability as well as constrain it. We should test whose movements this arrangement supports and whose it excludes, including those who would prefer to remain on the floor.

The desire for another opening remains productive. It asks which variable needs revision: connection, aperture, movement, body size, or collision. It also meets finite costs. The third cut would contain 8,000 retained cubes and smaller passages again. Its object count is a resource question, not an entropy score or proof of greater freedom. The value of the next detail must be encountered in relation to the bodies and uses it could make possible.
