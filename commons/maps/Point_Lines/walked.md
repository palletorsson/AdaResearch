## What this map holds (seed)

A line connects two points. Two lines cross — the X marks intersection. Parallel lines organize direction. Then: perspective, scale, the grid. Lines become measure. Measure becomes pleasure — the satisfaction of knowing distance, the elegance of proportion. Measure is also violence. What doesn't fit the grid is remainder, error, erased. The line inherits this: efficient, relentless, forgetting everything but endpoints.

## Why it was built (seed)

Concept: Multiplication of lines into relational systems — parallels produce direction, crossings produce intersection, grids produce metric frameworks. Discrete relations begin behaving as networks.
Sequence role: Second map. Extends Point_One's single mark into connection and multiplicity. Relation replaces isolation.
Critical angle: The politics of the grid — who decides which lines are parallel, which directions are privileged. Le Corbusier's Modulor as ideological measurement system; perspective as Renaissance power geometry.

## The walk

Point_One gave you one mark. Now the map gives it a companion and everything changes: with `line_builder_3d` you join two points, and the instant you do you have made the first *relation* — not a thing but a between. Grab the `grabbable_line`, cross it with another, and the `plus_line_puzzle` names what appears at the meeting: an intersection, a place that belongs to both lines and neither. `parallel_line_puzzle` teaches the opposite move — lines that agree never to meet, and in agreeing, produce **direction**. Then the map tilts toward measure: `perspective_lines` bends the parallels toward a vanishing point, `scale_lines` and `laser_measure` turn the space into distances you can read, and `dgrid` lets the whole thing settle into a network — the point's descendants, organized.

> **Dwell — `dgrid` · ~50s**
>
> Stop at the grid before you admire it. What you are looking at is almost
> nothing — an 8×8 lattice is sixty-four points and one hundred twelve edges,
> geometry Godot renders without effort — and that near-nothing is the most
> consequential object in the room. The word for what it does is cadastral,
> from *capitastrum*, the Latin register for the poll tax: a grid makes space
> countable, ownable, taxable, enforceable. Jefferson's survey drew
> square-mile sections across the American West with no regard for indigenous
> territory or watershed; the Ordnance Survey gridded Ireland for tax and
> garrison; the Torrens system erased Aboriginal claims by demanding proof in
> the colonizer's own index. The grid does not describe territory — it
> produces territory as property. And you are not outside it: this gallery is
> itself a 7×21 grid of cells, and the headset is reporting your position
> ninety times a second. Grab a point and pull — the lattice deforms, lines
> dragging their neighbors, and for a moment the frame admits it is made, not
> found. That is the thing to remember: every grid was imposed by someone,
> and then forgot it.
>

Two figures stand in the room as the map's arguments, not its furniture. `modulor_man_demo` is Le Corbusier's Modulor — a human body redrawn as a ruler, proportion made into a man made into a standard. And `klee_walking_point` is Klee's line "a dot that went for a walk" — drawing as *time and movement*, the line remembering it was once a gesture. The map lets you feel both readings of the same stroke at once: the line as measure, and the line as walk.

> **Dwell — `modulor_man_demo` · ~50s**
>
> Stand in front of him and ask the question the map wants asked: *which*
> body? Le Corbusier called the Modulor "a measuring tool based on the human
> body and on mathematics," but the body it is based on is specific — adult,
> male, European, able; 183 centimetres because that was declared the
> standard French height; arms that extend precisely, legs that stand erect;
> a person abstracted into three numbers, 113, 183, 226. This is particular
> universality: one body's proportions announced as everyone's, then poured
> into concrete — doorways, ceiling heights, room dimensions, all scaled to a
> man who does not exist. And a standard body is a machine for producing
> non-standard ones. The moment the Modulor becomes the unit, the too-tall,
> the too-short, the differently-shaped become error — deviation from a norm
> that presents itself as nature. Feel the pleasure the figure offers,
> because it is real: proportion is elegant, measure is satisfying, and the
> map never pretends otherwise. Then hold the cost next to it. *Whose body
> fits the grid?* was never a rhetorical question. Every doorway you have
> ever ducked through was an answer.
>

> **Dwell — `klee_walking_point` · ~50s**
>
> Watch the dot go for its walk, and notice what kind of line it refuses to
> be. Every other line in this room is straight, and straightness is not a
> neutral shape — it is a value system. The straight line privileges speed
> over attention, destination over journey, universality over locality,
> calculation over intuition. It is the geometry of the shortest path, the
> optimized route, the trackable trajectory; its command is simple: move
> straight, move efficiently, be trackable. Klee's line disobeys on every
> count. It curves, it circles back, it repeats itself, it takes detours that
> serve experience rather than arrival — the wandering path that explores
> instead of arriving, the return that refuses linear progress. And it
> remembers something the measuring instruments around you are built to
> forget: that a line was once a movement, that it had a body and a duration
> before it had endpoints. This is the trace's insight kept alive inside the
> measuring apparatus itself — a stroke that will not collapse into its two
> ends. The map sets it opposite the Modulor deliberately. One figure says
> the body is a ruler. This one says the body is a walk.
>

## The turn (critical)

The line is where geometry first becomes **pleasurable and dangerous in the same gesture.** The blurb says it exactly: measure becomes pleasure — the elegance of proportion, the satisfaction of knowing a distance — and measure is also violence, because every metric frame decides what counts and consigns the rest to remainder. `modulor_man_demo` is the map's evidence: Le Corbusier took a body and made it a standard, and a standard body is a machine for producing non-standard ones — the too-tall, the too-short, the wrong-shaped, all of it now *error* against a grid that presents itself as natural. Perspective is the same move at Renaissance scale, a geometry of a single privileged eye that the culture then called "realistic." Whose parallel? Whose vanishing point? Whose body is the unit?

And this is why `klee_walking_point` is set against the Modulor in the same room, because the map is not just diagnosing the line's violence — it is holding out the alternative the line tried to forget. Klee's walking point keeps the trace's insight (the line was a movement, it had a body and a duration) alive *inside* the measuring apparatus, as a refusal to let the stroke collapse fully into its two endpoints. The book's position lives in that tension: measure is real and useful and beautiful, and it is never innocent, and the walk is what it costs. `fontana_puncture` sits here too — the cut has not gone away; the line is just the wound extended.
