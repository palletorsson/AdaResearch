# The body that yields is the body that knows — orientation, flesh, and the politics of compliance

Sara Ahmed asks: what can this body reach? What is within its horizon of action, and what requires it to turn, strain, twist toward something the world did not arrange for it? The jelly cube is a body with an orientation problem. Its rest shape encodes an expectation — *this is what I was, this is what I should be* — but every force it encounters reorients it. Gravity pulls it toward the floor. The platform edge bends it over a boundary. The softmill grinds through it without permission. The cube does not choose its encounters. It is thrown into a physics that acts on it whether or not it consents.

Ahmed's concept of orientation is about more than direction. It is about what a body is aligned toward, what it can access without strain, and what it must deform to reach. The jelly cube's spring constants are its orientations made literal. High stiffness orients the cube toward its rest shape — it snaps back, refuses the world, maintains its given form. Low stiffness orients it toward compliance — it yields to whatever touches it, loses its remembered shape, becomes whatever the environment demands. The interesting regime is neither. At moderate stiffness, the cube negotiates. It deforms under pressure but retains enough memory to recover. It reaches toward the world without dissolving into it.

This is not metaphor layered onto physics. The spring-mass system *is* a model of orientation. Each spring encodes a relationship between two vertices — a constraint that says *this is how far apart we should be*. The rest length is not a neutral technical parameter. It is a norm. The cube's "natural" shape is the shape that power (the designer, the initial conditions, the code) inscribed into its springs before the simulation began. Every deformation is a deviation from that norm. Every recovery is a return to it. Ahmed would recognize this immediately: the rest shape is the straight line that the body is expected to follow, and deformation is what happens when the body encounters something that doesn't align with the path it was given.

Merleau-Ponty's flesh offers the other half. For Merleau-Ponty, the body is not an object in the world — it is the medium through which the world becomes available. The body touches and is touched. It sees and is seen. It is simultaneously subject and object, the thing that perceives and the thing perceived. The jelly cube, dropped from an elevated platform, is flesh in this precise sense. It does not merely *receive* the impact. It *registers* the impact through its entire topology. The bottom vertices hit first, stop, compress. The springs transmit the force upward. The top vertices continue downward, driven by momentum the bottom can no longer share. The cube flattens, oscillates, settles. The deformation *is* the cube's perception of the floor. Not a measurement taken from outside, but a structural reorganization that happens *because* the body is made of responsive material.

The softmill makes this vivid. When the rotating arm passes through the cube, the cube does not merely collide — it wraps. Points near the arm are displaced outward. Points far from the arm shift sympathetically, pulled by their spring connections. The entire body reorganizes around the encounter. This is Merleau-Ponty's reversibility: the cube shapes itself around the obstacle, and the obstacle's shape becomes legible *in* the cube's deformation. The mill's geometry is written into the cube's posture. The body becomes a record of what touched it.

The Verlet integration method deepens this. Velocity is not stored as a separate quantity — it is the difference between where the body is now and where it was one frame ago. The body's motion is literally constituted by its history. There is no present without past. Each position is a palimpsest: the current frame written over the previous one, the previous one still legible in the implicit velocity. Merleau-Ponty would call this the body's habitual knowledge — not a memory stored somewhere else, but a way of moving that carries the past *in* the movement itself. The jelly cube does not remember its previous position in some data structure called "memory." It remembers by being displaced from where it was. The displacement *is* the memory.

Ahmed and Merleau-Ponty converge on the rest shape. For Ahmed, the rest shape is an orientation — a direction the body has been turned toward by forces outside itself. For Merleau-Ponty, the rest shape is the body schema — the habitual posture that the body returns to when external demands relent. Both understand that this default is not neutral. The cube's rest shape was chosen by a designer. A different designer would have chosen differently. The cube does not know this. It simply recovers toward the shape it was given, calling that recovery "equilibrium," as though equilibrium were natural rather than constructed.

The elevated platforms matter here. They create a vertical hierarchy — some surfaces are higher, some lower. The cube, dropped from above, experiences gravity as a force that orients it downward regardless of what it wants. It cannot choose to fall upward. It cannot choose not to fall. Ahmed writes about compulsory orientations — the directions that bodies are forced to face because the world was built for particular trajectories. Gravity, in this simulation, is the compulsory orientation. The platforms are the built environment. The cube's deformation on impact is the cost of navigating a world that was not designed with soft bodies in mind.

The spring constant k, then, is not a physics parameter. It is a measure of how much the body insists on its own form versus how much it yields to the world's demands. At k approaching infinity, the body is rigid — it refuses all contact, maintains its shape, breaks rather than bends. At k approaching zero, the body has no self — it dissolves into whatever surrounds it. Between these extremes is the space where materiality happens. Where form is neither given nor abandoned but continuously renegotiated between what the body remembers and what the world requires.

This is the integration phase at its most literal. The soft body integrates — numerically, through Verlet steps, and existentially, through the negotiation between internal constraint and external force. The jelly cube is not a metaphor for the queer body. It is a demonstration of what all bodies do: hold a shape that was given to them, encounter forces that reshape them, and find some accommodation between memory and pressure that lets them continue.
