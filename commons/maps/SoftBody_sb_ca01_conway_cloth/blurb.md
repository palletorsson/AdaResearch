Run Conway's Game of Life for eight generations on a small grid. Whatever is alive at the end becomes a mass point. Whatever has a 4-neighbor that is also alive becomes a spring. Pin the top row. Drop the rest. What hangs is a tattered lattice — sparse, holed, with limbs that end where the cellular automaton happened to leave a dead cell.

Conway's rule never had a body in mind. It is two-dimensional birth and death on a flat grid. The DNA bridge takes the pattern at one frozen moment and treats it as the skeleton of a cloth. The cloth that results is not designed. It is what Conway happened to leave behind, hung from its top row, weighed by gravity, held by whatever springs the still-alive cells managed to chain together.

A small lattice with holes is barely a fabric. It tears under its own weight in some runs; in others the surviving structure makes an unrepeatable tapestry. The rule is the same. The boundary conditions and seed decide what cloth the rule wove on a given afternoon.
