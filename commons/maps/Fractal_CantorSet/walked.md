## The walk

Take a line. Remove its middle third. Two segments remain; remove *their* middle thirds. Keep going. The `cantor_set` runs this rule in front of you, and the tutorial content is what the rule leaves behind: after infinite removals the total length is zero — yet uncountably many points survive, more points than there are whole numbers, arranged in a dust with structure at every scale. The map gives you the number that names this in-between condition: D = log(N)/log(S) — N self-similar pieces, each 1/S the size — which for Cantor's dust is log 2/log 3 ≈ 0.631. Not zero (a point), not one (a line). Learn that formula here; it governs the next three maps. The `cantor pagoda` builds the deletion into architecture — each storey one iteration, the building thinning as it rises — and the radial version wraps the same rule around concentric circles, so you can walk the absence instead of squinting at it.

## The turn (critical)

The Cantor set is the curriculum's first object that exists **by subtraction alone**. Everything you have built so far was made by adding — place the point, draw the line, stack the cube. This thing is made entirely of removal, and what remains after infinite loss is not nothing: it is *richer* than the line it was carved from, an uncountable population with zero measure. Sit with how strange that is politically, not just mathematically — an identity constituted by what was taken, holding more structure than the whole that preceded the cutting. And its dimension, 0.631, is the first number in the book that refuses the census question: point or line, zero or one? *Neither.* Mathematics called these objects pathological for a generation — monsters, curiosities, not real geometry — and Cantor himself was driven to breakdown partly by colleagues who treated his infinities as disease. The lambda-edge reading inverts the diagnosis: the dust is not the exception to geometry but its habitat, and "pathological" was always a word for what the classifiers could not place. The middle third is also the book's seed of the uncomputable — a rule you can state in six words whose finished output no process can ever hand you. Fontana cut the canvas once, at Point_One. Here the cut recurses forever, and what it produces is not a wound but a world.
