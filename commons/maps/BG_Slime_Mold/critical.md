# BG_Slime_Mold — Critical Reflection

Physarum polycephalum has no brain. No neurons, no central nervous system, no plan. The slimemold artifact simulates its behavior through virtual agents depositing attractant on a diffusion grid — sniff, turn, step, mark. From this: transport networks, maze solutions, adaptive graphs that reroute when severed. The @identity block names the essence: "Physarum-inspired viscous tubes and pools — organic growth connecting food sources through chemical trail reinforcement." The critical parameter is viscosity. High viscosity produces cohesive tubes; low produces runny spreading. The organism's computational character is controlled by how thick it is.

Simondon's individuation framework encounters something genuinely new in the slime mold. Most of Simondon's examples — crystals, organisms, technical objects — individuate once and maintain their form. Physarum individuates continuously. It has no stable form. It extends, contracts, reroutes, splits, merges. Its individuation is perpetual and never resolved. The metastable state is not a precondition of individuation but the organism's permanent address. Physarum lives at threshold — always between one topology and the next, always resolving and re-destabilizing. This is what makes it computationally interesting and philosophically troubling: it is a system that never finishes becoming.

Hayles's concept of distributed cognition finds its strongest biological example here. Physarum's "intelligence" — its ability to solve mazes, approximate Steiner trees, rebuild Tokyo's rail network — is not located anywhere. There is no processing center. The slime mold thinks with its entire body, which is also its environment, which is also its memory (the chemical trails). Cognition, in Hayles's framework, is not a property of minds but a process that flows through material systems. The slime mold makes this literal: its cognition is its flow. Where the tubes thicken, information persists. Where they thin, information fades. Memory and forgetting are hydraulic events.

The circular observation pit — slime sprawling in a central void, player walking the raised ring — echoes the organism's radial expansion. But the echo is ironic. Physarum spreads outward from a point, and the player circles that expansion. The organism is centrifugal; the observer is orbital. They move in perpendicular geometries. The architecture stages the encounter between two incompatible spatial logics: the blob that spreads and the body that circles. Neither can occupy the other's trajectory.

What does this map normalize? Optimization without intent. The blurb insists: "The blob doesn't solve problems. It just grows, and solutions are what growth looks like when nothing is in charge." This is a powerful claim about the nature of solutions — that they need not be sought, that they can be side effects of persistence. But the claim also normalizes a particular view of intelligence: that it is a pattern to be recognized after the fact, not a capacity to be exercised in the moment. Physarum is intelligent because we can compare its networks to our rail systems. If we could not make that comparison, would we call it intelligent? Intelligence here is retrospective attribution, not prospective capacity.

What does it exclude? The organism's suffering, if suffering is the right word. The slime mold does not experience its optimization. It does not know it has solved a maze. The food sources it connects are consumed; the trails it abandons represent failed explorations that cost metabolic energy. The network is efficient from the outside. From the inside — if there is an inside — it is a history of hunger, waste, and blind extension. The beauty of the network is the observer's beauty. The organism's experience, whatever it is, is not consulted.

Simondon's philosophy insists that the technical object deserves respect for what it is, not for what it resembles. The slime mold is not a rail network. It is not a computer. It is not a problem-solver. It is a slime mold — an organism with its own mode of existence, its own temporality, its own relationship to its milieu. To read it as an optimization engine is to instrumentalize it, to make its existence legible only through the lens of human engineering. The critical question is not "how does the slime mold solve problems?" but "what does it mean that we can only recognize its existence by comparing it to our own technologies?"

The trails thicken where flow matters and fade where it doesn't. Growth without blueprint. But the blueprint was always ours, projected onto the blob after the fact. The slime mold has no paradox. We do.
