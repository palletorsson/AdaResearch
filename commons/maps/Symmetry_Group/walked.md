## The cast

pattern_maker_station · loom_bolt_memphis_p4g · pattern_artifact

## The walk

Now the four moves become one object. At the `pattern_maker_station` you paint a domain and choose the symmetries that act on it — translate, reflect, rotate, glide — and the crucial thing to notice is what happens when you *compose* them: do a reflection, then a rotation, and the result is another move that also leaves the pattern unchanged. It was already in the set. Try any two, in any order; the answer is always some third move already on the list. That property — the set contains its own consequences — is what makes it a **group**, and the station lets you feel it as a closed loop rather than read it as a definition. The `pattern_artifact` wraps the whole thing as a placeable pattern that carries its group as data. And beside it the `loom_bolt_memphis_p4g` unrolls a Memphis-palette bolt on the very same group a Bauhaus loom would use — same mathematics, wildly different look.

## The turn (critical)

Closure is the map's tutorial spine and its critical hinge at once: **a group is the complete set of moves that preserve a pattern, and composing any two of them lands you back inside the set.** Nothing you can do to a wallpaper by its own symmetries takes you outside its symmetries — the pattern is, in the exact mathematical sense, self-consistent under its own operations. That is a quietly profound thing to hand a body: you have been composing transforms since the transformation chapter, but here composition *closes*, and closure is the first appearance of the idea that will run to the end of the book — the complete, finite, self-contained system. Then the turn sharpens on the two looms. Memphis and Bauhaus, side by side, run the *same group* — identical symmetry, identical mathematics — and could not look more opposed: Bauhaus all discipline and primary restraint, Memphis all clash and refusal. The group is **innocent**. It sees translation and reflection; it cannot see the palette, the taste, the ideology, the politics of the design — and those are exactly what the two bolts fight about. This is the sequence's sharpest political note, and it forward-rhymes to the whole post-crisis argument: the formal structure is neutral, and everything that matters to people lives in what the structure cannot see. Same seventeen groups under Bauhaus and Memphis and the Alhambra and the tatami-maker's floor alike — and that last one earns a name the classification never gave it: the Japanese mat-makers who solved a *p3m1* packing as domestic-floor grammar, laying rush and rice-straw to a rule of thirds and mirrors centuries before it was a cell in the table, one more craft that walked into a symmetry group by hand and left without the credit. The mathematics never took a side, which is precisely why the sides had to be taken elsewhere — and why the many hands that closed the same census across the world, in tile and thread and mat and brick, are owed the same standing as the one European name that later counted them.
