Concept: A flock, a school, a swarm — Craig Reynolds' three rules (separation, alignment, cohesion) prove that coordinated group motion needs no choreographer. Each boid sees only its neighbors, applies three weighted forces, and the flock emerges. The boids algorithm is the founding demonstration that local rules produce global intelligence.
Sequence role: Third map in Swarm Intelligence (14th spine sequence, integration phase), intermediate difficulty. After Physarum (biological swarm) and flow fields (field-based navigation), this map introduces agent-to-agent interaction — the classic flocking model where the swarm behavior emerges from peer relationships rather than environmental fields. Connects forward to SwarmIntelligence_Agent_Based_Modeling_ABM where the agent model generalizes beyond flocking; follows SwarmIntelligence_FlowFeilds.
Technical angle: Three steering behaviors computed per frame for each boid: separation (steer away from nearby neighbors within a short radius), alignment (steer toward the average heading of neighbors within a medium radius), cohesion (steer toward the average position of neighbors within a long radius). Weighted sum of the three forces drives velocity update. Spatial partitioning (grid or k-d tree) for efficient neighbor queries. The three radii and three weights form a six-dimensional parameter space that produces qualitatively different swarm behaviors.
Critical angle: Reynolds published the boids paper in 1987 and proved that flocking is not a collective decision but an emergent property of individual rules. No bird knows the flock exists; the flock is a side effect of each bird knowing its neighbors. This is the philosophical core of swarm intelligence: the whole is not directed by any part, yet the whole exhibits properties no part possesses. The three rules are also a minimal ontology of social physics: avoid collision, match velocity, stay together.
Key artifacts: BoidsAlgorithm implements Reynolds' flocking model with configurable separation, alignment, and cohesion weights and radii, visualizing emergent flock behavior from individual agent rules.
Gap: Needs a predator-prey extension — an artifact that introduces a predator agent, triggering evasion behavior and revealing how the flock's shape changes under threat, demonstrating that flocking is also a survival strategy, not just an aesthetic phenomenon.
