A four-storey Neapolitan palazzo. The ground floor is a grid of stone pyramids — each block carved into a four-sided diamond that projects fifteen centimetres from the wall plane, catching raking afternoon light at every facet. Above, smooth ashlar runs the upper storeys. Windows on the rusticated ground are framed in flat smooth surrounds, so every opening reads as a small calm rectangle inside the loud diamond grid.

This is the principle surfaced. Where Classical applied every axis canonically and Baroque turned the rhythm dial up, this facade concentrates the entire compositional voice into *one* axis — the wall surface itself. Rhythm is regular. Hierarchy is canonical. Column order is Doric and quiet. But the rustication carries the building's politics: defensive (the pyramids scatter what's thrown at them) and decorative (the cost of carving thousands of identical pyramids by hand was the wealth being displayed). Texture as statement.

The deeper lesson the map carries: the assembly grammar's six axes are *independently dial-able*. Pushing one to maximum while leaving the others canonical still composes. The facade reads as a coherent argument — *this wall says: I am defensible, I am expensive, I am legibly Neapolitan-Renaissance* — because the rule held everywhere else, freeing the surface to shout. Walk past the diamond grid. Notice that the shouting is the message.
