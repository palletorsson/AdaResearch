Concept: VectorOperations introduces the three operations that make vectors useful beyond arithmetic: dot product, cross product, and projection. One island shows how the dot product measures alignment between two directions — A dot B = |A||B|cos(theta) — with a projection tool that casts one vector's shadow onto another. Another pairs basis vector rigs with the cross product, which creates a vector perpendicular to both inputs. The third grounds these abstractions in physical consequences: projection decomposes gravity on a tilted surface, normal force resists the perpendicular component.

Sequence role: Map 2 of 9 in Vectors & Forces: From Direction to Dynamics. The learner arrives from VectorFoundations carrying magnitude, direction, addition, and subtraction. This map extends that vocabulary with the two fundamental products — dot (scalar output measuring agreement) and cross (vector output measuring perpendicularity). These operations are load-bearing: dot product drives work and energy calculations later in the forces maps, cross product drives torque in VectorAdvanced, and both are essential for the turret targeting in VectorApplied. The map feeds into VectorApplied (map 3), where these tools get real jobs. In QFEP terms, dot and cross products are the measurement operators that extract specific information from the state vector S.

Technical angle: VectorDotProduct visualizes A dot B = |A||B|cos(theta) interactively — the learner drags two vectors and watches the scalar product change sign as the angle crosses 90 degrees. dot_product_projector casts one vector's shadow onto another, making the geometric meaning of projection (the component of A along B) visible as an actual shorter arrow. VectorCrossProduct demonstrates A x B producing a third vector perpendicular to both, with the right-hand rule determining direction. vector_projection_demo decomposes a vector into parallel and perpendicular components relative to an arbitrary direction. normal_force_demo applies this decomposition to physics: gravity on a tilted surface splits into F_normal (what the surface resists) and F_parallel (what makes you slide), with surface angle as the critical parameter — steeper means more sliding.

Critical angle: The dot product is a question asked of two vectors: how much do you agree? Positive means roughly aligned, zero means perpendicular, negative means opposed. The cross product asks a different question: what direction is perpendicular to both of you, and how strongly? These are not arbitrary operations — they are the only two bilinear products that produce geometrically meaningful outputs from pairs of 3D vectors. The QFEP connection runs through measurement: just as QFEP's formalism extracts observable quantities from system states, the dot product extracts the projection (a measurement of alignment) and the cross product extracts the normal (a measurement of perpendicularity). The normal_force_demo makes this concrete — when gravity meets a surface, the projection operation literally determines what you feel. The surface pushes perpendicular to itself, never upward; gravity does the rest. This decomposition is not a mathematical convenience but a physical fact, and it is the dot product that reveals it. The sequence truth that "acceleration is the only thing you feel" gets its first formal tool here: projection tells you which component of force produces the acceleration you experience.
