# What the court refuses to count

Euler's abstraction is powerful because it discards almost everything about Königsberg except connection. Streets, labor, ownership, weather, policing, accessibility, and the river's material life disappear so that degree can become visible. The court makes one of those exclusions explicit: a museum needs access routes that the theorem must not count.

The two dark tongues are therefore both practical and argumentative. They let a body enter the exhibit while refusing the easy fiction that every piece of walkable infrastructure belongs to the graph under study. The seven stone bridges remain the mathematical object; the access tongues disclose the institution that stages it.

The raised perimeter also changes who is allowed to know the result. A visitor can read all four red odd-degree landmasses from the outside before attempting the walk, or enter and discover the failure through movement. Neither mode is treated as more authentic. The court supports overview and embodiment without shrinking the world to a tabletop model.

What remains unresolved is historical specificity. The artifact names landmasses and bridges, but it still renders a clean computational city rather than the contested social geography of eighteenth-century Königsberg. The dedicated site gives that question enough room to be addressed later; it does not pretend the spatial upgrade has answered it.
