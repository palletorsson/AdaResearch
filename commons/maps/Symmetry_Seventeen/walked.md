## The walk

The census closes. The `wall_pattern_gallery` paints the walls with wallpaper-group tilings, and the map's whole tutorial claim is a number: there are exactly **seventeen** ways a flat pattern can repeat. Not roughly seventeen, not seventeen-so-far — seventeen, provably, the complete list, no eighteenth possible. Walk the gallery and you are walking the entire space of plane symmetry; every wallpaper that has ever existed or ever will is one of these. The `loom_alhambra_p6m` and `mill_alhambra_p6m` weave and turn the richest of them, p6m, the sixfold group the Alhambra's artisans used most. Then the room hands you the tools: at the `vr_tile_editor` you build your own pattern, and the `pattern_tile_puzzle` asks you to *classify* it — to say which of the seventeen you made. That naming is the proof of the whole chapter: you can look at any repeating pattern in the world and place it, with nothing left over. And between the census and the exit, the room ends in the makers' wing: the `pattern_atlas_gallery`, thirteen plates where the world's pattern traditions run as shaders — Kente strips, ikat's dyed-in blur, bingata, kanga, shibori, the tiger rug, Iznik, zellige, the meander, Albers, Delaunay, Vasarely, Riley — each with its makers named on the tag beneath. You do not leave this chapter past a wall of mathematics. You leave past the people.

## The turn (critical)

This is the one classification in the entire book that **finishes**, and the map is built to make you feel that as the rare, almost shocking thing it is. Everywhere else the curriculum has taught the uncountable and the unclosable — the fractal dimension that refuses a whole number, the Cantor dust of measure zero, the Mandelbrot boundary that never resolves, and, at the far end, Gödel's sentence that no system can close. Ada is a book about the open census, the list that never ends. And here, alone, the list *ends*: seventeen, complete, catalogued, done. Closure is the exception that makes the rest of the book's openness legible — you cannot feel what it means for dimension to refuse the integers until you have stood once in a room where the count actually closes. The Alhambra is the turn's crown, and it is a genuine historical astonishment: the tile-cutters and plasterers of al-Andalus — Muslim craftworkers forbidden figural images and pouring that constraint into geometry — **exhausted all seventeen groups by hand centuries before group theory existed to prove there were only seventeen.** Name them as the makers they were, not a period style: master *alarifes* and their workshops, the zellige-cutters who scored and snapped each glazed tile, the carvers of the stucco *sebka* — a guild of hands, almost none of whose names survive on the walls their mathematics finished. They did not know the theorem. They *completed* it — walked the entire space of plane symmetry with tile and compass, proof-without-theorem, mathematics done as devotion and as day-labor. When the mathematicians finally proved the list was complete, they were confirming what the workers of Granada had already built, and the honest thing this map can do — the return the ledger asks for — is to say that the one census in the whole book that closes was closed by anonymous craftspeople, and to stop calling that nobody. The census closes, and the people who closed it first were praying, and cutting tile, and are owed the theorem's other half of the credit.
