# The thread and the work

Sadie Plant's *Zeros + Ones: Digital Women + the New Technoculture* (1997) belongs beside this loom. Read its account of women and technological culture with Palle Torsson's *Mamma Mönster* (2022): a mother's textile practice, the labour of making, a child's patterned room, and the wish that different clothes might change how people live. [Book and edition record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Zeros_%2B_Ones.html?id=AEi0AAAAIAAJ).

For Ada, the productive question is whose work becomes visible when a pattern is called a program. This is our use of Plant, not a claim that a shared grid proves textile work and computation identical, or that technical capacities belong naturally to one gender. A precise quotation from Plant should be checked against its page and edition before publication; none is invented here.

The references to *Mamma Mönster* in Foundry, Array Patterns and the final garment hall draw on Palle Torsson's supplied manuscript, dated Vamlingbo, July 2022. Foundry returns to its account of the hope behind Kristina Torsson's soft, colourful clothing. The corridor passage follows the tennis-court work: Mimmi Torsson's drawings, experiments with stencils and paint, metal templates made locally, and a change of windbreak material because of weight. The garment passage returns to the author's description of his room filled with fabrics, mirrors, colours and reflections. These are paraphrases of that account, not newly invented memories.

That material history does not establish that every textile is woven, that all handwork follows one structure, or that changing a garment necessarily changes its wearer. It gives the sequence a situated desire and a record of work. The museum can put some of those questions within reach while leaving their consequences open.

Printing, weaving and knitting also need separate descriptions. Printing places colour on an existing textile. Weaving interlaces warp and weft. Knitting forms fabric through interlocking loops. The same textile may combine processes. The [V&A's weave analysis](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/projects/project-interwoven-studying-textiles-from-the-outside-in) and [knitting introduction](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/museum-life/how-things-are-made-knitting) make the structural distinction clear.

The museum's hand loom and card-controlled loom record over/under relationships as geometry. Its older pattern machines render images. Neither operation by itself simulates the tension and friction of yarn. That limit gives us a specific next question, rather than an invitation to treat every attractive patterned surface as woven cloth.

The Jacquard encounter uses a documented mechanism: successive punched cards select the warp lifts through which a shuttle carries the weft. The Science Museum Group describes this operation in its [ribbon loom with Jacquard head](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8404795/ribbon-loom-with-jacquard-head). Its [card collection](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co471243/jacquard-cards) also records cards joined with string and examples kept with the ribbon they produced. These sources support the connection between a removable instruction and its woven result; they do not establish that every textile practice is an early computer.

Ada's eight-card loop, twelve hooks and repeated 96-thread width are a teaching construction. Cards choose lifts; independent warp and weft sequences choose colours. The tartan is an authored study, not a named historical sett. Opening its layers is an inspection transform of the same recorded crossings. Card-chain tension and yarn mechanics are not implemented. SAVE CLOTH now transfers the committed crossing and colour record to the garment hall's cutting table. Its suit uses a texture derived from that record on ten prescribed panels; it does not simulate cutting individual yarns, seams under tension or cloth drape. The running pick takes its snapshot at the start of the cycle, before the animated card seats; later edits apply on a future reading.

In the tutorial, the looms are an optional investigation of stored choices and retained output. The core reconstruction remains the composed glide reader. In the reflection, the recurring card and growing cloth let us ask where work survives a changed instruction. That is Ada's interpretation of its own encounter, distinct from a claim about historical workers' experience.

The seven selectable samples extend this comparison. Stripes and checks share plain weave; tartan and houndstooth share 2/2 twill with different yarn-colour sequences. Chevron and herringbone keep the colours and change the reversing lift draft. These are bounded teaching constructions. For the structural vocabulary, see [CottonWorks, Weaving 101](https://cottonworks.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Weaving-101.pdf); the four-dark/four-light houndstooth study is also documented in [Handwoven, November/December 2013](https://www.datocms-assets.com/75077/1687814092-hwnd13.pdf).

“Optical checks” is Ada's descriptive working title, not a verified historical or catalogue name. It responds to the author's supplied textile photographs with graduated burgundy/cream bands in 2/2 twill. The reference has stronger complementary light/dark blocks; this related construction has a gentler optical effect. We have not established the original draft or attributed that textile to a manufacturer from the accompanying social-media screenshot.

In the model, the band widths are 8, 5, 3, 2, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5 and 8 threads. That 38-step colour sequence repeats independently of the twill lift. The closed surface has only its existing crossing crimp; opening deliberately raises successive picks. Apparent relief, actual yarn crossings and the inspection transform are three different things. The [native evidence](../../../doc/book/iterations/2026-10-10-optical-woven-checks/index.html) checks the recorded construction and controls. It does not establish how strongly any visitor will experience an illusion.
