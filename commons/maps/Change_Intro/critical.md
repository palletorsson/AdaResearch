# Between the samples

At x=1, the half-unit finite difference is negative while the local derivative is positive. The curve is rising at the selected point, but the next sample lies beyond a crest. The disagreement is inspectable. A pair of accurate endpoint values has left a change of direction out of the report.

This is a specific limit of the measurement, rather than evidence that the curve has become unknowable. Narrowing the interval exposes another relation. The derivative answers a local question; it does not restore the entire path discarded by an endpoint summary. A different task may need that path, its extrema, or an accumulated quantity.

The near-zero label also has an implementation: |df/dx| < 0.05. It is useful for a legible sign display, but it neither identifies an exact stationary point nor classifies the surrounding shape. Naming the threshold lets a student question the interface instead of treating its category as the curve's own declaration.

The body question lands at the boundary between this plot and movement. A height field could be sampled for incline; a controller could use that incline to allow, slow or refuse a crossing. This primary does not implement that controller. Keeping the distinction visible prevents a mathematical representation from silently standing in for the bodily permissions it might later govern.

## Zero is an answer to a question

The bridge rises and falls; its endpoint height difference is zero. Integrating the signed rate recovers that difference while cancelling the climb against the descent. An account of net change can be correct while omitting the difficulty of the route. Adding rate magnitudes would answer another question. Neither should silently become the universal account of a journey.

A derivative also leaves the starting height undetermined. The same slopes can occupy different heights, and those placements may admit different crossings for a body.
