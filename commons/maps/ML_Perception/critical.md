# ML_Perception — Critical Reflection

A gallery. Open center, three alcoves cut into walls, each framing an artifact like a painting in a museum. Computer vision in one alcove. Convolutional neural networks in another. The third alcove is structurally present but empty in the interactables layer. The gallery format is the map's thesis: perception is something you observe from a distance. The gallery format is also the map's blind spot: perception is the one thing you cannot observe from a distance, because observation is already perception.

Hayles describes how computational media restructure what it means to see. The CNN artifact is the literal restructuring: a small window slides across an image, multiplying and summing, extracting edges, textures, shapes. Each layer sees more abstraction. The convolution operation treats every region of the image with the same filter — translation invariance — which means the network assumes that a feature meaningful in one location is meaningful in every location. A face in the corner and a face in the center are treated identically. A tumor in a scan and a scanner artifact are processed by the same weights. The CNN does not know where things are; it only knows what things look like. Spatial meaning is the first casualty of convolutional perception.

The computer vision artifact sits in the opposite alcove, and the spatial arrangement creates a dialogue across the gallery's open center. A pixel is a number. A grid of pixels is a matrix. Vision starts there — raw arithmetic on light. This is Hayles' informational ontology made literal: the image is data before it is image, numbers before it is scene, computation before it is perception. The artifact normalizes the transformation from visual experience to numerical representation as though it were costless. But the transformation is where everything happens. The choice of resolution determines what can be seen. The choice of color space determines what can be distinguished. The sensor's dynamic range determines what is light and what is darkness. Machine perception begins with hardware decisions that foreclose entire categories of visual experience before the first pixel is processed.

Chun writes about the face as the paradigmatic object of computational vision — the site where recognition, identification, and surveillance converge. The gallery layout aestheticizes this convergence. The CNN learns to detect edges, then textures, then objects, and the progression is presented as increasing sophistication. But the face is not a neutral test case. Facial recognition systems trained on unbalanced datasets produce unequal error rates across demographic groups. The features the network learns to detect are features of the training distribution, not features of the human face. The CNN's learned filters encode the demographics of its dataset as the geometry of its feature detectors. The gallery presents this as technical achievement without presenting the misidentification rates.

The blurb mentions attention — "Instead of scanning left-to-right, the model asks: what matters here?" — but no attention artifact occupies the third alcove. This absence is structurally significant. Attention mechanisms are the dominant paradigm in contemporary machine perception, and their absence creates a gallery frozen in the convolutional era. Attention would complicate the gallery's clean narrative because it introduces a computational gaze — a learned decision about where to look before deciding what to see. Attention is perception with an agenda, and the agenda is learned from data, which means the model's gaze is shaped by the distribution of gazes in its training set. The absent alcove is the gallery's most honest wall: a blank space where the politics of computational attention should hang.

The gallery format normalizes the separation between perceiver and perceived. You stand in the open center and look into alcoves. The artifacts look back at you in the sense that they process visual input and produce representations. But the gallery maintains a safe distance. You are not the data being classified. You are the viewer appreciating the classification. Hayles would note that this separation is the foundational gesture of Western epistemology — the subject who observes without being changed by observation — and that computational perception dissolves it. Every camera pointed at a face is also a classification system pointed at a person. Every perceptual model is also a surveillance architecture. The gallery refuses this collapse. It keeps you in the center, safely observing.

Perception is compression with an agenda. The CNN compresses an image into features. The features compress a scene into categories. The categories compress a world into decisions. At every stage, something is discarded to make the remainder tractable, and the discarding is guided by the loss function's definition of what matters. The gallery invites you to see how machines learn to see. What it does not invite is the harder question: what do machines learn not to see, and whose invisibility is the cost of their clarity?
