# BG_Crack_Propagation — Critical Reflection

Stress accumulates invisibly. The material holds — until it doesn't. The crackpropagation_ca artifact runs a cellular automaton on a 2D stress grid: cells crack when local stress exceeds a threshold, propagating fracture to their neighbors. The @identity block names the desire plainly: "to watch destruction emerge from accumulation." The critical parameter is CRACK_THRESHOLD — the breaking point. Lower values create dense shatter patterns. Higher values yield sparse, clean fractures. The parameter decides how much a material can bear before it fails.

Simondon's individuation never considered failure as a mode of becoming, but it should. A crack is an individuation event. The intact material is metastable — it holds stress in a distributed field, no single point bearing more than it can manage. When the threshold is exceeded at one point, that point individuates as fracture, and the individuation propagates through the material along stress gradients and grain boundaries. The crack is not the opposite of growth. It is growth's dark twin — structure emerging through rupture rather than accretion, the material discovering its internal topology by breaking along it.

N. Katherine Hayles's concept of material metaphor — where the physical form of a text participates in its meaning — finds literal expression here. The crack pattern is the material's autobiography. Grain orientation, stress concentration, boundary conditions: the material's history is written in its failure lines. Every fracture is a sentence in a language the material speaks only when it breaks. The simulation mounted on the wall in this compact gallery is a reading device. It makes legible what was always there but invisible — the internal stresses, the hidden weaknesses, the fault lines that precede the fault.

What does this map normalize? That destruction is informative. That failure reveals structure. That the most honest map of a system is the one drawn by its collapse. This is a powerful and seductive claim. Forensic engineering depends on it — read the fracture pattern, deduce the failure mode, assign causation. But the normalization runs deeper: it suggests that systems are best understood at their breaking point, that the truth of a material lies in how it fails rather than how it holds.

What does it exclude? The possibility that the material's truth lies in its endurance. The cells that did not crack, the regions that absorbed stress and redistributed it, the grain orientations that channeled force away from vulnerable boundaries — these are invisible in the fracture pattern. The simulation shows only what broke. Resilience has no representation. The intact portion of the grid is rendered as background — featureless, uninteresting, merely the space where nothing happened. But nothing happening is itself a feat of distributed load-bearing. Stability is not absence of information; it is information of a different kind.

Simondon's concept of the transindividual — the collective that exceeds any individual's capacity — appears in the stress distribution before fracture. Each cell in the grid bears a portion of the total stress. No single cell is responsible for the whole. The load is transindividual: distributed, shared, mutual. The crack destroys this commons. It concentrates stress at the fracture tip, forces neighboring cells to bear more than their share, and propagates through the system by making each cell's failure the next cell's overload. The crack is the privatization of stress — the moment when distributed burden becomes concentrated collapse.

The compact gallery frames this as wall-mounted display — close viewing, intimate scale. You stand near enough to see individual cells fracture. The architecture says: this is something to inspect, not something to inhabit. You are not the material. You are the engineer reading the failure report. But Hayles would insist that reading is never neutral — that the interface shapes what can be thought. The wall-mounted display flattens crack propagation into spectacle. The three-dimensional stress field that produced the fracture is compressed into a 2D image. Depth is lost. The crack's path through the material's thickness — the most structurally informative dimension — is invisible.

Cracks do not break rules. They reveal where the rules were already breaking. But who set the threshold?
