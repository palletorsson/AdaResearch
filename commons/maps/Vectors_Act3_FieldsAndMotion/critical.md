# A rule needs a receiver

The room begins with two markers and a difference in what they do at the centre. Only afterward do we uncover the two assignments producing that difference. This makes the implementation available as an argument: sharing a field does not entail sharing a path.

The field alone cannot explain either body's movement. Cyan replaces its velocity with a sample; pink retains its existing velocity and adds a new contribution. Both carry a position. Only one carries the earlier velocity into the next update. Calling one obedient and the other free would be too quick: both follow exact rules, and the difference is a specific capacity that one receiver has been given.

For the research question, what bodies are possible, this is a useful experiment. An inward rule can permit approach or repeated crossing. A swirling rule can produce different paths depending on how it is received. The vector's visible shape is insufficient to determine the body's future. The apparatus lets us locate a difference in code without reducing all differences to one universal explanation.

Reverse the field while the bodies are moving. Pink does not immediately reverse; its accumulated velocity must be changed over time. This carries the previous chapter's unfinished influence into a spatial relation. The next instruction meets a past, even when the trace has discarded that past's early samples.

The visitor occupies the same visible space but is not a receiver of this field. The markers pass through the controls; their two heights are display choices for one planar calculation. The gold perimeter freezes observations rather than responding with contact. These seams distinguish the simulation from the museum enclosing it. They are concrete places to ask what would change if another body were admitted to the rule.

Neither overshoot nor a complicated trail measures freedom or entropy here. No QFEP threshold is computed. The contribution is methodological: vary the coupling between a spatial rule and a state, watch which forms of movement become possible, and keep the omissions inspectable. The next force room must make an equally explicit account of the bodies its forces actually reach.
