## What this map holds (seed)

Three points close a boundary, and now: rigidity. The triangle does not flex without breaking. Three distances mutually constrain three angles; move one vertex, the others resist. This is where measurement stabilizes, where the Pythagorean theorem first has somewhere to live.

`interactivetriangle` lets you drag a vertex and feel the constraint. `pythagorean_triangle_angles` makes the algebra visible. The `folded_strip` shows how quads decompose into paired triangles, flexible where the triangle is fixed. Rigidity and flexibility are competing spatial ethics. The triangle chose: no deformation without rupture.

## Why it was built (seed)

Concept: The triangle as first rigid relational structure — three points mutually constrained produce invariant measurements. Where the Pythagorean theorem first resides, and quads emerge by decomposition.
Sequence role: Sixth map. Deepens closure into structural rigidity; the triangle becomes a constraint system, and quads arrive as paired triangles bridging toward polyhedra.
Critical angle: Rigidity as geometric ideology; the Pythagorean theorem as the West's foundational geometric claim; rigidity vs flexibility as competing spatial ethics.

## The walk

Grab a vertex of the `interactivetriangle` and pull. This is the sensation the whole map is built to deliver: the other two vertices **fight back**. Three points, once joined, are no longer three free things — they are a single constrained system, three distances that mutually decide three angles, and you cannot move one without the others resisting. It is the first time in the sequence that the geometry *pushes against your hand*. Where the point was a free decision and the line a free connection, the triangle is the first form that has a will of its own.

> **Dwell — `interactivetriangle` · ~55s**
>
> Keep pulling, and notice what does not happen. The square next door would
> have sheared — push one corner of a four-bar frame and it slides into a
> parallelogram without a single edge stretching. Nothing here slides. The
> triangle is the minimal rigid polygon; that is not a metaphor but a theorem
> in structural mechanics, and it is why bridges, trusses, and geodesic domes
> are triangles all the way through. It is also why the GPU speaks nothing
> else: three points define exactly one plane, four do not, so every surface
> you will ever see rendered decomposes into this form. What your hand is
> feeling is the same property twice — structural enforcement and
> computational guarantee. And enforcement is the honest word. Rigid
> structures resist change, maintain form, hold position: predictability,
> stability, control — bought with inflexibility, brittleness, breaking
> instead of bending. And the constraint logic generalizes. Rules are
> enforced; violations are impossible; outcomes are determined; freedom is
> bounded. Swap the geometry out and the grammar keeps working: legal
> systems, immigration criteria, property, type systems. The triangle is
> where you first feel, in your fingers, how a formal system rules — not by
> punishing the forbidden configuration but by making it impossible to hold.
>

`pythagorean_triangle_angles` lifts the constraint into the algebra you can read — one right angle, the other two forced to sum to its complement, the theorem given a body to live in. Then `folded_strip` shows the alternative ethic: fold a quad and watch it *flex*, decomposing into paired triangles, rigid along the shared edge and loose across the fold. `quad` and `quad_line_puzzle` let you feel the trade directly — the triangle cannot deform without rupture; the quad deforms rather than break. Two spatial ethics, held in your two hands.

> **Dwell — `pythagorean_triangle_angles` · ~55s**
>
> The theorem is a statement about area, and this station insists on it.
> Three squares grow live from the triangle's three sides; drag a vertex and
> they swell and shrink together, the interior angles redrawn as arcs at
> every corner. When one angle hits exactly ninety degrees, the two smaller
> squares together fill precisely the area of the large one — nine plus
> sixteen makes twenty-five, not stated but shown, made visually undeniable.
> That undeniability is the thing to dwell on. Geometry is working here as a
> truth-making apparatus: given two sides, the third is required — no choice,
> no variation, no negotiation. In the right triangle the relationship is
> law, and from that law trigonometry, coordinate systems, and every 3D
> distance you will ever compute all follow. But ask what the apparatus
> leaves in shadow. Non-right triangles have their relationships too — the
> law of cosines, the law of sines — and they get no visual proofs in
> elementary education. The right triangle is privileged as the most
> important triangle, and the privileging disappears into the certainty it
> produces. Constraint reduces uncertainty by excluding forms: that is the
> trade. The theorem feels discovered. The choice of which triangle gets to
> carry the truth was made.
>

> **Dwell — `quad` · ~55s**
>
> Rotate the quad and watch the colors betray it. Four colors on what claims
> to be one surface — because the quad is never whole. Every rectangular face
> you will ever model is secretly two triangles, split by a diagonal you did
> not draw: here it runs corner zero to corner two, and the alternative
> diagonal would have produced different triangles, different normals,
> different shading. Which diagonal? You don't decide. This is the first
> geometry in the curriculum where representation diverges from
> implementation — what you see is not what runs — and it prefigures the
> whole of computational culture: high-level code compiling to machine
> instructions, a button click triggering a hundred hidden functions. Now
> pull one corner off the plane. The fiction dissolves in your hand:
> triangle A stays in one plane, triangle B in another, and the surface
> creases — the first geometry that can fail to be what it claims. The quad
> survives as diplomacy: modelers want quads, hardware wants triangles, and a
> translation layer negotiates whose convenience wins. Convenience
> abstractions obscure underlying execution. Hold the twisted quad a moment
> longer and ask, of every clean interface you will meet from here on: where
> is the diagonal, and who chose it?

## The turn (critical)

Rigidity is not neutral, and this map is where the sequence first shows that a shape can carry an **ideology**. The triangle "chose": no deformation without rupture. It is the form of the truss, the bridge, the frame that will not yield — stability purchased by the refusal to bend, and a structure that answers stress by holding until it *breaks* rather than adapting. The quad chose the opposite: it bends, it flexes, it survives deformation by giving. Set side by side these are not just engineering options but competing pictures of what strength *is* — the rigid ideal that equates integrity with unyieldingness, and the flexible one that equates it with the capacity to change shape and not shatter. The book knows which one it finds more livable, and it lets you feel the argument in your hands rather than making it in a sentence.

And the map plants a flag the whole later curriculum will return to: the Pythagorean theorem as **the West's foundational geometric claim**, the moment measurement became certainty. That certainty is exactly what `foundationscrisis` will come back to break. Here it is offered at its most seductive — a relation so clean it feels discovered rather than chosen — precisely so that its later crisis lands as a loss and not a technicality. The triangle is where geometry first says *this is necessarily so*, and the book files that "necessarily" away for later.
