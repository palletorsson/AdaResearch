Concept: A surface made of springs — stretch, shear, and structural constraints define the three force types that turn a flat grid of mass points into convincing fabric. The tall vertical room gives cloth room to drape, fold, and cascade under gravity, revealing how spring topology determines material character.
Sequence role: Fifth map in Soft Bodies (13th spine sequence, integration phase), intermediate difficulty. After the gallery maps established general soft body-obstacle interaction, this map narrows focus to cloth specifically — the 2D manifold case of spring-mass simulation. The intermediate difficulty reflects the added complexity of three distinct spring types. Connects forward to SoftBodies_Playground_of_Joy where all soft body types combine in free play; follows SoftBodies_Obsticals_Part2.
Technical angle: Cloth as a spring-mass grid with three spring categories: structural springs (horizontal/vertical, resist stretching), shear springs (diagonal, resist skewing), and bend springs (skip-one connections, resist folding). Verlet integration for position-based dynamics. Self-collision detection to prevent the cloth from passing through itself. The tall vertical room emphasizes gravity's role — longer drape distances amplify the visual difference between spring configurations.
Critical angle: Cloth simulation reveals that material behavior is topological before it is physical — the same mass points with different spring connectivity produce silk, canvas, or rubber sheet. This is the integration phase argument at its sharpest: form emerges not from the elements but from the relations between elements. The three spring types map onto structural, shear, and bending resistance — the same triad that governs real textile engineering.
Key artifacts: softstopscene demonstrates cloth draping, collision, and constraint behavior in the tall vertical room, likely including fixed attachment points and obstacle interaction.
Gap: Needs a spring-type isolation tool — an artifact that lets the learner toggle structural, shear, and bend springs independently, seeing how cloth behaves with only one or two of the three active, making the contribution of each spring type visually separable.
