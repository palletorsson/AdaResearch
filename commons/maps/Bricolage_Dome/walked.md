# Bricolage_Dome — walked

> ghost-drafted from the working map (close slot, bricolage thread); Palle rules the voice.

## The cast

icosahedron_base · subdivision_demo · strut_inventory · dome_builder · geodesic_dome · dark_sphere

## The walk

The chapter closes on its most finished object and its most surprising claim — that this one was not built by hand at all. The icosahedron_base stands as the seed, twenty faces, the closest a regular solid gets to a sphere. The subdivision_demo shows one face becoming four, then nine, the corners pushed out to the sphere each time. The strut_inventory racks the result: hundreds of edges, only a handful of distinct lengths. The dome_builder raises the base band while the rest still lies as stock. And in the centre, raised above the room, the geodesic_dome itself — a hemisphere of triangles standing on its ring.

## The close

The seed to the sequence's whole argument. Everything before this was bricolage as improvisation — parts at hand, arrangements tried, constraints met by feel. The dome is bricolage *formalised*: nobody chose its curve, someone chose a seed solid and a subdivision frequency, and the sphere did the rest. It is the triangulation lesson from the constraints room repeated over a whole surface — every quad would hinge, every triangle cannot — and it is the moment recombination stops being a knack and becomes a *method*, one you can write down and hand to someone else. The chapter that opened by refusing the engineer's blueprint ends by discovering that the bricoleur, working long enough with parts and constraints, arrives at something the engineer would recognise: a procedure. Construction without a blueprint, followed to its end, writes the blueprint. That is the sequence's quiet twist, and its truest picture of how anything gets made — from the pile, through the constraint, to the method, and only then to the drawing.
