# ML_Classification — Critical Reflection

Three zones at three heights, separated by wall ridges. Zone A holds k-means. Zone B holds SVM. Zone C holds random forest. The ridges between them are the decision boundaries made architectural — the places where one category ends and another begins. Walk the narrow passes. The map puts classification in your body before it puts it in your head: to cross from one zone to another, you must navigate a boundary that someone built.

K-means drops centroids and iterates. Points migrate toward their nearest center. Clusters crystallize from unlabeled chaos, and the critical parameter is `K` — how many clusters to impose. The artifact lets you watch colored regions form, centroids drifting to their resting places, the algorithm converging on a partition. What it normalizes is the assumption that partition is the goal. K-means does not discover clusters; it imposes them. Set K=3 and there will be three clusters, whether the data contains three natural groupings or not. The number of categories precedes the analysis. Chun's analysis of software as ideology finds its clearest expression here: the algorithm presents categorization as discovery while the most consequential decision — how many kinds of thing exist — was made before the first iteration.

The SVM visualizer sits in the adjacent zone, separated by a wall ridge you must cross. Its identity is purity: `max margin: min ||w||^2 s.t. yi(w*xi + b) >= 1`. The maximum-margin hyperplane crystallizes between classes, support vectors glowing gold at the boundary. The critical parameter is `C` — regularization. Low C allows misclassification for a wider margin. High C demands perfection.

This is a trade-off between tolerance and precision, and the artifact frames it as a technical dial. But Chun would notice that the trade-off is also ethical: how many individuals on the wrong side of the boundary are acceptable? How much misclassification is the system willing to tolerate, and who decides? The margin is the space between the classes, and widening it requires accepting that some points will be sacrificed. The support vectors — the few points that define the boundary — bear the entire weight of the classification. Everyone else is irrelevant. A system where the boundary cases determine the rule and everyone else is noise is a particular political arrangement presented as geometric optimization.

Random forest occupies the third zone, the furthest from spawn, reached through the most constrained passage. Its identity is democratic: `prediction = majority_vote(tree_1(x), tree_2(x), ..., tree_n(x))`, each tree trained on a bootstrap sample with random feature subsets. A forest of weak learners voting their way to strong predictions. Hayles would find the posthuman condition here — not in any single tree's intelligence but in the aggregate. No individual tree is accurate. The collective is. Intelligence as a property of the ensemble rather than the component. The critical parameter is `num_trees`, and more trees reduce variance through the wisdom-of-crowds effect. The forest normalizes a form of knowledge production where error is not eliminated but averaged out, where the individual is wrong but the system is right. This is a statistical epistemology: truth as the central tendency of many biased observations.

The three zones create a spatial argument. You begin in k-means territory (unsupervised, centroid-based), cross a ridge to SVM territory (supervised, margin-based), then cross another ridge to random forest territory (ensemble-based, vote-based). The progression suggests evolution — from simple to sophisticated, from geometry to democracy. But this progression is authored. The map places the artifacts in an order that tells a story about classification getting better, when what is actually happening is classification getting differently wrong. K-means misclassifies by assuming spherical clusters. SVM misclassifies by assuming linear separability (before the kernel trick). Random forest misclassifies by inheriting the biases of its training data across every tree. The forest does not escape the data's politics; it distributes them across a hundred crooked copies.

Hayles writes about how computational media produce the illusion of dematerialization — stripping away the body to leave pure information. Classification is this operation formalized. A data point has features. The classifier reads the features. The features are numbers. The numbers define a position in feature space. Everything about the data point that cannot be expressed as a feature coordinate vanishes. The map's intent document notes the gap: "Needs a feature-selection artifact showing how the same data produces radically different boundaries depending on which two features are chosen as axes." That absent artifact is the map's blind spot made visible. The decision boundary depends entirely on which features are measured, and the choice of features is the act of deciding what matters about a thing before asking what kind of thing it is.

The narrow passes between zones are the most honest part of the map. Decision boundaries are not walls you can see or ridges you can measure. They are thresholds in abstract space, defined by mathematics that has no width, no height, no friction. But you experience them as physical constraints — tight passages between tall walls — because the map insists that classification has a body. The boundary is where the algorithm is most uncertain and most consequential. The pass is where you are most constrained and most aware of the architecture. Every classifier draws a line and calls it knowledge. The question that lingers in the narrow passes is whose hand held the pen.
