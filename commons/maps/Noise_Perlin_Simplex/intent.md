Concept: Two algorithms for the same problem — Perlin noise (1983) and Simplex noise (2001) compared side by side. Perlin interpolates gradients on a hypercubic grid; Simplex uses a triangular (simplicial) grid. Eighteen years of algorithmic refinement visible in the artifacts each method leaves behind.
Sequence role: Eighth map in the Noise sequence; the algorithmic comparison that grounds the sequence's practical knowledge in implementation history. After seven maps of using noise, this map asks how noise is made. The configurable_portal connects this map to other sequences, positioning it as a crossroads. Connects backward to every prior map (all used Perlin or Simplex implicitly) by making the underlying algorithms explicit; connects forward to Lab_Path by completing the technical curriculum before the threshold exit; follows Noise_Space_10.
Technical angle: Perlin noise — gradient vectors assigned at integer lattice points, dot product with distance vector, smoothstep interpolation across the hypercube. Axis-aligned artifacts from the cubic grid visible as directional bias. Simplex noise — simplicial grid (triangles in 2D, tetrahedra in 3D) via skewing transform, fewer gradient evaluations per point (N+1 versus 2^N corners), no axis-aligned artifacts. The noise_terrain and perlin_noise_terrain artifacts show identical parameter configurations rendered through both algorithms; simplex_noise and perlin_noise isolate the raw function outputs for direct comparison.
Critical angle: Perlin noise carries the grid that made it — axis-aligned artifacts are the signature of the hypercubic lattice leaking through the interpolation. Simplex noise erases its scaffolding more thoroughly, but at the cost of patent restrictions (until 2022) and implementation complexity. The history matters: Perlin invented gradient noise for Tron (1982), then spent two decades refining it. Simplex is not a replacement but an evolution — same principle (local gradients, smooth interpolation), different geometry (cubes to simplices). The comparison teaches that algorithms are not neutral: their internal geometry shapes their output, and artifacts are the visible trace of invisible structure.
