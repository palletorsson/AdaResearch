# The same everything else — what a comparison costs

Perlin noise and simplex noise solve the same problem: produce smooth, pseudo-random values that vary continuously across space. Both were invented by Ken Perlin, eighteen years apart. The first hangs random gradients on a square lattice; the second on a triangular one. The difference looks cosmetic. The consequences are structural: a lattice signs everything built on it, and in Perlin's case the artifacts travel with the grid, not with the geometry. Whether a visitor can see that signature at four octaves and half-metre cubes is exactly the kind of question this room now lets them put to the fields instead of to their names.

For a year the room could not. Two displays stood side by side, one named Perlin, one named Simplex, and both drew simplex noise. The visualizer chose its basis in a match statement whose default was simplex, and the scene named Perlin was never handed the word. Nothing on the display could tell you; the name was the only evidence, and the name was wrong. This is the ordinary condition of a comparison in a museum of algorithms: a title is a claim, and the claim is checked, if at all, by reading code the visitor cannot see.

The 2026-09-10 repair is small and it is the whole lesson. The Perlin display is placed with `generator:perlin`. Both displays are given the same seed, the same octave count, the same frequency and gain, the same field size, and one shared colour ramp. Each gets a reachable panel and a readout that reports the basis back from the generator — not from the scene — together with the seed, the parameters, and the field's value at two fixed coordinates. Two readouts, differing in one word and two numbers. That sentence cost four things held still, and the holding is the argument: a difference can be attributed to a rule only after everything that is not the rule has been named and fixed.

What the room refuses to promise is correspondence. The same integer seeds two generators into repeatable states, but the states are unrelated, and no ridge on the left has a partner on the right. Repeatability is per basis: press REPLAY and each field returns to itself. Comparison is of character — how variation turns, whether it leans along the room's axes, how the two values at one coordinate drift apart under one shared dial — and it is made by the visitor with the dials, not asserted by the label.

Thomas Kuhn's account of refinement fits the pair better than a progress story does. Perlin and simplex are not a succession; the 1983 algorithm is still in use, and the 2001 algorithm did not displace it. Perlin's cost grows exponentially with dimension because a hypercube has 2^n corners; simplex's grows polynomially because a simplex has n+1. At two dimensions the difference is trivial. At four it decides. The choice between them is domain-specific, and a room that ranked them would be making the same mistake as the room that named them without checking.

The queer question here is about which variation was given a control. The panel offers frequency and octaves and withholds amplitude, seed by hand, and the animation offset the Perlin scene can run. Those are decisions about what a visitor is allowed to vary, and each of them narrows the space in which the two bases can be told apart. A field is not only a rule; it is a rule plus the conditions under which someone is permitted to look at it.

The learner leaves with both bases in their toolkit, with the readout's habit of reporting what a thing does rather than what it is called, and with a distinction to carry into the cells: a field is sampled at a coordinate, and a neighbourhood is updated from its stored states.
