Concept: Three-dimensional cellular automata — cells arranged in a volumetric lattice with 26 neighbors instead of 8, where local rules generate solid structures, cavities, and organic forms that can only be understood by moving through them.
Sequence role: Ninth map in the Cellular Automata sequence; the dimensional leap from 2D surfaces to 3D volumes. Beginner difficulty signals accessibility — the concept is spatially intuitive even though the combinatorics explode (26 neighbors, vastly larger rule tables). This map realizes what CA_6's 3D tree previewed: full volumetric automata. Connects backward to CA_8's topological generalizations (sphere) and forward to CA_10's agent-based approach (Langton's ant), shifting from field-like bulk dynamics to individual-level behavior.
Technical angle: 3D Moore neighborhood: 26 neighbors (3^3 - 1) surrounding each cell in a cubic lattice. Rule tables scale as k^(k^26) for k states — practically infinite, so most 3D CA use totalistic or threshold rules. Birth/survival notation extends naturally: B5-7/S4-6 means a cell is born with 5-7 live neighbors and survives with 4-6. The ca_chair_test artifact is a structural test piece — evaluating whether 3D CA rules can generate stable, furniture-scale geometries from local interactions alone.
Critical angle: Three dimensions transform CA from a screen phenomenon into an architectural one. Structures have interiors, load paths, and occlusion — you cannot see everything at once, and navigation becomes part of comprehension. This is the point where cellular automata meet spatial computing: VR and AR environments could be procedurally grown rather than manually designed. The 26-neighbor explosion also demonstrates why nature uses chemistry (continuous, quantum) rather than lattice automata — discrete 3D grids are computationally expensive.
Key artifacts: ca_chair_test evaluates whether 3D cellular automata rules can generate stable volumetric structures, serving as a benchmark for architectural-scale procedural generation.
Gap: Needs a cross-section slicer — an artifact that cuts through the 3D volume at adjustable planes, revealing the internal structure layer by layer so the learner can connect the 3D bulk behavior to the 2D slice dynamics they already understand.
