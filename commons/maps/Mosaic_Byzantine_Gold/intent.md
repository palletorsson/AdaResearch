Concept: Mosaic_Byzantine_Gold examines the gold-ground technique that defines Byzantine mosaic art — reflective tesserae set at deliberate angles so they catch light differently depending on where the viewer stands. The pattern is not fixed in the surface but exists in the interaction between surface, light source, and observer position. This map treats angled gold tesserae as a problem of directional reflection, where tessera tilt becomes a shader parameter and the image shifts with the viewer's movement through the space.

Technical angle: The core technique is shader-based simulation of angled reflective surfaces. Each gold tessera is modeled as a small planar reflector tilted at an angle from the wall surface. The reflection vector R = 2(N dot L)N - L (where N is the tessera normal and L is the light direction) determines which viewing positions receive specular highlights. By varying tessera tilt angles across the surface in a systematic pattern, Byzantine mosaicists created images that shimmer, shift, and reveal different details at different distances. The shader implementation maps tessera angle to a per-pixel normal offset, computes the specular reflection for the current camera position, and blends specular with a gold base color. The critical parameters are tessera tilt angle (controlling reflection direction), light source position (determining which tesserae are "active"), and camera distance (affecting the spatial frequency of the shimmer pattern). tiles_explained demonstrates how viewing angle and reflection interact to create pattern perception.

Critical angle: Gold-ground mosaics encode viewing distance into the pattern itself. A mosaic in a cathedral apse is designed to be seen from 20 meters below — the tessera angles are calibrated so the image resolves at that distance and dissolves into abstract shimmer up close. The algorithm changes with the observer's position. This is a physical instance of state-dependent computation: the "output" of the mosaic (the perceived image) is not stored in the surface but computed in real time by the interaction of surface normals, light, and eye position. The QFEP connection is direct — the sequence formula states "pattern = symmetry_group(motif, lattice) + material_noise(hand, stone, light)" and Byzantine mosaics make the light term dominant. The lambda parameter here lives not in hand-cut irregularity (as in Pompeii) but in the angular precision of tessera placement: a tessera tilted one degree off its intended angle redirects its reflection to the wrong viewing position, creating a flicker that no flat shader replicates. This is noise that carries information — the material signature of hand-setting thousands of gold squares at controlled angles, where the imperfection is not random but structured by the limits of the craftsman's hand. The sequence truth that "the tesserae are data" takes on a richer meaning here: each tessera is not just a color sample but a directional reflector encoding both color and viewing angle in its physical placement.
