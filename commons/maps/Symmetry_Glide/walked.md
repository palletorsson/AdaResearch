## The walk

The fourth and sneakiest symmetry: reflect, *then* slide. A glide is a mirror image shifted along its own axis, and its signature is that no mirror line survives to point at — the reflection is real, but the thing it reflects across has walked off. Footprints in snow are the everyday case: left, right, left, each a mirror of the last but moved forward, and there is no line down the middle you could fold the trail along. The `mill_escher_p4g` runs a group built on exactly this — off-centre mirrors that only work once you also translate — and the `loom_escher_mirror` weaves the Escher version, a monochrome tiling that interlocks against its own reflection. Walk the `pattern_tile_brick` and the `pattern_tile_herringbone`: masonry and parquet have obeyed the glide for centuries, each brick a shifted mirror of its neighbour, which is exactly why the joints never line up into a weak seam.

## The turn (critical)

The glide is the symmetry you cannot see until someone shows you, and the map's turn is about *who* showed it: **Escher, the outsider who taught the mathematicians to see their own theorem.** He was not a group theorist — he sat in the Alhambra with a sketchbook, copying the Islamic tilings by hand, obsessed with how the shapes interlocked and reflected and slid, and out of that copying came images so exactly organized that crystallographers used them to *teach the wallpaper groups*. The artist became the group theorist's instrument. That is a genuinely queer arrangement of authority, and worth naming — but if we are naming, the naming should not stop at Escher. He copied; someone *made* the thing he copied. The glides he traced into fame were laid by the zellige-cutters of Granada, the same anonymous al-Andalus tile-workers the seventeen-map credits, who had put the glide into the floor four hundred years before a Dutch draughtsman found it there. The formal knowledge did not descend from the mathematics into the art; it rose from the tiles — from the workers' hands, through a copyist's eye, into the proof — and the copyist got the monograph while the makers got "Moorish ornament." The glide is the perfect carrier for this because it is the symmetry that *hides* — present everywhere, nameable by almost no one, felt in every brick wall and never noticed — and there too it has makers worth naming, the bricklayers and masons and parquet-layers who set the half-offset course that *is* a glide and the herringbone that *is* two glides crossed, running the symmetry in load-bearing wall and swept floor for as long as there have been walls and floors, none of them told they were doing group theory and every one of them doing it, filed by history under "vernacular" the way the Granada tilers were filed under "ornament." Escher's gift was not invention; it was attention, held long enough on the Alhambra's floor that the theorem became visible — but attention is still a second act, and the first belonged to the workers whose floor it was. Hold the map's quiet claim, now doubled: some structures are seen first by the people who love them enough to copy them, and only later by the people who prove them — and *made* first, before either, by the people history filed under decoration.
