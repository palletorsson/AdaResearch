Concept: Mosaic_Facade_Ornament reads Neapolitan baroque facades as recursive grammars executed in carved stone and stucco. The scrollwork, volutes, and acanthus leaves that cover these buildings repeat at multiple scales — the same curling form appears in door surrounds, window pediments, cornice brackets, and balcony supports, scaled and varied but structurally identical. This map treats that ornamental logic as L-system production rules: an axiom (a single curl) expands through branching substitutions into the dense decorative programs that stonecutters carved by hand for centuries.

Sequence role: Map 4 of 7 in Mosaic Analysis: Grid Logic in Stone and Glass. The learner arrives from Mosaic_Majolica_Tiles carrying experience with 2D painted tile patterns on regular grids. This map moves from surface tiling to recursive structural ornament — from repeating the same tile across a plane to growing complexity through branching rules. The transition is significant: wallpaper groups tile by translation and reflection (rigid motions), while L-systems grow by substitution and branching (recursive expansion). Both produce patterns from rules, but the topology is different — flat repetition versus hierarchical growth. It feeds into Mosaic_Capri_Villa (mixed-period fragment analysis) and eventually into Mosaic_WFC_Reconstruction (constraint-based pattern generation). In QFEP terms, this map introduces recursive grammar as a form of cultural computation — the workshop tradition encoded L-system logic in apprenticeship and template sets centuries before Lindenmayer formalized it.

Technical angle: L-system generation models baroque scrollwork as a string-rewriting system: an axiom string undergoes iterative substitution according to production rules, and the resulting string is interpreted as drawing commands (forward, turn left, turn right, push state, pop state). Branching angle controls the tightness of curls — small angles produce tight spirals (volute cores), larger angles produce spreading acanthus leaves. Scale reduction at each branch level produces the self-similar scaling visible in actual facades. pattern_tile_puzzle isolates repeating ornamental modules from facade photographs, testing whether a single carved element and its production rule can regenerate the full ornamental program. tessellation_lattice_demo renders the lattice structure that organizes ornamental elements on the facade grid (column bays, story heights). The lsystem_editor (from the sequence artifact group) provides interactive production rule editing — the learner writes rules and watches the generated ornament grow, then compares it to photographs of actual baroque carvings.

Critical angle: Baroque ornament is a recursive grammar that craftsmen executed without formal notation. The volute — a spiral scroll that appears in Ionic capitals, console brackets, and staircase balusters — follows a production rule: start with an arc, split the endpoint into two smaller arcs at a branching angle, recurse. The acanthus leaf follows a similar rule at a different branching angle. Neapolitan stonecutters learned these rules through apprenticeship — copying templates, internalizing proportions, scaling by eye — and the L-system was implicit in their workshop tradition centuries before Lindenmayer published his 1968 paper on mathematical models of plant development. The QFEP reading of this is not that stonecutters were doing mathematics without knowing it (a reductive claim) but that the same structural logic emerges independently in biological growth, mathematical formalism, and craft tradition. The question the map poses is whether the algorithmic description captures the craft or just approximates its surface. An L-system can generate a convincing volute, but the carved stone version carries material resistance (chisel against marble), spatial judgment (how the curl reads at street level), and stylistic variation (each master's hand producing slightly different proportions). The lambda parameter here lives in the gap between the production rule and the carving — the same rule generates different ornament depending on who holds the chisel. The sequence truth that "the pattern is the algorithm made visible across centuries" takes on its most literal meaning: the algorithm is visible in the stone, but the stone also records everything the algorithm cannot express.

Key artifacts: pattern_tile_puzzle lets the learner isolate repeating ornamental modules from facade photographs — a single volute, a scroll bracket, an acanthus cluster — and test whether iterated application of the module's production rule regenerates the larger decorative program. tessellation_lattice_demo renders the architectural grid (column bays, story heights, cornice lines) that organizes ornamental placement on the facade, making the structural framework visible beneath the decorative surface. The lsystem_editor (from the sequence artifact group) provides interactive L-system rule editing where the learner writes production rules, adjusts branching angles and scale factors, and watches the generated ornament grow in real time for comparison against baroque originals.

Gap: The map needs a branching-angle parameter explorer that shows how the same production rule produces different ornamental families at different angles — tight spirals (volutes) at 15-30 degrees, spreading leaves (acanthus) at 40-60 degrees, open branching (candelabra motifs) at 70-90 degrees. A scale-across-facade tool that demonstrates how the same ornamental module scales from door surround (large) to window pediment (medium) to cornice bracket (small) while maintaining proportional relationships would connect the L-system's scale parameter to architectural practice. A comparison view placing the L-system output next to a photograph of the actual carved ornament would make the lambda gap between algorithmic generation and craft execution visually measurable.
