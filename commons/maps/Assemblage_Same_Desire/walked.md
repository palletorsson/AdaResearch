## The walk

Midway through the spine, the long thread ties. You enter past the loom — an **array with rules**, the first computer, still weaving — and beside it a 10 PRINT bolt running one coin-flip inside its symmetry machine, order and chance already collaborating. Then the world's makers: thirteen traditions as living shaders, Kente strips and ikat's dyed-in blur, zellige stars and the Pompeii meander, each plate with its makers named beneath. At the room's heart stands the origin — Kristina Torsson's patterns, *varma backen* winding, the check oscillating, Mothers Dream breathing between weave and machine — the landscape this whole work began in, now standing inside the work. And at the far end, facing the cloth across the whole room, two hard objects: Gödel's sentence, and the balance that cannot weigh itself. Walk the axis slowly. The room is arranged so the weave and the proof have to look at each other.

## The turn (critical)

The map exists to say one sentence, and to make the architecture say it before the words do: **the desire in Gödel is the same desire as in the Kente patterns.** Not similar — the same. The Ewe weaver composing a named proverb in strips is building, inside a strict rule system (the loom's bindings, the strip's width, the block grammar), a statement that means *more than the system can say* — a proverb, a lineage, a politics, carried by cloth that is only ever warp and weft. Gödel, inside the strictest rule system humans ever built, composed a sentence that means more than the system can prove. Both take a finite grammar and make it point past itself. Both are acts of reaching, by rule, beyond what the rule can hold — and both were done under constraint, the weaver's material and social, the logician's formal, and the reaching is *how the constraint is answered*. This dissolves the hierarchy the curriculum has been quietly dismantling all along: mathematics is not the refined form of what craft does crudely. They are one practice at different desks — the essay's own thread made walkable, loom to Jacquard to Ada Lovelace to this room, Sadie Plant's thread, material not metaphor. And the placement carries the argument's timing: midway — after you have learned the rules (the grid, the transforms, the groups, the grammars) and before the crisis teaches their limits — because this is the moment to learn that the limit was never a wall. It is the place where every maker, at every desk, has always leaned out. The room ends with the balance that cannot weigh itself standing across from a bolt of cloth that says more than thread can. Neither is finished. That is the desire.
