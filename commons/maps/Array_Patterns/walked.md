# Array_Patterns — walked

> R-021/R-028: considered critical tutorial, ghost-drafted from the working map;
> Palle rules the voice. The walk (tutorial) woven with the turn (critical).

## The cast

pattern tiles · wallpaper-group analyzer · loom / punch-card reference

## The walk

You paint one small tile and the room does the rest: the tile **repeats**, reflected and rotated across a floor that builds itself from that single cell. This is the sequence's first claim, made before a single word about "data structures" — that an array is not a box you put things in, it is a machine for turning *one* into *many by rule*. Change the tile and the whole carpet re-derives; the wallpaper-group analyzer names what your hand discovered — p1, pm, p4m — and tells you there are exactly seventeen ways to tile a plane, no more, a completeness you can *feel* by trying to invent an eighteenth and failing.

## The turn (critical)

The chapter opens here, not with a spreadsheet, because arrays need rescuing from the idea that they are neutral storage — and the fastest way to do it is to point at a carpet and say: **that is an algorithm, it is a thousand years older than the computer, and it was run by hand.** Whose hands is the part the word "array" was built to forget, so name them: the weavers — overwhelmingly women, across every continent and every millennium — who found the seventeen symmetry groups at the loom centuries before mathematicians proved those were all of them, and who executed pattern through threading, tie-up, and treadling in a discipline that is, exactly, programming a two-dimensional grid. Jacquard's loom carries his name, but the punch-cards only *read* the pattern; the pattern was drafted and thrown by workers history declined to write down, and the same abstraction that lets a computer call a carpet "just data" is the one that let their computational labor go uncounted. The array's real politics surface in that erasure: it does not store *anything* — it imposes regular spacing, integer indexing, discrete sampling, and calls that "just addressing," the way the loom's makers were called "just labor." The handmade-to-computational bridge is the chapter's whole thesis, and it starts by refusing to let the grid pretend it is innocent, or that it invented what the weavers already knew.
