# One wave inside another

The floor's wave has passed under your feet. In the last hall, a disturbance travelled between points that kept their places. Here a wave enters another rule. Two balls sit in separate cages above desks. Begin with one note.

The blue ball belongs to the carrier, the orange to the modulator. Press BASELINE, let the note end, then HOLD. The instrument keeps this setting and its sound as A.

Leave the blue ball where it is. Lift the orange one a little. A sound may become brassy, brittle, bell-like, or acquire a quality for which you have no ready name. Keep listening before choosing a familiar object to stand in for it.

While you drag, the instrument can start a fresh note every three tenths of a second. A movement becomes a run of beginnings. Let the ball rest and press PLAY. Give the note time to reach its ending. Was the difference at the beginning, through the middle, or in the tail?

COMPARE returns A, leaves a quarter-second gap, then plays the current B. You do not have to preserve the first sound intact in memory. The `differs:` line lists the parameters your hand changed. If the lift changed only I, the modulation index, we have one change to listen through. A hand can alter several coordinates while intending only one.

At index zero, the orange oscillation contributes nothing. The modulator is inside the sine's argument. Raising the index increases how far it advances and delays the carrier's phase. The second wave changes how the first proceeds. The sound's rise and fading are controlled elsewhere. What reaches the ear as one event has several rules that can be separated.

Keep the orange ball's height steady and change its depth along the desk. That direction changes how long the modulation persists. Listen through the tail. The altered character can remain while the note grows quiet. Something we first heard as one fading has separated into two processes.

Try the ball's left-to-right direction last. It changes the ratio between the oscillators' frequencies. Keep a reference and compare. Listen for a relation you want to return to, even if it refuses the name of an instrument you know. A setting that would spoil one imitation may be the beginning of another sound.

BASELINE is a chosen reference too: a 440-hertz carrier, an attack and decay, index zero. Four hundred and forty returns a second. The slow motion we could follow with our eyes has become a pitch. The generator still shapes and scales its output. Calling this the beginning makes comparison possible; it does not uncover a sound before all decisions.

HOLD can keep a note without knowing what you liked about it. COMPARE gives that decision back to your ear. The rig reports changed parameters, but there is no field in its list for the difference that made you want to listen again. What the desks leave in the hand is a habit: fix one thing, change one thing, listen through the tail before deciding what you heard.
