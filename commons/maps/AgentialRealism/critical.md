# AgentialRealism — Critical Reflection

Karen Barad's proposition: things do not exist before they interact. There are no pre-given objects waiting to be measured. The apparatus and the phenomenon co-constitute each other. Cut here, not there — and different worlds materialize. The "agent" is not the human holding the instrument. The agent is the cut itself. The agential cut does not discover boundaries — it enacts them.

Barad's agential realism dismantles a foundational assumption of computational ontology: that objects exist first and interactions happen second. In every game engine, in every physics simulation, in every data model, entities are instantiated and then allowed to interact. The object has properties before it meets another object. Collision is something that happens to pre-existing bodies. Barad says this is exactly backward. There are no bodies until there is an interaction. The boundary between "this object" and "that object" is produced by the measurement — by the apparatus, by the agential cut that resolves an entangled system into distinguishable components. Before the cut, there are not two things waiting to be separated. There is one phenomenon, and the cut produces the two.

Judith Butler's theory of performativity reinforces this. For Butler, identity is not a property but a performance — an ongoing, citational practice that produces the appearance of a stable self through repetition. Gender is not something a body has; it is something a body does, repeatedly, in contexts that constrain what doing is possible. The artifacts in this map shift depending on where the cut falls. What counts as data depends on what counts as apparatus. Identity is not a property. It is a performance — ongoing, material, never settled.

What does this map normalize? The entanglement of observer and observed. The idea that measurement is not passive recording but active constitution. Every act of observation changes what is observed — not as a minor perturbation, but as a constitutive condition. The map normalizes the radical view that the world does not come pre-parsed into objects. Objects are cut from the world by our apparatuses, our instruments, our categories, our code. `typeof(x)` does not discover x's type — it produces x-as-typed by the agential cut of the type system.

What does it exclude? The stubborn recalcitrance of matter. Barad insists that agential realism is not idealism — that matter kicks back, that the world constrains what cuts are possible. But the map's presentation risks sliding toward constructivism: the idea that reality is infinitely malleable, that any cut is as good as any other, that the apparatus has unlimited freedom to produce phenomena. This is not what Barad argues. The agential cut is constrained by the material-discursive practices within which it occurs. You cannot cut an electron into a wave and a particle simultaneously. The world's entanglements have structure. Not any cut will do.

Butler's concept of the material-discursive operates at this junction. Bodies are not blank surfaces upon which culture writes. They are material agencies that participate in their own constitution. The artifacts in this room are not infinitely interpretable — they push back, resist certain readings, afford certain interactions and not others. The cut produces the object, but the object was never nothing. It was entangled potential — a field of possibilities constrained by material conditions that no discourse alone can override.

What does this map open? A computation that does not begin with objects. A game engine where entities are not instantiated and then allowed to collide, but where collision itself produces the entities. Where the grid cell does not contain a floor tile until the player's foot enacts the floor. Where the artifact does not have properties until the apparatus of interaction queries them. This would be a Baradian game engine — one where the world is always entangled, always unresolved, and every interaction is an agential cut that produces both the subject and the object of the interaction.

The room invites you to walk through it. The artifacts shift depending on where the cut falls. But the cut that matters most is the one the map cannot show: the cut between you and the map, the apparatus (VR headset, game engine, rendering pipeline) that produces "you" and "the room" as separable phenomena. Before you entered, there was no room and no visitor. There was an entanglement of code, hardware, light, and flesh. The agential cut that separates viewer from viewed is not given — it is performed, every frame, sixty times a second.

Observation is not passive. Every measurement is an incision that produces what it claims to discover. The question agential realism leaves open is not whether cuts are real, but who is accountable for the worlds that particular cuts produce.
