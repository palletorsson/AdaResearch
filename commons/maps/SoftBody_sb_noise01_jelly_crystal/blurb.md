A jelly grid is built, settled, and then frozen. After the springs have done their work and the lattice has reached equilibrium under gravity, a second rule arrives. A cellular noise function — Worley, Voronoi, whatever you want to call it — is sampled at every mass point's final position. The output is a displacement. Where the noise is high, vertices push outward; where it is low, they pull inward. What was a smooth settled jelly becomes a fractured surface, faceted into cells whose boundaries are the noise function's own Voronoi diagram.

The body is the same body. The springs have not changed. The pin pattern has not changed. Only the post-process changed. And yet what reads at the end is not a jelly — it is a gemstone, cracked along planes that the simulation never computed and never knew about. The crystalline geometry comes entirely from where in the noise field each vertex happened to land.

This is the rule-as-loom set's noise variant. Conway and Life-without-Death wrote the topology of springs; L-systems wrote the branching skeleton; reaction-diffusion wrote the stiffness field; cellular noise writes the surface displacement. Four different rule families, four different layers of the same body. The soft-body gallery is not really about soft bodies — it is about which layer of the body a given rule writes onto.
