# BG_Tree_Forms — Critical Reflection

A grammar writes a tree. One axiom — F — becomes F[+F]F[-F]F, becomes branching, becomes canopy. L-systems encode growth as string rewriting: each generation applies production rules to every symbol simultaneously. The trunk does not know about the leaves. The leaves do not know about the wind. The tree_gen artifact produces infinite variety from simple rules, with parametric branching adding angle, length, and thickness as variables the environment can modulate.

Simondon's theory of individuation has a specific term for what L-systems do: they are an example of crystalline individuation applied to biological form. The production rules are the lattice — the fixed structure that determines how growth propagates. Each generation of rewriting adds a layer to the crystal, extending the structure along prescribed axes. The tree is not growing in the biological sense; it is being computed. The grammar specifies the possible forms; randomness selects among them. The L-system tree is a crystal that happens to look like a plant.

Hayles's work on inscription technologies becomes relevant here. For Hayles, every medium of inscription shapes what can be inscribed. The L-system encodes growth as string — a linear sequence of characters that is parsed into branching structure by convention (brackets for push/pop, +/- for angle). The tree's three-dimensional form is an interpretation of a one-dimensional text. This is not innocent. To encode branching as string rewriting is to commit to a specific ontology of growth: that it is sequential, rule-governed, and expressible in discrete symbols. The tree that results looks organic but is, at its deepest level, typographic. Every branch is a sentence parsed from a formal language.

The open grove — the tree growing in a sunken center, the player circling at ground level, looking up into the canopy — performs the relationship between grammar and form. You cannot see the grammar. You see only the tree. The production rules are hidden in the code; the canopy is visible in the air. The gap between text and tree is the gap between notation and phenomenon, between score and music, between genotype and phenotype. The grove makes this gap spatial: the grammar is below (in the code, in the void), the tree is above (in the canopy, in the light).

What does this map normalize? That growth can be fully specified by grammar. That the immense variety of tree forms — oak, birch, pine, baobab — reduces to parameter differences in a shared production system. The blurb makes this explicit: "The same grammar produces oak and birch — only the parameters differ." This is a universalist claim: one formalism, all trees. The grammar is sufficient. Morphological diversity is variation within a theme, not genuine difference.

What does it exclude? Everything that makes a real tree a real tree. Phototropism — the trunk bending toward light. Thigmomorphogenesis — the wood thickening in response to wind. Hydraulic architecture — the tapering of vessels from root to leaf. Wound response — the callus growing over a broken branch. The L-system tree responds to nothing. Its parameters are set at birth and never change. It grows in a void where light comes from everywhere and wind comes from nowhere. The "seasonal modulation" mentioned in the blurb — spring extends, autumn drops, winter holds — is a parameter sweep, not a response. The tree does not sense the seasons. It is told about them.

Simondon's distinction between technical and biological individuation cuts to the heart of this. Technical objects individuate through their relationship with an associated milieu — the engine's cooling system, the vacuum tube's thermal environment. Biological organisms individuate through their relationship with a milieu they also transform — the tree that shades the soil that retains the moisture that feeds the tree. The L-system tree has no milieu. It individuates in isolation, its form determined entirely by its grammar and its random seed. It is a technical object performing biological individuation, and the performance exposes what it cannot capture.

Stand in the grove. Look up. Every branch is a derivation in a formal language, yet no two trees match. The grammar does not describe a tree — it describes a becoming. But this becoming has already been fully determined by its initial conditions. The blueprint is a verb, the blurb says. But a verb conjugated entirely in the past tense.

What would it mean for the grammar to listen — to rewrite its own rules in response to what it has already produced?
