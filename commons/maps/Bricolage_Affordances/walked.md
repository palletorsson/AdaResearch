## The walk

The same primitives return, but each one twice, wearing a job instead of a name. The cylinder lies down carrying two wheels — an axle — and stands up bearing a lintel — a column: the identical shape, its meaning decided by which way you set it. The plane held flat on legs is a seat; stood on edge it is a wall. The sphere with four spokes is a hub; the sphere where two members meet at an angle is a joint. The clipboard names the move: parts defined by action, not category.

## What it teaches

This is the chapter's primitive, in the strict sense — the smallest operation from which everything later is built. An affordance is what a thing *lets you do*, and the room's claim is that a part has no fixed function, only a set of latent ones that orientation and context select between. The cube on the shelf next door was a candidate; here you watch candidacy resolve into use, reversibly, the cylinder switching from axle to column with a quarter turn. Bricolage lives in exactly this gap — the engineer's part means one thing, the bricoleur's part means whatever the current arrangement asks of it. To build without a blueprint is to keep every part's meaning open until the structure needs it closed.
