# Bricolage_Affordances - Critical

## Philosophical Framing

Gibson's affordance theory emerged from ecological psychology—the study of perception as action-oriented rather than representational. We don't perceive "a chair" and then compute "I could sit on it." We perceive "sit-on-able." The world is experienced as a field of possibilities for action.

This is radical: it dissolves the subject/object boundary. An affordance isn't in the object (the chair doesn't "contain" sittability) or in the subject (I don't "project" sittability onto neutral matter). It's in the relation between them.

## What This Concept Cannot Hold

The map presents affordances as discoverable and enumerable. But:

- **Affordances are infinite**: Any part has unlimited potential uses we haven't imagined
- **Affordances are cultural**: A cylinder affords "rolling" universally but "axle" only within wheeled technology
- **Affordances conflict**: A seat affords sitting but also affords standing-on—which is primary?
- **Affordances can be hidden**: Design can suppress affordances (smooth cylinder = no grip = no "holdable")
- **Affordances can be false**: A glass floor affords walking but triggers the affordance "fall-through"

The catalog approach makes affordances seem finite and known. Real making involves discovering affordances that weren't in any catalog.

## Politics of Affordance

Norman (1988) brought affordances into design, but transformed them:

- **Gibson's affordances**: What IS possible (ecological fact)
- **Norman's affordances**: What SEEMS possible (design intention)

This gap is political:
- **Hostile architecture**: Benches designed to suppress the "sleep-on-able" affordance
- **Accessibility**: Stairs suppress the "traverse-able" affordance for wheelchair users
- **Surveillance**: Cameras suppress the "hidden" affordance of public space

The bricoleur might resist intended affordances—using a bench arm as a skateboard obstacle, a fence as a trellis, a wall as a canvas.

## Queer Reading: Affordance as Refusal

Queer theory reads affordance through the lens of "designed for" vs. "used by":

- **Heteronormative affordances**: Park benches afford sitting for couples (armrests separate individual seats)
- **Queer refusal**: Using the bench "wrong"—lying across, sitting on the back
- **Repurposing**: Finding affordances the designer didn't intend or actively suppressed

The bricoleur's relationship to affordance is always potentially queer: asking "what can this become?" rather than "what was this designed for?" is a refusal of intended use.

## The Demonstration Problem

The paired demonstrations (column/axle, seat/wall, joint/hub) suggest:
- Two uses per primitive
- Clear distinction between uses
- Choice as binary

But real affordances are:
- Continuous (a cylinder at 45° is partially-column, partially-beam)
- Overlapping (a seat is also a shelf is also a step)
- Emergent (new contexts create new affordances)

The pedagogical clarity comes at the cost of affordance's fundamental ambiguity.

## Questions That Remain Open

1. Who decides which affordances are "legitimate"? The designer? The user? The materials themselves?

2. Can affordances be wrong? If I perceive "sit-on-able" and the chair breaks, was the affordance false or did I misperceive?

3. The map shows affordances for single parts. But most affordances emerge from assemblies—a single cylinder affords less than four cylinders arranged as legs. Where do assembly-level affordances come from?

4. Gibson studied animal perception (flies perceiving "land-on-able," fish perceiving "swim-through-able"). Are human affordances continuous with animal affordances, or does language fundamentally change them?

5. If a primitive has "infinite" affordances, what makes some affordances more salient than others? History? Biology? Culture? Physics?

## QFEP Connection

In the Queer Free Energy Principle, affordances represent the E(S) potential—the unrealized configurations that a part could enter. Each part is a field of latent entropy waiting to be reduced (by assembly) or expanded (by creative misuse).

The λ parameter (edge-of-chaos modulator) appears in affordance perception: too few perceived affordances = rigid, uncreative assembly; too many perceived affordances = paralysis, inability to commit. The skilled bricoleur operates at the λ edge—seeing enough affordances to have options, not so many as to be overwhelmed.
