Concept: Mosaic_Pompeii_Floors treats Roman floor mosaics from Pompeii as computational artifacts preserved under volcanic ash. The learner identifies which of the 17 wallpaper groups each floor pattern belongs to, then decomposes it into its minimal repeating tile — the fundamental domain from which the entire floor can be regenerated by symmetry operations alone. Ancient craftspeople at the Casa del Fauno and Casa dei Vettii worked without group theory, yet their patterns satisfy the same mathematical constraints that crystallographers formalized two millennia later.

Sequence role: Opens the Mosaic Analysis: Grid Logic in Stone and Glass sequence (map 1 of 7). The learner arrives from the patterngeneration prerequisite carrying wallpaper group vocabulary, WFC mechanics, and tessellation tools. This map grounds those abstractions in physical material — real stone floors from documented archaeological sites. It establishes the method (identify group, extract motif, map color palette) that every subsequent map will apply to different materials and traditions. It feeds into Mosaic_Byzantine_Gold, where the same grid logic meets reflective surfaces and viewing-angle dependence. In QFEP terms, this map introduces cultural_computation — the recognition that algorithms were executed in craft traditions long before they were formalized in mathematics.

Technical angle: The core task is wallpaper group classification of Roman opus tessellatum patterns. The learner uses pattern_tile_puzzle to isolate the fundamental domain — the smallest region that, when tiled by the group's symmetry operations (rotations, reflections, glide reflections, translations), reproduces the full floor. tessellation_lattice_demo renders the underlying lattice (square, hexagonal, or oblique) that organizes the tesserae into repeating units. Roman floors predominantly use p4m (square with full diagonal symmetry) and p6m (hexagonal with full symmetry), but irregular borders and figural panels break the symmetry locally, requiring the learner to distinguish the global group from local deviations. Color palette extraction maps the limited Roman material palette (white marble, black basalt, red porphyry, yellow limestone) to algorithmic color spaces for procedural regeneration.

Critical angle: The sequence truth declares: "Every mosaic encodes a symmetry group. The tesserae are data. The grout is the grid. The pattern is the algorithm made visible across centuries." Pompeii's floors are the purest test case because the volcanic preservation froze them mid-use — no restoration, no reinterpretation, just the original craftsmen's placement decisions readable two thousand years later. The grout lines form a discrete grid indistinguishable from the adjacency structure of a WFC solver: each tessera sits in a cell, and the adjacency rules (which colors can neighbor which) determine the pattern. The QFEP connection runs through the lambda parameter — the gap between the intended pattern (F, the formal symmetry group) and the executed pattern (E, the actual placement with its hand-cut irregularity). Every tessera is slightly off-square, slightly misaligned, slightly variable in hue. This noise is not error; it is the material signature of human craft, and no procedural shader replicates it. The lambda gap between algorithmic tessellation and physical mosaic is visible in every grout line. This is the productive tension QFEP reads as the difference between formalism and phenomena: the symmetry group is exact, the execution is not, and the beauty lives in both.

Key artifacts: pattern_tile_puzzle lets the learner isolate the fundamental domain from a mosaic photograph, testing whether the extracted motif tile reproduces the original pattern under the selected wallpaper group's symmetry operations. tessellation_lattice_demo renders the underlying lattice type (square, hexagonal, oblique) that organizes the tesserae into repeating units, making the invisible grid structure visible as an overlay. Together they implement the core analytical method: decompose a physical pattern into its mathematical skeleton (lattice + group + motif), then verify by reconstruction.

Gap: The map currently lacks a direct comparison tool that places the algorithmically reconstructed tiling side-by-side with the original mosaic photograph, highlighting where the symmetry group matches and where hand-cut irregularity breaks the perfect pattern. A color palette extraction artifact that maps physical tessera materials (marble, basalt, porphyry, limestone) to algorithmic color indices would formalize the material-to-data translation. A wallpaper group classifier that auto-detects the symmetry group from a user-selected region would provide feedback on the learner's manual classification, connecting the analytical skill to automated pattern recognition.
