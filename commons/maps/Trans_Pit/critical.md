# The operation has a victim — translation, rotation, scaling, and the politics of acting on inhabited space

Geometric transformations are usually taught as neutral operations. A translation shifts; a rotation turns; a scaling grows. The operations act on objects, and the objects can be recovered by applying the inverse. The neutrality of the operation is often treated as a feature, something like the neutrality of arithmetic. Trans_Pit refuses the neutrality. The three rooms of the map turn each transformation into a lethal hazard and insist that the operation's effect on inhabited space is not neutral when the space is inhabited.

Judith Butler's work on bodily precarity argues that the conditions under which a body can survive are unevenly distributed. Some bodies live in infrastructures that protect them; others live in infrastructures that threaten them; the protection and the threat are not random but follow political logic. Trans_Pit stages this unevenness mechanically. The fire hazard is the constant across all three rooms; the transformations are the variables. The pit does not care which operation sent the learner into it, and the operations do not care whether the space they act on was safe before they fired.

The first room is a corridor lined with pusher_block artifacts. Each block translates along a fixed axis at a set speed, with configurable distance and pause interval. The corridor runs past a row of fire pits. Translation becomes displacement. Stepping in front of a pusher at the wrong time sends the learner over the edge. The operation the first room enacts is T(p) = p + v, and the v is a vector that does not consult the body it displaces.

The second room is a circular arena with a revolving_wall rotating at constant angular velocity around a central axis. Fire pits line the arena's outer edge. Rotation becomes sweep. The wall clears the floor in an even arc, and the learner has to move with it rather than against it. The operation is R(p) = M·p, where M is the rotation matrix and p is the position. The matrix does not ask whether the position is currently occupied.

The third room is a chamber whose centre is occupied by a grower_block expanding at a steady scale rate. Fire pits surround the chamber's perimeter. Scaling becomes pressure. As the block grows, the safe footprint shrinks toward the walls, and the learner is eventually displaced out of the chamber or into the pit. The operation is S(p) = s·(p − c) + c, where c is the growth centre and s is the scale factor. The factor does not consult the bodies being compressed.

Butler's politics of precarity apply to each room in turn. The pusher corridor makes the learner's body a contested position in a lane that pushers claim by schedule. The revolving wall makes the body's survival depend on reading a rotation's timing from an unfavourable standpoint. The grower room makes the body's continued presence a function of how fast the centre claims space. In every case, the transformation operates on the learner without asking, and the asking is the part that political ethics would insert.

The politics of neutral operations are in the recovery argument. Transformations are often defended as invertible: you can reverse a translation, rotation, or scaling by applying the inverse. Trans_Pit demolishes this defence by making the consequence irreversible. Stepping into the fire pit is not undone by a translation back; the body does not recover by being translated. The invertibility of the geometric operation is decoupled from the survivability of the body the operation acts on, and the decoupling is the map's closing political observation.

Within the sequence, Trans_Pit converts transformation from observation into stake. Previous maps in the Transformation sequence introduced translation, rotation, and scaling as operations to study. This map insists that the operations have stakes when they act on inhabited space, and the stakes are not symmetric: the operation survives its own application, but the body the operation acts on may not. The map hands the learner forward with the algebra felt rather than only named, and the feeling is the part that later maps can build on.
